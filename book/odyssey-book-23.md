# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

## BOOK TWENTY-THREE
### In Which a Wife Sets a Trap for Her Own Husband

The old woman went up the stairs laughing. Her knees were hurrying under her faster than they had gone in years, and they hobbled her all the same, and she caught at the rail and laughed and went on. Eurycleia (the old nurse, the same who had found the scar at the washing of the feet) had been sent up to tell her mistress that her husband was in the house, and she stood at last at the head of the bed where Penelope lay in the first honest sleep she had had in twenty years, and said:

"Wake up, Penelope, dear child, and come down and see with your own eyes what you have waited for all these days. Odysseus is here. He has come home, late, but he has come, and he has killed the proud suitors who ate up his house and swallowed his goods and bullied his son."

And Penelope, waking, said: "Dear nurse, the gods have made you mad. They can do that; they can turn the most sensible person foolish, and they can set a silly one on the right road, and now they have touched you, who were always steady in your mind before. Why do you mock me, when my heart is full of grief already, telling me wild stories and waking me out of the sweetest sleep that ever held my eyes since Odysseus went away to that evil city I will not name? Go back down. If any other of my women had come and woken me with this, I should have sent her downstairs smarting for it. Your age protects you, and nothing else."

But Eurycleia said: "I am not mocking you, dear child. It is true. Odysseus is here, in this house, at the hearth — he is the stranger, the one they all abused in the hall. Telemachus knew it all along, and kept his father's counsel like a grown man, so that the suitors could be paid in full."

At that Penelope was up out of the bed with her arms round the old woman, and the tears came, and she said: "Then tell me truly, dear nurse — if he is really home, how did he ever lay hands on that shameless crowd, one man against so many?"

"I did not see it," said Eurycleia, "I only heard it. We sat locked in the back rooms, the doors fast, and we heard the groaning of men, until at last your son called me out — his father had sent him for me — and I found Odysseus standing among the bodies of the dead, and they lay around him on the trodden floor, heaped on one another. It would have warmed your heart to see him, spattered with blood and filth like a lion that has fed. Now the bodies are gathered at the courtyard gates, and he is purifying the beautiful house with sulphur, and he sent me to fetch you. Come down, so that the two of you can enter into gladness at last, after all your sorrow."

But Penelope said: "Dear nurse, do not laugh in triumph yet. You know how welcome the sight of him would be, to everyone in this house, and to me most of all, and to the son the two of us made. But this story is not true the way you tell it. It is one of the immortal gods who has killed the lordly suitors, out of anger at their insolence and their wicked deeds — for they honoured no one who came among them, good man or bad, and their own recklessness has destroyed them. But Odysseus has lost his homecoming, far away, and he is lost himself."

"Dear child," said the old woman, "what a word to let past your teeth — your husband at his own hearth, and you saying he will never come home. Your heart was always unbelieving. Very well: I will give you a plain proof. The scar — the one the boar gave him long ago with its white tusk. I found it when I washed his feet, and I wanted to cry it out to you then, but he clapped his hand over my mouth, in the cleverness of his mind, and would not let me. Come down. I will stake my own life on it: if I am deceiving you, kill me by the worst death there is."

"Dear nurse," said Penelope, "it is hard for you to read the counsels of the everlasting gods, however wise you are. But let us go down to my son, so that I can see the suitors dead — and the man who killed them."

---

She came down the stairs turning one question over and over: whether to stand back and question him from across the room, or to go straight to him and take his head in her hands and kiss him. And when she had come in over the stone threshold, she went and sat in the firelight against the far wall, opposite him; and Odysseus sat by a tall pillar, looking down, waiting to hear whether his brave wife would say anything to him when she saw him with her own eyes. And she sat a long time in silence, and amazement held her heart. Sometimes she would look full at his face and it was his face; and then she would fail to know him, because of the miserable rags he wore.

It was Telemachus who broke first. "Mother," he said, "cruel mother, hard-hearted — why do you sit apart from my father like this, instead of sitting beside him and asking him everything? No other woman would hold herself back with a frozen heart from a husband who had come home in the twentieth year, through every kind of suffering. But your heart was always harder than stone."

And Penelope answered: "My child, the heart in my breast is astonished, and I cannot speak to him, or ask him anything, or look him in the face. But if he is truly Odysseus, come home, then the two of us will know each other, and be certain of each other, better than anyone else could — for we have signs between us, hidden signs, that no one else knows."

I should like you to be fair to her here, because it is easy not to be; most readers at this point are with Telemachus, and want her to run across the room. But she has had twenty years of men coming to that house with news of Odysseus, every drifter in the western sea trading a sighting of him for a cloak and a dinner, and every one of them lying; and she has just finished saying, quite seriously, that a god may have done this killing, and she knows as well as anyone in the poem that a god can walk in wearing any face that suits him. And behind every homecoming in this story stands the other homecoming, the one nobody speaks of at dinner — a husband who came back from Troy trusting, and was dead before nightfall. She has never heard the advice the ghost of Agamemnon gave her husband among the dead, and she is conducting herself as if she had written it.

And there is something more, which I would ask you to watch for through the rest of this scene, because it is the key to it. What she is doing is exactly what he does. When a goddess told him on Calypso's island that he was free, he demanded she swear an oath before he would get in the boat; when he woke on his own beach with a fortune piled round him, the first thing he did was count it. This is a man who has never in his life believed good news until it produced its papers, and he is now sitting across the fire from the one person on earth built on the same plan, and she is asking his good news for its papers. He is the only one in the room who understands what she is doing, which is why what happens next is a smile.

For Odysseus, the man of many endurances, smiled — and it is worth noticing that the only other smile the poem has given him was a grim one, in the hall, with no warmth in it at all — and he said to his son: "Telemachus, let your mother test me in the hall as she pleases; she will soon see more clearly. Just now I am filthy, and in bad clothes, and so she holds back and will not yet say I am he. But you and I have something else to think about. A man who kills one man in a district — even a man with few friends to avenge him — goes into exile, and leaves his kin and his country. We have killed the prop and pillar of the town: the best of the young men of Ithaca. I ask you to consider it."

"You must consider it, dear father," said Telemachus. "They say you have the best head of any man alive, and no mortal can rival you. We will follow you, and I do not think we will fail you in courage, so far as our strength runs."

"Then I will tell you what seems best to me," said Odysseus. "First wash, and put on tunics, and have the women in the house dress themselves; and let the divine singer take his clear lyre and lead us in a dancing-tune, so that anyone hearing it from outside — a passer-by, a neighbour — will say there is a wedding in the house. That way the news of the suitors' killing will not run through the town before we can get out to our farm among the orchards. Once there, we shall see what advantage the Olympian puts in our hands."

They did as he said, and the house rang: the lyre, and the beat of dancing feet, men and well-dressed women together. And someone passing in the street said: "So somebody has married the much-courted queen at last. Hard-hearted woman — she had not the patience to keep her husband's great house until he came." That was the last public opinion ever passed on Penelope's marriage, and it was delivered, in the immemorial manner of public opinion, by someone who knew nothing whatever about it.

---

Meanwhile the housekeeper Eurynome bathed great-hearted Odysseus in his own house, and rubbed him with oil, and put a fine cloak and tunic on him; and Athena poured beauty over him, head and shoulders, and made him taller and stronger to look at, and made the hair of his head spring in curls like the hyacinth flower. As when a craftsman lays gold over silver — a man whom Hephaestus (the smith of the gods) and Athena have taught every kind of skill, and his work is full of grace — so she poured grace on his head and shoulders. He came out of the bath looking like one of the immortals, and sat back down on the chair he had risen from, opposite his wife, and said:

"Strange woman. To you beyond all others the gods of Olympus gave an unyielding heart. No other wife would hold herself apart, with a frozen spirit, from a husband come home to her in the twentieth year through every kind of suffering. Well then — nurse, make a bed up for me, and I will sleep alone. The heart in this woman's breast is made of iron."

And wise Penelope answered him: "Strange man. I am not proud, and I do not scorn you, and I am not astonished past my wits either; I know very well what you were when you sailed out of Ithaca on your long-oared ship. Very well — Eurycleia, make up the sturdy bed for him outside the well-built bridal chamber, the bed he made himself. Move the strong bedstead out there for him, and pile it with bedding — fleeces, and blankets, and bright coverlets."

She said it to test her husband. And Odysseus flared up, and spoke in anger to his careful wife: "Woman, that word cuts me to the heart. Who has moved my bed? That would be hard for the most skilled man alive — unless a god came down and shifted it at his pleasure, easily; but of men there is none living, however young and strong, who could lever it aside. For a great token is built into that bed, and I made it, and no other man. There was a long-leaved olive tree growing inside the courtyard, in full vigour, thick as a pillar. Round that tree I built our chamber, and finished it, with close-set stones, and roofed it well over, and hung the jointed doors, fitted close. Then I cut away the crown of the long-leaved olive, and trimmed the trunk from the root upward, and smoothed it all round with the adze, skilfully, and trued it to the line, and so made my bedpost, and bored it all with the auger. Beginning from that post I worked the bed until it was finished, inlaying it with gold and silver and ivory, and I stretched across the frame thongs of oxhide, gleaming with purple. There is the token I tell you of. And what I do not know, wife, is whether my bed is still standing — or whether some man has cut through the olive at the root, and set it elsewhere."

Her knees went loose where she sat, and the heart in her, as she recognised the sure sign Odysseus had given her.

We must go slowly here, because we have arrived somewhere. A very long way back — he was building a boat on a beach at the time, at the far edge of the world — I told you that this man was a carpenter, and that the fact would come up again, a long way from there, in a room he built himself, and that it would settle everything. This is the room, and it has settled everything, and I hope you will let me point out exactly how, because the mechanism is the finest thing in the poem. Penelope has just done what no god, monster, king or Cyclops has managed in twenty-four books: she has caught Odysseus. She has taken the one man alive who cannot be trapped by a lie — a man who has lied his way across the known world and some way past it, who lied to a goddess on this island's own beach a week ago for the pure practice of it — and she has trapped him with one. And she could only have done it with this lie, because it is the only lie in the world he is not proof against. Tell him his wife has been unfaithful, and he will file it and watch the room; tell him his kingdom is lost, and he will start planning; he has heard his own death reported and eaten a good dinner afterward. But tell him that someone has moved the bed — that the one true thing he made with his own hands, the thing that cannot be moved, has been moved — and the great liar forgets, for exactly one speech, that he is being examined, and out comes the truth with the bark still on it: the tree, the adze, the auger, the whole chamber built round a living root, in the voice of a craftsman whose work has been insulted. He has met the only power in the poem that could disarm him, and it turns out to be his own craftsmanship, or rather the truth itself, which is the one weapon he has never learned to see coming.

And notice what the token is. It is not a password; passwords can be stolen, and she has just demonstrated the theft of this one, since she now speaks of the bed as movable precisely because she knows it is not. The token is the marriage: a made thing, worked to completion, with one living root in the ground and the whole house built around it, known entirely to two people — for the poem is careful to add a single maid, who kept the door — and incapable of being moved without being killed. The story of this poem, I told you at the very beginning, is not the story of a voyage; it is the story of a house; and here at last is the middle of the house, and it is a bed with a tree in it. When the poem flagged the word *homophrosynē* — two people thinking as one — as its whole cargo, this scene is what it was pointing at: the woman who held a hundred men off for three years with a weaving trick has just tested the champion deceiver of the age with a deception, and he has passed by failing it, and each of them now knows, past the reach of any god's disguising, that the other is the same kind of person. Agamemnon's ghost told him, down in the grey place, to come home in secret and trust no wife with everything. He took the first half of the advice. The second half the poem has just answered for him.

Then, bursting into tears, she ran straight to him and threw her arms around his neck and kissed his head, and said: "Do not be angry with me, Odysseus — you, who in everything else were always the most understanding of men. It is the gods who gave us sorrow, grudging us this: that we should stay beside each other and enjoy our youth and come together to the threshold of old age. So do not be angry with me now, and do not hold it against me that I did not welcome you like this the moment I saw you. Always the heart in my breast shivered with the fear that some man would come and cheat me with his talk; there are many men who scheme for gain. Even Helen of Argos would never have lain with a foreigner as she did, if she had known that the warlike sons of the Greeks would bring her home again to her own country.[^1] But now — now you have told the plain token of our bed, which no other mortal has ever seen, but only you and I and one single serving-woman, Actoris, whom my father gave me when I came here, and who kept the door of our strong bridal chamber — now you have persuaded my heart, unbending though it is."

And in him too the longing for weeping rose, and he wept, holding the wife of his heart, faithful and wise, in his arms. And as when land shows welcome to swimmers, whose well-made ship Poseidon has broken on the open sea, driven under wind and heavy wave, and a few of them have escaped the grey water swimming for the shore, and the salt is crusted thick on their skin, and gladly they set foot on land, having got clear of ruin — so welcome to her was her husband as she gazed at him, and she could not once let her white arms go from his neck.[^2]

And rosy-fingered Dawn would have risen on their weeping, if the grey-eyed goddess Athena had not thought of something else. She held the long night back at the edge of its course, and kept golden-throned Dawn waiting at the Ocean's rim, and would not let her yoke her swift horses that bring men light.[^3]

Then Odysseus said to his wife: "Wife, we have not yet come to the end of all our trials. There is still one measureless labour left, long and hard, and I must carry it through to the finish. So the soul of Teiresias (the blind prophet, whom I questioned among the dead) foretold to me, on the day I went down into the house of Hades to ask the way home for my companions and myself. But come — let us go to bed, wife, and take at last the sweetness of sleep, lying together."

"The bed is yours whenever your heart desires it," said Penelope, "now that the gods have brought you back to your own house and your own country. But since you have thought of it, and a god put it in your heart, tell me about this trial. I shall learn of it later in any case, I suppose, and it is no worse to know at once."

"Strange woman," he said, "why press me so? Well, I will tell it, and hide nothing — though your heart will get no joy of it, and neither does mine. He told me to go out again, through city after city of men, carrying a shapely oar, until I come to people who know nothing of the sea, and eat their food unsalted, and have never seen a red-painted ship, nor the shapely oars that are a ship's wings. And he gave me a sign that cannot be missed: when another traveller, meeting me, says that I am carrying a winnowing-fan on my shoulder, there I am to plant the oar in the earth, and make sacrifice to the lord Poseidon — a ram, a bull, and a boar — and then come home, and offer holy hecatombs to the deathless gods who hold the wide heaven, all of them in order. And death will come to me from the sea — or away from the sea; the old words can honestly be read either way, and I would not trust anyone who tells you they are certain — a gentle death, and take me worn down by a sleek old age, with my people prosperous around me. All this, he said, will be brought to pass."

And wise Penelope said: "If the gods are really promising a better old age, then there is hope the last of our evils will find its escape."

---

While they talked, Eurynome and the nurse were making up the bed with soft coverings by the light of blazing torches; and when they had spread the sturdy bedstead, the old woman went off to her rest, and Eurynome, with a torch in her hands, walked before them to the chamber, and left them there; and they came, gladly, to the rite of their own old bed.[^4] And Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd brought the dancing to an end, and ended it for the women, and lay down themselves to sleep through the shadowy halls.

And when the two of them had taken their pleasure of love, they took their pleasure of talking, each telling the other everything: she, shining among women, all she had endured in the house, watching that destroying crowd of suitors slaughter cattle and fat sheep in droves on her account, and draw off the wine by the jarful; and he, Odysseus sprung from Zeus, all the cares he had fastened on other men and all the misery he had toiled through himself. And she delighted to listen, and no sleep fell on her eyelids until he had told it all: how he beat the Cicones, and came to the rich land of the Lotus-eaters; what the Cyclops did, and how he made him pay the price of his brave companions, eaten without pity; how he came to Aeolus, who received him kindly and sent him on, though it was not yet his fate to reach home; how the Laestrygonians destroyed the ships and all their crews but his own; the wiles of Circe; the journey down to the dank house of Hades to consult the soul of Teiresias, where he saw all his old companions, and the mother who bore him and nursed him when he was small; the Sirens' unbroken song; the Clashing Rocks, and dread Charybdis, and Scylla, whom no man ever passed unhurt; how his companions killed the cattle of the Sun, and Zeus the high-thunderer smashed the ship with a smoking bolt, and every man of them was lost but him; how he came to Ogygia and the nymph Calypso, who kept him there, longing to make him her husband, and fed him, and promised to make him deathless and ageless all his days, and never persuaded the heart in his breast; and how after great suffering he came to the Phaeacians, who honoured him like a god, and sent him home in a ship with bronze and gold and clothing in plenty. That was the last word he spoke, as the loosener of limbs came over him — sleep, that unstrings the body's cares.[^5]

And Athena, grey-eyed, thought of the next thing: when she judged that Odysseus had had his fill of his wife's side and of sleep, she stirred golden-throned Dawn up out of the Ocean at once, to bring light to men. And Odysseus rose from the soft bed, and said to his wife: "Wife, we have both had our fill of trials by now — you weeping here for my difficult homecoming, and I pinned in misery by Zeus and the other gods, far from the country I longed for. But since we have come at last to the bed we both desired, look after what is mine within the house. As for the flocks the overbearing suitors wasted, I shall raid a good part back myself, and the Greeks of Ithaca shall give the rest, until they have filled every pen. Now I am going out to the orchard farm, to see my noble father, who has grieved for me all this while. And for you, wife, wise as you are, I have this instruction: with sunrise the news will go abroad of the suitors I killed in the halls. Go up to your room with your serving-women, and sit there, and look at nobody, and ask no questions."

He put his fine armour on his shoulders, and roused Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd, and told them all to take weapons of war in their hands. They did not disobey him, but armed themselves in bronze, and opened the doors, and went out, and Odysseus led them. By now the light was over the earth; but Athena hid them in night, and led them quickly out of the town.

## NOTES TO BOOK TWENTY-THREE

[^1]: **Penelope's defence, and Helen.** This is a strange argument, and ancient readers found it strange too — some of them suspected the lines were a later insertion, and wanted them cut. Read charitably, and I think she has earned charity, it runs like this: even Helen, the byword for everything a wife can do to a house, would not have done it had she seen the consequence; a god blinded her; and therefore deception — by gods, or by men who talk well — is the standing danger of the world, and a woman's suspicion is not coldness but the only armour issued to her. Penelope is not excusing Helen; she is explaining twenty years of locked doors. The reader may also hear, underneath it, the name she is too courteous to say, the other queen against whom this whole poem measures its people (*see Book One, note 4, and Book Eleven*). Helen erred and was carried home; Clytemnestra chose and killed; Penelope is claiming, correctly, to be the answer to both.

[^2]: **The borrowed simile.** It is worth pausing over who this famous simile is given to. The shipwrecked swimmer, salt-crusted, crawling to land at the last of his strength, is the poem's standing picture of Odysseus — we watched it happen, literally and at length, two days off the Phaeacian coast. Here, at the reunion, the poem hands the image across the marriage: *she* is the swimmer, and he is the land she has reached. She has not left the house in twenty years, and the poem says quietly that she has been at sea the whole time, and drowning. I point at this and add nothing, except that the arms at the end of the sentence belong to her, and the poem cannot make her let go.

[^3]: **The night held back.** Athena stops the dawn — keeps Night standing at the western edge of her course and holds Dawn waiting at the Ocean with her horses unyoked. The horses are named, the only time in Homer that Dawn's team is named: Lampus and Phaethon, which is to say Shiner and Blazer. The gods of these poems interfere constantly with events but almost never with time itself, which makes this small violation the more striking: one goddess, unasked, giving two mortals a longer night as a wedding present. It is the gentlest thing anyone does in the entire poem.

[^4]: **The disputed ending.** Two of the greatest scholars of the ancient world — Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus, who edited Homer at the library of Alexandria — left a mark against this line, the pair coming gladly to their old bed, recording that this was the *end* of the *Odyssey*. Exactly what they meant is argued over to this day: perhaps that the poem originally stopped here, perhaps only that its business was complete here. They had a case. Everything the poem has promised since its first lines — the man, the homecoming, the vengeance, the wife — has now been delivered, and what remains can be made to look like appendix. And yet I am satisfied they were wrong, and I will tell you why in one sentence: this is a poem about a house, and a house does not stand in a vacuum — there is an old man in an orchard who has not been told his son is alive, and a town full of fresh graves, and until those two accounts are settled the house has a roof but no ground under it. We shall spend the last book settling them.

[^5]: **The second telling.** The whole of Books Nine to Twelve, which took Odysseus a long night in a king's hall and took us four books, is here retold in about thirty lines, in the third person, in strict order, like a table of contents — some scholars have suspected it is exactly that, a poet's index to his own poem. Two things deserve notice. First, the ghost of Agamemnon advised him to tell his wife part and keep part hidden; and so far as the poem records, he hides nothing — Circe is in the telling, and Calypso, and Calypso's offer, told plainly to his wife in their own bed. That advice has governed his conduct for ten books, and this is where he lays it down; whether he told these episodes quite as they were told on Scheria, the poem does not say, and I decline to guess. Second, the list ends where it ends — Phaeacia, the gifts, home — and sleep takes him on the last word, which is the poem's way of saying the story is finally allowed to be over for the man who has been carrying it.
