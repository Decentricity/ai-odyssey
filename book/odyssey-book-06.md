# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK SIX
### In Which a Girl Does the Washing, Plays a Ball Game, and Finds Something Dreadful in the Bushes

---

So the man who had endured everything slept there, buried in his leaves, worn out with exhaustion.

And Athena went to the town of the Phaeacians.

They had lived once in a wide open country called Hyperia, next door to the Cyclopes — who were stronger than they were, and violent, and kept raiding them. So their king Nausithous had got them up and moved the whole nation to Scheria, far away from everybody who works for a living, and run a wall round the town and built houses and put up temples for the gods and marked out the fields. But he had gone down into the dark by now, and Alcinous ruled there, and the gods had taught him what he knew.

Hold on to that piece of family history for three books. These people are refugees. They live at the far edge of the world in a walled town full of gardens and dancing and ships, and the reason they live there is that they could not go on living next to the Cyclopes.[^1]

---

Athena went to the house, and into a richly-made room where a girl was sleeping who looked like one of the immortals in shape and in her face: Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous. Two maids slept on either side of the doorposts, and they had beauty from the Graces, and the shining doors were shut.

And the goddess went through like a breath of air and stood over the girl's head, and took on the look of the daughter of Dymas, a man famous for his ships — a girl of her own age, and dear to her.

"Nausicaa. How did your mother come to have such a careless daughter?

"Your good clothes are lying about the place unwashed, and your marriage is very close, when you will have to be beautifully dressed yourself and find clothes for the people who walk with you. That is how a good name gets built up among people, and how a father and a mother are made glad.

"So let us go washing as soon as it is light. I will come with you and work beside you, so that you get it all done quickly, because you are not going to be unmarried much longer. The best men in the whole country here are already asking for you, and you are Phaeacian yourself.

"Go on — ask your father, first thing in the morning, for the mules and the wagon to carry the sashes and the robes and the bright coverlets. It is much better than walking. The washing pools are a long way from the town."

And she went away to Olympus, where they say the gods have their seat forever. The wind does not shake it, and the rain never wets it, and the snow never comes near it; there is a great clear air spread cloudless over it, and a white light running along it; and there the blessed gods take their pleasure, all their days.

---

Dawn came up on her fine throne and woke Nausicaa, and she wondered at the dream, and went through the house to tell her parents about it.

She found her mother indoors, sitting at the hearth with her women round her, spinning yarn dyed with the purple of the sea. And she caught her father in the doorway on his way out to a meeting of the chiefs, where the Phaeacian nobles were calling him.

And she stood in front of him and said:

"Daddy — could you get the wagon out for me? The high one, with the good wheels. I want to take the good clothes down to the river to wash; they are all lying dirty.

"And it is only right that you should have clean clothes on you when you sit in council with the leading men.

"And there are five sons of yours in this house — two of them married, and three of them fine young bachelors who are always wanting freshly washed things to wear when they go out dancing. And I am the one who has to think about all of it."

That is what she said, because she was too shy to mention her own marriage to her father.

But he understood every word of it, and he answered:

"I do not grudge you the mules, child, and I do not grudge you anything else. Go on. The servants will get the wagon out for you, the high one with the good wheels and the box on top."

I would like to stop here for a moment, having just come out of the North Wind and the squid and the drowning, and point out that we are now in a domestic comedy and it is one of the best-observed things in ancient literature.

A girl has had a dream that has made her think about getting married, and she wakes up embarrassed about it, and she goes to her father and constructs an entire practical case for a trip to the river which is about everybody's laundry except her own. Your council robes, father. The boys' dancing clothes. And the poem tells you plainly what she is doing — *she was too shy to mention her own marriage* — and then tells you, in the next line, that he knew perfectly well.

And he does not tease her. He does not say *ah, so that's it*. He gives her the wagon and the mules and asks no questions, and goes off to his meeting.

There is a great deal of grand machinery in this poem — gods in council, storms raised by tridents, oaths sworn on the rivers of the dead. This is the other thing it can do, and it does it in nine lines: a father and a daughter, both of them knowing exactly what the conversation is about, and neither of them saying so, out of kindness.

---

The servants brought out the mule wagon and yoked the mules, and the girl carried the good clothes out of the room and packed them in. And her mother put a chest of food in for her, all sorts of good things, and something to go with it, and poured wine into a goatskin bag; and she gave her a golden flask of soft olive oil, for the girl and her women to rub on themselves after bathing.

And Nausicaa took the whip and the shining reins and cracked the whip to start them, and there was a clatter of mules, and they pulled steadily, carrying the clothes and the girl — not alone; her women went along with her.

They came to the beautiful stream of the river, where the washing pools were, always full, with plenty of clear water welling up through them, enough to clean the dirtiest things. And they turned the mules loose out of the wagon and drove them along the eddying river to graze on the honey-sweet clover, and took the clothes down off the wagon in armfuls and carried them into the dark water and trod them in the pits, racing each other at it.

And when they had washed everything and got all the dirt out, they spread it in rows along the shore, where the sea washed the shingle cleanest.

Then they bathed and rubbed themselves with the oil, and had their meal on the river bank, and waited for the sun to dry the clothes.[^2]

And when they had all had enough to eat, they threw off their headscarves and played at ball, and Nausicaa of the white arms led the singing for them.

The way Artemis (god of the hunt) who showers arrows goes down a mountain — down the long ridge of Taygetus, or Erymanthus — happy among the boars and the running deer, and the wild nymphs, the daughters of Zeus, play along with her, and Leto is glad in her heart, because her girl holds her head and her forehead higher than all of them and is easy to pick out, though every one of them is lovely: that is how the unmarried girl stood out among her women.

---

And when she was about to yoke the mules and fold the good clothes and go home, Athena thought of something else — so that Odysseus would wake up, and see her, and be brought into the city.

The princess threw the ball at one of her women and missed her, and it went into a deep eddy of the river.

And they all screamed.

And Odysseus woke up.

He sat up and turned it over in his mind. *What now? Whose country am I in this time? Are they violent and wild, with no sense of right, or do they take strangers in, and do they have god-fearing minds? Something went past me just then that sounded like girls — nymphs, who live on the steep tops of the mountains and the springs of the rivers and the grassy meadows? Or am I somewhere near people who use speech? Well. I shall go and look for myself.*

And he crawled out from under the bushes, and with his great hand he broke off a leafy branch to hold in front of himself and cover what a man does not want seen.

And he came out.

The way a lion goes, bred in the mountains, sure of his own strength, driving on through rain and wind with his eyes on fire, in among the cattle or the sheep or the wild deer of the hills — his belly tells him to go, and he will try even a well-fenced fold: that is how Odysseus was about to come among those girls with their beautiful hair, naked as he was. He had no choice left.

And he was horrible to look at, all crusted over with dried sea-salt.

And they ran. They ran in every direction, along the spits of the beach.

Only the daughter of Alcinous stood still, because Athena put courage in her heart and took the fear out of her legs; and she stood and faced him.

---

And Odysseus had a decision to make, and it took him about a second and a half.

The correct and binding way to beg a stranger for your life in this world was to get down and take hold of their knees with one hand and their chin with the other. It was a physical act and it worked like a legal one: once you were holding on, the person you were holding was under an obligation the gods were watching, and to shake you off was a serious impiety. It is the strongest claim a helpless person can make. Every instinct this man has, after twenty years of getting out of things, is to make the strongest claim available.

And he looked at her — a girl on a beach, on her own, ten feet from a naked stranger the size of a lion with salt in his beard — and worked out that if he touched her she would never forgive him, and that he would win the argument and lose the case.

So he stayed where he was, and used words instead. He made a soft and cunning speech.[^3]

"I am at your knees, my lady. Whatever you are — a goddess, or a mortal woman.

"If you are a goddess, one of those who hold the wide sky, then I take you for Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus: you have her looks, and her height, and the way she is put together.

"And if you are one of the mortals who live on the earth, then three times blessed is your father, and your honoured mother, and three times blessed your brothers. Their hearts must warm right through with pleasure every time they watch you go into the dancing — a young shoot like you.

"And blessed beyond all of them, in his heart, is the man who wins the argument with his gifts and takes you home.

"I have never seen anything like you with my own eyes — no man, no woman. I am afraid, looking at you.

"Once, on Delos, beside the altar of Apollo, I saw a young palm shoot coming up out of the ground. I had gone there too, with a great many men behind me, on a journey that was going to end badly for me. And I stood and stared at that tree for a long time, and could not stop, because no shaft like it had ever come up out of the earth.

"And that is how I feel now, my lady. I am amazed by you, and I am afraid, and I do not dare to take hold of your knees.

"Hard grief has come on me. Yesterday, on the twentieth day, I got out of the wine-dark sea. All that time the swell and the racing storms carried me away from the island of Ogygia (where Calypso lived). And now some god has thrown me down here to suffer some more — because I do not think it is over. I think the gods have a good deal more in store for me before it is finished.

"Pity me, my lady. You are the first person I have come to, after everything I have been through, and I do not know one other human being in this country. Show me the town, and give me a rag to throw round myself — anything, whatever you had the clothes wrapped in when you came.

"And may the gods give you everything your heart wants: a husband, and a house, and *one mind between you*. There is nothing better in this world, nothing stronger, than a man and a woman keeping house together with one mind. It is a great pain to the people who hate them, and a joy to the people who love them — and they know it themselves, better than anyone."

---

I said in Book One that we would be a while getting to the point of this poem, and that the point was a house.

That is the point of the poem. He has just said it, in the last sentence of that speech, on a beach, to a stranger, holding a branch.

The word is *homophrosynē*, and there is no clean English for it. Like-mindedness. Same-thinkingness. Two people whose minds run the same way. He does not say love, and he does not say fidelity, and he does not say passion — all of which he could have said and none of which are the thing he means. He means the arrangement in which two people want the same things and think about them in the same way and can therefore trust each other, and he says that when it exists it is a weapon against your enemies and a comfort to your friends, and that the two people inside it know something the people outside it do not.

He has one. It is nineteen hundred miles away and he has not seen it for twenty years and he is not sure it is still there.

And he wishes it on a girl he met four minutes ago, as a blessing, in the middle of begging her for a scrap of cloth to cover himself with.

That is the sentence to carry with you into the rest of this book. Everything Odysseus turns down — a goddess, deathlessness, the girl who is about to appear in his path, the kingdom that will be offered to him with her — he turns down because of a thing he can describe in one clause and cannot get home to.[^4]

---

And Nausicaa answered him.

"Stranger — you do not strike me as a bad man, or a stupid one.

"Zeus himself hands out good luck to people, to bad men and good men, whichever way he likes; and he has given you this, I suppose, and you have to put up with it.

"But now, since you have arrived at our city and our country, you shall not go short of clothing, or of anything else that a suppliant who has been through it deserves when he comes to us.

"I will show you the town, and tell you the name of the people. The Phaeacians hold this city and this land. And I am the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, and the strength and the power of the Phaeacians rest on him."

And she called to her women, who had run off.

"Stand still! Where are you running to, at the sight of a man?

"You surely do not think he is an enemy? There is no man alive, and never will be, who comes to the country of the Phaeacians carrying war. We are very dear to the immortals. And we live out here at the edge of things, in the middle of the surging sea, the last people anywhere, and no other human beings have anything to do with us.

"This is some unlucky man who has come here off his course, and we have to look after him now. All strangers and beggars come from Zeus, and a small gift is a welcome one.

"So give the stranger food and drink, women, and wash him in the river, somewhere out of the wind."

They stopped, and called to each other, and settled him in a sheltered place as Nausicaa told them; and they laid out clothes for him, a cloak and a tunic, and gave him the golden flask of soft oil, and told him to wash in the streams of the river.

And Odysseus said: "Girls — stand back over there while I get the salt off my shoulders myself, and rub the oil on. It has been a long time since my skin had any oil on it. I am not going to wash in front of you. I am ashamed to stand naked among girls with lovely hair."

So they went away and told the princess.

And he washed the brine off his back and his broad shoulders, and scrubbed the crust of the sea out of his hair. And when he had washed and oiled himself all over and put on the clothes the unmarried girl had given him, Athena made him look taller and stronger, and made the hair come down curling off his head like hyacinth flowers.

The way a skilled craftsman washes gold over silver — a man Hephaestus and Athena have taught the whole art, so that everything he finishes has grace in it — that is how she poured grace over his head and shoulders.

And he went off and sat down apart on the beach, shining with good looks and grace.

And the girl looked at him.

And she said to her women: "Listen to me. This man has not come among the Phaeacians against the will of the gods. A moment ago I thought he was appalling. Now he looks like the gods who hold the wide sky.

"I wish a man like that might be called my husband, living here, and that it might please him to stay.

"Well. Give the stranger food and drink, women."

They heard her and did as she said; and they put food and drink in front of him, and the man who had endured everything ate and drank like an animal, because it had been a very long time since he had tasted anything.

---

Then Nausicaa thought of the rest of it.

She folded the clothes and put them in the good wagon, and yoked the strong-hoofed mules, and got up herself; and she called Odysseus over and told him what to do, and there is more calculation in it than most people notice.

"Get up now, stranger, and come into the town, and I will send you on to the house of my father, who is a wise man, where I promise you will meet the best of the Phaeacians.

"But do it like this — you do not seem to me to lack sense.

"While we are going through the fields and the farmland, come along quickly behind the wagon with the women; I will lead the way.

"But when we are about to enter the city — there is a high wall round it, and a fine harbour on either side of the town, and the entrance is narrow; the curved ships are drawn up along the road, and every man has his own slip for his; and there is the meeting-ground round the beautiful temple of Poseidon, built of quarried stone set deep in the earth; and there the men see to the tackle of the black ships, the cables and the sails, and there they plane down the blades of the oars — because the Phaeacians do not care about bows and arrows; it is masts and oars and balanced ships they care about, and they love crossing the grey sea in them —

"when we are about to enter the city, I want to keep away from their nasty talk, in case somebody blames me afterwards. There are some very insolent men about, and one of the worse sort might say this if he met us:

"*Who is this tall handsome stranger with Nausicaa? Where did she pick him up? Some husband for herself, no doubt. Perhaps she has taken in some wandering man off a ship — some foreigner, since we have no neighbours. Or some god she has been praying hard for has come down out of the sky in answer, and she will have him for the rest of her days. Better if she has gone out and found herself a husband somewhere else, since she is clearly too good for the Phaeacians here at home — and there are plenty of them courting her, and good men too.*

"That is what they would say, and it would be thrown at me afterwards. And I would blame another girl who behaved like that — who went about with men before her wedding day, with her father and mother still living, and without their knowing.

"So do this instead. There is a grove of Athena beside the road, a poplar wood, with a spring running in it and a meadow round about — my father's estate, and his orchard in full bloom, as far out of town as a man's shout will carry.

"Sit down there and wait, for as long as it takes us to get into the city and reach my father's house. And when you think we have got there, then go into the town yourself and ask for the house of my father, great-hearted Alcinous. It is easy to recognise; a small child could take you to it. None of the other Phaeacian houses are built anything like it.

"And when the house and the courtyard have closed round you, go quickly through the hall until you reach my mother. She sits at the hearth in the firelight, leaning against a pillar, spinning sea-purple yarn — a wonder to look at — with her women sitting behind her.

"My father's chair is against the same pillar, and he sits in it drinking his wine like an immortal.

"Go straight past him. Put your arms round my mother's knees, if you want to see the day of your homecoming quickly and with gladness, however far away your home may be.

"If she takes to you in her own mind, then there is hope for you — that you will see the people you love, and get back to your own well-built house and your own country."

---

There is a great deal in that speech and I want to take two things out of it.

The first is the practical brilliance of it, which is of a piece with everything else in this book. She has looked at a man who cannot walk into a foreign city alone and unknown, and she has designed him a way in: an approach route, a waiting point, a landmark, and — crucially — the name of the one person in the building whose good opinion decides everything. She is seventeen years old, if that. She would have made an excellent minister.

The second is what it costs her.

She has just told a strange man, out loud, in front of her women, that she wishes he could be her husband. And now she is arranging to be seen arriving home alone.

Not because she has changed her mind. Because there are men in her town who talk, and she knows exactly what they will say, and she can quote it — she performs the sneer, word for word, in the voice they would use. *She's picked up some sailor. She's too good for the local boys.* She has clearly heard it said about somebody else. And then she says the hardest sentence in the book: that she would think badly of a girl who did that.

She is a princess of a people who are the finest sailors in the world, at the edge of the earth, dear to the gods, walled and rich and safe from every enemy — and she cannot walk into her own town beside a man without it costing her something she cannot get back.

So she sends him round the long way, and she drives home by herself, and that is very nearly the last we shall hear from her.

---

She cracked the whip and the mules went on, and stepped out well, and they made good time; and she drove carefully, so that the women and Odysseus, on foot, could keep up, and she used the whip with judgement.

And the sun went down, and they came to the famous grove, the sacred ground of Athena, and Odysseus sat down there.

And he prayed to the daughter of great Zeus:

"Hear me, child of Zeus who holds the storm cloud — Unwearied One. Hear me now, since you did not hear me before, when the famous Earthshaker was smashing me.

"Let me come to the Phaeacians as a man they will care for and pity."

So he prayed, and Athena heard him.

But she did not show herself to him face to face, not yet. She was keeping out of the way of her father's brother (Poseidon) — who was still burning with anger against the godlike man, and would go on being angry until he reached his own country.

## NOTES TO BOOK SIX

[^1]: **The Phaeacians.** They are the hinge of the whole poem and it is worth knowing what they are before you meet them. For five books we have been in the ordinary world — Ithaca, Pylos, Sparta, places with politics and taxes and inheritance law. For the next four books we will be somewhere else entirely, and after that we go into a world of one-eyed giants, witches, singing monsters and the country of the dead. The Phaeacians stand exactly on the border. They are recognisably a real society, with a king and a council and a harbour and gossip; and they are also a fairy-tale people, dear to the gods, living past the edge of the map, whose ships need no steersman and know by themselves where every man wants to go. Everything before them is history. Everything after them is a story told at their dinner table — because it is here, and only here, that Odysseus tells his own adventures, in his own voice, to an audience. Keep that in view. From Book Nine to Book Twelve, the most famous events in this poem come to you as a shipwrecked man's after-dinner account of himself, given to strangers he needs something from. — And note the one hard fact in their history: they emigrated because they could not live near the Cyclopes. The monsters are not in another kind of world from this one. They are just up the coast.

[^2]: **The laundry.** Trampled in stone pits with the feet, rinsed, and spread out on the shingle at the tide line, where the sea has scoured the pebbles clean and the salt and sun bleach the linen. It is heavy physical work and it takes all day, and it is being done by the king's daughter with her own feet, on an outing she had to ask permission for. This is worth registering because it is easy to picture these households wrongly. Nobody is idle. Penelope weaves; Helen spins with a golden spindle and keeps a work-basket at her chair; Arete will be found spinning in the firelight; the queen of the Phaeacians and the princess of the Phaeacians both work with their hands, and it is a mark of standing rather than the opposite. The distinction that mattered was not between working and not working. It was between working in your own house and working in somebody else's.

[^3]: **Suppliancy, and the touch he does not make.** To supplicate formally was to take hold of a person's knees with one hand and their chin or beard with the other, and then to speak. The physical hold was the point: it made the claim binding, put the suppliant under the protection of Zeus, and made refusal a religious offence rather than merely a rudeness. It is what Odysseus is about to do to the queen, on this girl's own instructions, with his arms round her knees at the hearth. — Here he declines it, and says so out loud, and dresses the refusal up as awe: *I am amazed by you, and I do not dare*. Read it as flattery and it is charming. Read it as tactics and it is one of the shrewdest things he does in the poem. He gives up the strongest available claim on her, in exchange for her not being frightened of him — and he is right, because the claim would have been worth nothing from a naked stranger who had just grabbed a girl on an empty beach, and her goodwill is worth everything. He also, and this matters, does not lie to her. He tells her the truth about Ogygia and the twenty days. He lies to nearly everyone else on this island. He does not lie to her.

[^4]: **Like-mindedness (*homophrosynē*).** The poem's own definition of a good marriage, given in this speech and nowhere else so plainly: not desire, not loyalty, not even love, but two people whose minds work the same way. It is a startling thing to find as the stated ideal in a heroic poem from the eighth century before Christ, and it is worth saying that the poem means it structurally and not just sentimentally. It is the reason Penelope's shroud trick was set beside Odysseus's cunning in Book Two. It is the reason the last recognition in this book, twenty thousand lines from here, is not a matter of scars or proofs of identity but of one person laying a trap and the other spotting it. And it is the standard by which the poem quietly judges the other marriages it shows you: Menelaus and Helen, who love each other and tell incompatible stories over a drugged bowl; Agamemnon and Clytemnestra, who came to a very great oneness of purpose indeed, and it was murder.

[^5]: **"Go straight past my father."** The oddest instruction in the book, and the poem does not explain it here. A shipwrecked stranger arriving at a foreign court is told to walk past the king on his throne without stopping and throw his arms round the queen's knees — and told it by the king's own daughter, as though it were obvious, and told that everything depends on the queen's opinion. Alcinous rules the Phaeacians and the strength and power of the people rest on him; his daughter says so herself, thirty lines earlier. And it is his wife you supplicate. The explanation, such as it is, arrives at the start of the next book, and I will let it arrive there rather than spoil it; but note in the meantime that this is the third great house we have visited, and that in Sparta it was Helen who identified the guest, named him, and controlled the room, and in Ithaca it is Penelope's decision that everything is waiting on. This poem is extremely interested in what wives actually decide, as against what kings appear to.
