# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK NINETEEN
### In Which a Husband and a Wife Talk All Night, and an Old Woman Washes His Feet

---

Odysseus was left behind in the hall, turning over the killing of the suitors with Athena's help. And he spoke quickly to Telemachus.

"Telemachus, we must get the weapons of war stowed away, every one of them. And when the suitors miss them and ask you about it, put them off with soft words: *I have laid them away out of the smoke, because they are no longer what they were when Odysseus left them behind and went to Troy; they are quite spoiled, as far as the breath of the fire has got at them. And here is a better reason, which some god put into my mind: I was afraid you might quarrel when you were in drink and wound one another and shame the feast and the courtship. Iron by itself draws a man on.*"

Telemachus called out to Eurycleia. "Nurse, shut the women up in their rooms while I put my father's weapons away in the store — good weapons, standing about neglected in the house, going dull with the smoke, since my father went away. I was a child then. I want to lay them away where the breath of the fire will not reach them."

"Child, I only wish you would take charge of the house and look after all your property. But who is to go with you and carry a light, if you will not let the maids come out?"

"This stranger. I will not have a man idle who eats my bread, however far away he has come from."

So she shut the doors of the hall. And the two of them jumped up, Odysseus and his son, and carried in the helmets and the bossed shields and the sharp spears; and Athena went in front of them with a golden lamp and made a beautiful light.

And Telemachus said suddenly: "Father, this is a great marvel I am looking at. The walls of the hall and the fine panels and the crossbeams of pine and the pillars standing up — they are all shining in my eyes as though there were a fire in here. Some god must be inside, one of those who hold the wide sky."

"Be quiet. Hold your thoughts in and ask no questions. This is the way of the gods who hold Olympus. Go to bed now. I shall stay here, to try the maids a little more, and your mother; and in her grief she will ask me about everything."

So Telemachus went off through the hall by the light of the blazing torches, to the room where he always slept, and lay down and waited for morning. And Odysseus was left in the hall, turning over the killing of the suitors with Athena's help.

---

Then Penelope came down out of her room, looking like Artemis (goddess of the hunt) or like golden Aphrodite (goddess of love). They set her chair by the fire where she always sat, worked with ivory and silver, which the craftsman Icmalius had made long ago; and he had put a footstool on it, part of the same piece, and a great fleece was thrown over it.

And the women came out and cleared away the piles of bread and the tables and the cups the men had been drinking from; and they threw the fire out of the braziers onto the ground and heaped fresh logs on them, for light and for warmth.

And Melantho started on Odysseus for the second time.

"Stranger, are you going to be a nuisance here all night as well, wandering about the house and spying on the women? Get out of doors and be satisfied with your dinner, or you will be hit with a torch and go out that way."

And Odysseus looked at her from under his brows.

"Strange woman, why do you come at me with such anger in your heart? Is it because I am dirty and wearing bad clothes on my body and I beg round the country? Necessity is on me. That is what beggars and vagrants are like.

"I too once lived in a house of my own among men, and I was rich, and my house was rich; and I gave a great many times to a wanderer, whoever he was and whatever he came in need of. And I had servants past counting and everything else that men live well by and are called wealthy for.

"And Zeus brought it all to nothing; he chose to. So take care, woman, that you do not lose all the beauty you shine with among the maids at present — in case your mistress grows angry with you and turns hard, or Odysseus comes home; there is still some share of hope left. And if he really has died and is never coming back, still there is his son Telemachus, who by the grace of Apollo is a grown man now; and there is not a woman in these halls behaving badly who will escape his notice, because he is not a child any longer."

Penelope heard him and rounded on the girl.

"You bold and shameless creature. You are not hiding your monstrous behaviour from me in the least, and you shall wipe it off with your own head. You knew perfectly well — you heard it from me — that I meant to question this stranger in my halls about my husband, because I am in great distress."

Then she said to the housekeeper: "Eurynome, bring a chair over and put a fleece on it, so the stranger can sit and talk to me and listen; I want to ask him some things."

---

She began where anybody would begin. "Stranger, this I shall ask you myself, first of all. Who are you, and where do you come from? Where is your city, and who are your parents?"

And he turned it aside without seeming to.

"Lady, no mortal on the boundless earth could find fault with you. Your name goes up to the wide sky, like the name of a blameless king, a god-fearing man ruling over many strong people and upholding justice, so that the dark earth bears him wheat and barley, and the trees are heavy with fruit, and the flocks never fail to bear, and the sea gives fish, all because of his good leadership; and the people prosper under him.

"So ask me anything else you like here in your house, but do not ask about my family and my own country, in case you fill my heart still fuller of pain by making me remember. I am a man of many sorrows. And it is not fitting for me to sit crying and grieving in another person's house; endless mourning does no good. One of your women might be angry with me, or you might yourself, and say I was swimming in tears because my head was heavy with wine."

"Stranger, the immortals destroyed my excellence and my beauty and my figure on the day the Greeks embarked for Ilium (Troy), and my husband Odysseus went with them. If he were to come and take care of my life, my name would be greater and better. As it is I am in trouble; a god has let loose so much of it on me.

"All the nobles who rule the islands — Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus — and those who live in clear-seen Ithaca itself, are courting me against my will and wearing away my house. And so I take no thought for strangers or suppliants or heralds, but I waste my heart away wanting Odysseus.

"They press on with the marriage, and I wind my schemes.

"First a god breathed it into me to set up a great loom in the hall and weave at a wide fine web; and I said to them: *Young men, my suitors — since Odysseus is dead, be patient about the marriage, however eager you are, until I finish this cloth; I do not want my spinning wasted. It is a shroud for the lord Laertes (father of Odysseus), against the day the pitiless end of death takes him. I will not have the Greek women in this country talking against me, that a man who won so much should lie without a covering.*

"So I said, and their proud hearts agreed. And after that I wove at the great web by day, and unravelled it by night, when I had torches set beside me. Three years I kept it from them and got away with it. But when the fourth came round with the seasons, then my women told them — shameless creatures — and they came and caught me at it, and shouted at me; and I finished it, against my will, because I had to.

"And now I cannot escape the marriage, and I cannot think of any other trick. My parents are pressing me to marry, and my son is angry as he watches his living eaten up, because he understands it now; he is a man, well able to look after a house that Zeus grants honour to.

"But tell me your family, all the same, and where you come from. Because you are not sprung from the oak in the old story, or from a rock."

---

And Odysseus told her a lie.

He told her he came from Crete, from the great city of Cnossus, where Minos was king; that his father was Deucalion, and his brother Idomeneus, who had gone off to Troy in the beaked ships; and that his own name was Aethon, and he was the younger of the two.

And he told her he had met Odysseus there — that the wind had driven Odysseus off course past Malea on his way to Troy and forced him into an awkward anchorage; that he had come up to the city looking for Idomeneus, calling him a dear and honoured friend, and had missed him by ten days or eleven; that he himself had taken Odysseus in and entertained him properly out of the household stores, and gathered barley and wine and cattle for sacrifice out of the public store for the men who were with him; and that they had stayed twelve days, penned in by a North Wind that some hard god had raised, and had put to sea on the thirteenth when it dropped.

He made all these lies of his sound like the truth as he told them.

And as she listened, the tears ran down and her face melted. The way the snow melts on the high mountains, that the East Wind thaws when the West Wind has piled it up, and as it melts the rivers run full — that is how her lovely cheeks melted as she cried for her husband, who was sitting there beside her.

And Odysseus pitied his wife in his heart as she grieved.

But his eyes stood in his head as though they were made of horn or iron, and he did not move them; and by guile he kept his tears back.

---

There is nothing in the poem I find harder than that pair of sentences, and I do not think the poem finds it easy either.

He pities her. The text says so plainly, and gives him the emotion before it gives him the concealment. And then it describes the concealment with a piece of physical detail so precise that you can feel him doing it: the eyes held still, not blinking, because a blink would spill it.

Everything he has been praised for in eighteen books is in that stillness. It is the same faculty that got him out of the cave and off the beach at Scheria and past the dogs at the farm, and it is not being used on an enemy. It is being used to watch his wife cry over him for as long as it takes.

I shall not tell you whether he is right. He has been told by a goddess to trust nobody, and there are a hundred and eight armed men in the town, and one loose word from a servant would finish him and his son together. The reasoning is sound. What the poem does is refuse to let the soundness of the reasoning make the thing comfortable, and it does that by giving him the pity first.

---

Then she decided to test him.

"Now, stranger, I think I shall try you, and find out whether you really did entertain my husband there in your halls with his companions, as you say. Tell me what sort of clothes he was wearing, and what kind of man he was himself, and tell me about the companions who went with him."

"Lady, it is hard to say, after so long. It is twenty years now since he went away from there and left my country. But I shall tell you as my heart pictures it.

"Odysseus was wearing a fleecy purple cloak, doubled over; and there was a brooch on it made of gold with a double clasp, and the front of it was worked in a curious way. A hound was holding a dappled fawn down between his forepaws and gripping it as it struggled; and everybody was amazed at it, at the way the two of them were gold and yet the hound was throttling the fawn and looking at it, and the fawn was writhing with its feet and trying to get away.

"And I noticed the tunic on his body, shining like the skin of a dried onion, it was so soft; and bright as the sun. A great many women looked at it and wondered.

"But I will tell you something else, and you must take it in. I do not know whether Odysseus wore those clothes at home, or whether one of his crew gave them to him when he went aboard the fast ship, or whether some host did; because Odysseus was dear to a great many men, and few Greeks were his equal.

"I gave him a bronze sword myself, and a fine purple cloak with a double fold, and a fringed tunic, and I sent him off with honour in his well-benched ship.

"And there was a herald attending him, a little older than he was, and I will tell you about him too. He was round in the shoulders and dark-skinned and curly-headed, and his name was Eurybates; and Odysseus honoured him above the rest of his companions, because the two of them thought alike."

And she cried still harder, because she knew the tokens Odysseus had given her, and they were unmistakable.[^1]

And when she had had her fill of weeping she said: "Stranger, until now you have only been pitied in this house; from now on you shall be loved and honoured. I gave him those clothes myself, the ones you describe. I folded them and brought them out of the store-room and put the bright brooch on them to be an ornament for him.

"And I shall never welcome him home again, coming back to his own dear country. It was an evil fate that took Odysseus off in the hollow ship to see that Ilium (Troy) that should not be named."

---

I want to point at the brooch, because of what he does with it.

He is asked for proof and he produces it, and he produces the right thing: not the sword, which she never saw, but the cloak she folded with her own hands and the pin she fastened on it. And the thing worked on that pin is a hound holding down a fawn and watching it struggle.

He picked that out of twenty years of memory and described it to her, in detail, twice — the gold that seems to move, the fawn's feet working, the eyes of the dog on it — while she sat opposite him and wept and did not know who she was talking to.

The poem does not remark on it. It simply hands you a little golden object, in a room where one person is being slowly and deliberately held, and lets it lie there.

---

Then he told her the rest of what he had told the swineherd — that he had heard of Odysseus close by, in the rich country of the Thesprotians, alive, and gathering treasure to bring home; that his ship and his companions had been lost off Thrinacia because the crew ate the cattle of the Sun; that the Phaeacians had honoured him like a god and were ready to send him home; and that he had gone to Dodona to ask the great oak of Zeus how he should come back to his own country, openly or in secret.

"So he is safe, and he will be here very soon. He will not be far from his friends and his own country much longer. And I shall swear you an oath on it. Let Zeus be my witness first, and this table of hospitality, and the hearth of Odysseus that I have come to: all of it will happen exactly as I say. Within this very month Odysseus will come here — as the old moon goes out and the new one comes in."

"Ah, stranger. I wish that word might be fulfilled. Then you would know my friendship soon enough, and get so many gifts from me that any man who met you would call you a lucky one.

"But this is how it will be, I think, in my heart. Odysseus is not coming home, and you will not get your passage from here, since there are no such masters in this house as Odysseus was among men — if there ever was such a man — to welcome honoured strangers and send them on their way.

"But come, women, wash him, and make up a bed — a bedstead and blankets and bright rugs — so that he can lie warm until the golden Dawn. And in the morning bathe him and rub him with oil, so that he can sit in the hall beside Telemachus and think about his breakfast. And it will be the worse for any man of them who annoys him and grieves his heart; that man will get nothing done here after that, however furiously he rages.

"Because how are you to find out about me, stranger — whether I really am better than other women in judgement and careful sense — if you sit down to eat in my hall unwashed and badly dressed? Men do not live long. If a man is harsh himself, and his mind is harsh, everybody prays that he may suffer while he lives, and when he is dead they all mock him. But if a man is blameless, and his mind is blameless, strangers carry his name far and wide across the world, and a great many people call him a good man."

"Honoured wife of Odysseus, cloaks and bright rugs became hateful to me on the day I left the snowy mountains of Crete behind me and went off in my long-oared ship. I shall lie down as I have lain through many sleepless nights before this. I have spent a great many nights on a poor bed, waiting for the bright Dawn.

"And footbaths give me no pleasure either. No woman of those who are servants in your house is going to touch my foot — unless there is some old and faithful one who has suffered as much in her heart as I have. I should not begrudge such a woman touching my feet."

"Dear stranger, no man so sensible has ever come to my house out of all the strangers from far away; everything you say is so well judged. I have an old woman with an understanding heart, who nursed and reared that unlucky man and took him in her arms the day his mother bore him. She shall wash your feet, weak as she is.

"Come now, sensible Eurycleia, get up and wash a man of your master's own age. Odysseus's feet must be like this by now, and his hands like this. Men grow old quickly in bad fortune."

---

The old woman covered her face with her hands and let the hot tears come.

"Oh, my child. I can do nothing for you. Zeus must have hated you above all men, god-fearing as your heart was. No mortal ever burned so many fat thigh-pieces to him, or such chosen sacrifices, as you gave him — praying that you might come to a sleek old age and bring up your glorious son. And now he has taken your day of homecoming away from you alone.

"I suppose the women in some strange country mocked at him the way these bitches all mock at you here, when he came to some great lord's fine house; and it is to get away from their insults and their taunting that you will not let them wash you.

"But the daughter of Icarius, sensible Penelope, has told me to, and I am not unwilling. I shall wash your feet, for Penelope's sake and for your own, because the heart in me is stirred with sorrow.

"And listen to what I am going to say. A great many hard-tried strangers have come here, but I do not think I ever saw a man so like another as you are like Odysseus — in your build, and your voice, and your feet."

"Old woman, everybody who has seen the two of us with their own eyes says the same: that we are extraordinarily like one another. You have noticed it yourself, and said so."

So the old woman took the shining cauldron she used for washing feet and poured cold water into it and added hot.

And Odysseus sat down at the hearth and turned quickly towards the dark, because it came to him all at once that when she handled him she might notice the scar, and everything would come out.

---

She came up and began to wash her master. And she knew the scar at once.

He had been given it long ago by a boar, with its white tusk, on Parnassus, when he had gone there to visit Autolycus and the sons of Autolycus — his mother's father, who was the finest man alive at thieving and at swearing oaths, a skill the god himself had given him, Hermes (the god of travellers, of luck, and of theft), because Odysseus's grandfather used to burn him acceptable offerings of the thighs of lambs and kids, and Hermes went along with him willingly.

Autolycus had come once to the rich country of Ithaca and found his daughter's son newly born; and Eurycleia had set the baby on his knees as he finished his supper, and said: "Autolycus, find a name yourself now for your child's own child. He has been prayed for a long time."

And Autolycus said: "Son-in-law, daughter — give him the name I say. I have come here as a man who has been angry with a great many people, men and women both, over the fruitful earth. So let his name be **Odysseus**, the man of anger. And when he is grown and comes to his mother's family's great house on Parnassus, where my property is, I shall give him some of it and send him home happy."[^2]

So he had gone there for the gifts, when he was a young man; and Autolycus and his sons had welcomed him and clasped his hands, and his grandmother Amphithea had put her arms round him and kissed his head and both his beautiful eyes; and they had killed a five-year-old bull and feasted all day.

And at first light they had gone out hunting, the dogs and the sons of Autolycus, and Odysseus with them, up the steep wooded mountain, into the windy hollows. The sun was just striking on the fields, coming up out of the deep-flowing Ocean, when the beaters came into a glade. The dogs went in front, tracking, and the sons of Autolycus behind them, and Odysseus close up behind the dogs with his long spear swinging.

There was a great boar lying in a thick lair, where the force of the wet winds never blew and the bright sun never struck through and the rain could not get in, it was so thick; and there was a great heap of fallen leaves in it. And the noise of the feet of men and dogs came round him as they closed in, and he came out of the lair against them with his back bristling and his eyes flashing, and stood at bay close in front of them.

Odysseus went in first, holding the long spear up in his heavy hand, wanting to strike. And the boar was too quick for him and came in sideways above the knee, and tore a long gash in the flesh with his tusk, and did not reach the bone. And Odysseus hit him in the right shoulder, and the point of the bright spear went clean through, and he went down in the dust with a scream and the life flew out of him.

And the sons of Autolycus busied themselves over the carcass and bound up the wound of faultless godlike Odysseus skilfully, and stopped the black blood with a charm; and they went back quickly to their father's house, and healed him, and gave him splendid presents, and sent him off gladly to Ithaca. And his father and his honoured mother were glad when he came, and asked about everything, and about the scar, and how he had come by it. And he told them the whole story properly.

---

That is where the name came from, and the poem has kept it back for nineteen books.

I told you in Book Nine that *Odysseus* is built on a verb meaning to be angry with, to hate, to deal out pain, and that it runs both ways at once — the man who is hated, and the man who causes trouble. Here at last is the naming itself, and it is done by a grandfather who is famous for thieving and for perjury, over his supper, with the baby on his knees. He does not name the child for a hope, or a god, or an ancestor. He names it after his own bad temper.

And the poem produces the whole story here, of all the places — inside the sentence where an old woman's hands are moving down a leg towards the mark that will tell her who he is. It is the longest digression in the poem and it arrives at the worst possible moment, and readers have complained about it for two thousand years. I think the placing is the point. Everything is suspended: the water, the hands, the room, the wife eight feet away. And into that gap the poem pours the one thing it has never told you, which is what this man's name means and who gave it to him.

Then the hands find the scar.

---

The old woman took the scar in the flat of her hands and knew it by the touch, and let the foot go. The leg dropped into the basin, and the bronze rang, and it tipped over, and the water went out on the floor.

And joy and grief took hold of her at the same moment, and both her eyes filled with tears, and her voice would not come out.

She put her hand on his chin and said: "You are Odysseus. Dear child. And I never knew you — not until I had handled the whole body of my master."

And she looked across at Penelope, wanting to tell her that her husband was in the room.

And Penelope could not meet her eye and did not notice, because Athena had turned her mind aside.

And Odysseus felt for the old woman's throat with his right hand and took hold of it, and pulled her closer to him with the other.

"Mother. Do you want to destroy me? You nursed me yourself at your own breast. And now, after suffering a great deal, I have come in the twentieth year to my own country. But since you have found me out, and a god has put it in your heart — be quiet. Let nobody else in this house hear of it.

"Because I will tell you plainly, and it will be brought about. If a god beats the lordly suitors down under me, I shall not spare you, nurse though you are and though you nursed me, when I kill the other serving women in my halls."

"My child, what a thing to let past the barrier of your teeth. You know how firm I am, and how unyielding. I shall hold as hard as solid stone, or iron.

"And I will tell you something else; take it in. If a god beats the lordly suitors down under you, then I shall tell you about the women in the halls — which of them dishonour you and which are innocent."

"Nurse, why would you tell me about them? There is no need. I shall take note of every one of them myself and find them out. Keep it to yourself, and leave the rest to the gods."

---

Sit with that for a moment, because it is the coldest thing he does in the poem.

An old woman who fed him at her own breast has just recognised him, from a scar, on her knees, with her hands in the water; and she is crying with joy; and his response is to take her by the throat.

He does it for the same reason he does everything, and the reason is good: she was on the point of calling across the room, and if she had, he and his son would both be dead by morning. And he does not merely silence her. He tells her that when the killing starts he will kill her too if she is among the women who have gone over, nurse or no nurse.

She is not offended, which is the strangest part. She says only that she will hold like stone, and then offers to give him the names — and he refuses even that, because he does not want the list from her. He wants to watch them himself.

---

Then she went off to fetch more water, since the first lot was all spilled; and when she had washed him and rubbed him with oil, Odysseus pulled his chair nearer the fire again to get warm, and hid the scar under his rags.

And Penelope said:

"Stranger, I shall ask you one small thing more myself, because it will soon be time for rest — for anybody on whom sweet sleep comes, however troubled he is. But a god has given me sorrow past all measuring. All day I take a kind of pleasure in mourning and grieving while I see to my own work and the women's in the house; and when the night comes and sleep takes everyone, I lie on my bed, and the sharp cares come crowding thick about my beating heart and torment me while I mourn.

"And my heart goes to and fro in doubt — whether to stay with my son and keep everything safe, my property and my servants and my great high-roofed house, respecting my husband's bed and what the people say; or to go now with whichever of the Greeks courting me in the halls is the best man and brings the most gifts.

"My son, while he was a child and thoughtless, would not let me marry and leave my husband's house. Now he is grown and has come to the full measure of manhood, and he prays me to go out of the hall again, because he is troubled about his property, which the Greeks are eating in front of him.

"But listen to this dream of mine, and interpret it for me.

"I have twenty geese about the house that come out of the water and eat wheat, and it warms me to watch them. And a great eagle with a crooked beak came down off the mountain and broke all their necks and killed them; and they lay strewn about the hall in a heap, and he went up into the bright sky.

"And I cried and wailed, in the dream, and the Greek women gathered round me while I grieved bitterly because the eagle had killed my geese.

"And he came back and perched on a jutting roof-beam, and checked my crying with a mortal man's voice, and said: *Take heart, daughter of far-famed Icarius. This is no dream but a waking vision, a good one, and it will be brought about. The geese are the suitors; and I who was the eagle a moment ago am your husband, back again, and I shall let loose a cruel death on all of them.*

"And the honey-sweet sleep let me go. And I looked round and saw the geese in the hall pecking at their wheat by the trough, exactly where they always are."[^3]

"Lady, there is no way of turning that dream aside and reading it otherwise. Odysseus himself has told you how he means to bring it about. Destruction is plain for every one of the suitors; not a man of them will get away from death."

"Stranger, dreams are awkward things, and hard to read, and they do not all come true for people.

"There are two gates for shadowy dreams. One is made of horn and one of ivory. The dreams that come through the sawn ivory deceive us, and bring words that come to nothing. The ones that come out through the polished horn bring true things to pass, when a mortal sees them.

"But I do not think my strange dream came from there. If it had, it would have been very welcome to me and to my son.

"And I will tell you something else; take it in. This coming dawn is the evil-named one that will take me out of the house of Odysseus. Because now I mean to set them a contest: the axes, which he used to stand up in a row in his halls, like the props of a ship, twelve of them altogether; and he would stand a long way off and shoot an arrow through the whole line.

"Now I shall set this contest before the suitors. And whichever man most easily strings the bow in his hands and shoots an arrow through all twelve axes — with him I shall go, and leave this house of my marriage, so beautiful, and so full of everything a life needs, which I think I shall remember even in my dreams."

"Honoured wife of Odysseus. Do not put this contest off any longer in your halls. Odysseus of the many devices will be here before these men can handle that polished bow and string it and shoot an arrow through the iron."

"Stranger, if you were willing to sit beside me in the hall and give me pleasure, sleep would never be shed on my eyelids. But people cannot go without sleep forever. I shall go up to my room and lie down on my bed, which has become a bed of mourning for me, always wet with my tears since the day Odysseus went off to see that Ilium that should not be named. I shall lie down there; and you lie here in the house — either put something on the floor, or let them set a bedstead for you."

And she went up to her bright upper room, and her women went with her; and when she had gone up she cried for Odysseus, her own dear husband, until Athena cast a sweet sleep on her eyelids.

---

I shall not tell you what she knows, because I do not think anybody can, and I would rather leave you holding the question than hand you a bad answer.

Consider only the order of events. She has spent the evening with a stranger who described her husband's clothes, down to the pin she fastened herself. He has sworn her a formal oath, on the hearth, that Odysseus will be here inside the month. She has told him a dream in which an eagle kills the suitors and announces that he is her husband — and then said, immediately, that she does not think it was a true dream, which is a thing people say when they have been told something they want too much to believe.

And then, that same night, having waited twenty years and put the marriage off by every device she could invent, she announces that tomorrow she will bring out her husband's bow and marry whichever man can use it.

It may be despair. She says it is: this is the evil dawn, my son is grown, I cannot hold them off any longer. It may be that a woman who has been listening very carefully all evening has decided to put a bow into the room and see what happens. The poem does not say, and the argument has run for two thousand years without settling.[^4]

What I will say is this. Whatever she does or does not suspect, she has just arranged the one contest in the world that her husband cannot lose and no other man can win, on the one night he happens to be sitting by her fire, and she has done it herself, without being asked, without being told to, and against her own interest.

## NOTES TO BOOK NINETEEN

[^1]: **The tokens.** In a poem full of recognitions this is the first, and it establishes the pattern all the others follow: not a face, not a voice, but a *thing that only two people could know*. Penelope does not ask what her husband looked like; she asks what he was wearing, which is the harder question and the better one. Anybody who had seen Odysseus could describe his build. Only somebody who was there could describe the pin. — Note also what she does with the answer. She does not leap up. She weeps, and says that from now on he will be honoured in her house, and then goes on talking. Every recognition in this poem is followed immediately by a test of some kind, and by somebody keeping their head.

[^2]: **The name, and the man who gave it.** See Book Nine, note 1, where the meaning was set out and the story promised. Here it is: Autolycus, the hero's grandfather on his mother's side, celebrated for two accomplishments — theft and perjury — both of them gifts from Hermes, who was pleased with his offerings. He arrives, finds a newborn on the premises, and is invited to name it; and he chooses a name that means, roughly, *the one who gives and gets grief*, on the grounds that he himself has been angry with a great many people in his time. It is the only naming scene in the poem and it explains a good deal, including where the cleverness came from and why nobody in this family expects the world to be simple.

[^3]: **The gates of horn and ivory.** This is the passage's most famous line and it is a pun, which is why it works in Greek and merely sounds handsome in English. The Greek for *horn* is close to a verb meaning to fulfil or bring to pass, and the Greek for *ivory* is close to a verb meaning to deceive. So true dreams come through the gate whose name sounds like *fulfil*, and false ones through the gate whose name sounds like *deceive*. It is not an explanation; it is a piece of wordplay offered as one — and Penelope, who has just been told a dream that says exactly what she wants to hear, uses it to argue herself out of believing it. That is the real point of the passage, and it is heartbreakingly ordinary: it is what people do with good news they cannot afford to trust.

[^4]: **Does she know?** The central argument of *Odyssey* criticism, and nobody has won it. The case for yes: she suddenly finds this beggar dearer than any guest she has had; she asks him for exactly the proof he can give; she tells him her most private dream; she comes down and announces the contest of the bow within an hour of being given a sworn oath that her husband will arrive this month; and the contest she chooses is one only her husband can win. The case for no: the poem says several times that Athena put things into her mind, which would be a strange way to describe a woman acting on her own conclusions; she says plainly and repeatedly that she does not believe he is coming; and when the truth finally arrives she does not behave in the least like someone who suspected it. — My own view, for what it is worth, is that the question may be badly posed. People are perfectly capable of knowing a thing and not knowing it at the same time, and of taking an action for reasons they could not state if you asked them. The poem gives us her behaviour and withholds her mind, and it does that on purpose, and it does it to nobody else in the book.
