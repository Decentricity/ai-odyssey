# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK ELEVEN
### In Which He Digs a Hole, and the Dead Come Up to Talk

---

This is the middle — not roughly the middle, but the middle. Everything before this book goes away from home and everything after it comes back towards home, and the hinge is a hole in the ground at the edge of the world with a man sitting beside it holding a sword. It is also the first descent to the country of the dead in European literature, and every one that came afterwards walks in its footprints. One warning and then he can have it back: about halfway through, the poem will stop him and take us out to the dining hall for a few minutes. That is not a join in the manuscript. Wait for it.

---

We went down to the ship and dragged her into the bright water and put the mast and the sail in her, and took the sheep aboard, and went aboard ourselves, in a bad way, letting the big tears fall. And Circe sent us a following wind behind the dark-prowed ship, a good companion, to fill the sail. All day her sail was stretched as she crossed the sea, and the sun went down and all the ways grew dark.

And she came to the edge of deep-flowing Ocean, where the country and the city of the Cimmerian people are, wrapped in fog and cloud. The shining sun never looks down on them with his rays — not when he climbs up towards the starry sky, and not when he turns back down again — and a deadly night is stretched over those wretched people.

We ran the ship ashore and got the sheep out, and went along beside the stream of Ocean until we came to the place Circe had described. There Perimedes and Eurylochus held the animals, and I drew the sharp sword off my thigh and dug the pit, a forearm's length this way and that. And I poured the offering round it to all the dead: first with milk and honey, then with sweet wine, and the third time with water; and I sprinkled white barley meal over all of it. And I prayed hard to the strengthless heads of the dead, and promised them that when I got to Ithaca I would sacrifice a barren heifer in my halls, the best I had, and pile the altar high with good things; and that to Teiresias by himself I would offer a ram, black all over, the finest in my flocks.

And when I had finished praying I took the sheep and cut their throats over the pit, and the dark blood ran into it.

And they came up out of the darkness underneath. Brides came, and unmarried boys, and old men who had suffered a great deal, and soft young girls with hearts new to grief; and a great many men wounded with bronze spears, killed in war, still in their bloodied armour. They came crowding round the pit from every direction with an unearthly crying, and a pale fear took hold of me.

And I told my companions to flay the sheep and burn them and pray to the gods — to mighty Hades and to dread Persephone (his queen, who rules the dead beside him). And I drew the sharp sword off my thigh and sat there, and would not let the strengthless heads of the dead come near the blood until I had asked Teiresias.[^1]

---

The first soul that came was my own companion Elpenor. He had not been buried under the wide-wayed earth; we had left his body in Circe's house, unwept and unburied, because another task was pressing us.

I cried when I saw him. *Elpenor — how did you get down here under the fog and the dark? You have come quicker on foot than I did in my black ship.*

And he groaned and answered me. *Son of Laertes. An evil fate from some god did for me, and an immeasurable quantity of wine. I lay down on Circe's roof and never thought to go back down by the long ladder, and I came down head first, and my neck was broken out of its spine.*

*And now I beg you by the ones left behind, who are not here — by your wife, and by your father who brought you up, and by Telemachus, the only son you left in your halls — because I know that when you go from here you will bring your ship in at Aeaea again — there, my lord, remember me. Do not go away and leave me unwept and unburied and turn your back on me, or I may become a reason for the gods to be angry with you. Burn me with my armour, all that I have, and heap up a mound for me on the shore of the grey sea, for an unlucky man, so that people yet to come will know. Do that for me. And plant on the mound the oar I used to row with, when I was alive and with my companions.*

*I will do all of it, my poor friend. I will see to it.*

So the two of us sat there exchanging these unhappy words, I on one side holding my sword over the blood, and the ghost of my companion on the other side, saying a great deal.

---

And then came the soul of my dead mother, Anticleia, the daughter of great-hearted Autolycus, whom I had left alive when I sailed for holy Troy. I cried when I saw her, and I pitied her in my heart. And even so I would not let her come near the blood, however badly it hurt, until I had asked Teiresias.

And the soul of Teiresias of Thebes came, holding a golden staff, and he knew me.

*Son of Laertes. Why have you left the light of the sun, you unlucky man, and come to see the dead and this place where there is no joy? Stand back from the pit and hold that sharp sword away, so that I can drink the blood and tell you the truth.*

I stepped back and pushed the silver-studded sword into its sheath. And when he had drunk the dark blood, the faultless prophet spoke.

*You are asking about a sweet homecoming, glorious Odysseus. And a god is going to make it hard for you. Because I do not think you will get past the Earthshaker (Poseidon). He has stored up anger against you in his heart, and he is furious about his own dear son, whose eye you put out.*

*Even so, you may still get home, though you will suffer for it — if you can hold your own spirit in, and your companions'. When you first bring your ship in at the island of Thrinacia, escaping the violet sea, you will find the cattle grazing there, and the fat flocks of Helios, who sees everything and hears everything. If you leave those alone and keep your mind on getting home, then all of you may still reach Ithaca, badly off as you will be. But if you harm them, then I am telling you now: destruction for your ship and for your men. And if you get out of it yourself, you will come home late, and in a bad way, having lost every one of your companions, in somebody else's ship. And you will find trouble in your house — arrogant men eating up your living, courting your godlike wife and offering her bride-gifts.*

*But you will pay them out for their violence when you come. And when you have killed the suitors in your own halls, whether by a trick or openly with the sharp bronze — then go. Take a well-made oar and go on until you come to men who know nothing about the sea, and eat their food without salt in it, and have never heard of ships with painted cheeks, or of the shaped oars that are the wings of ships. And I will tell you a very clear sign, and you will not miss it. When another traveller meets you on the road and says you have a winnowing fan on your broad shoulder — then plant your shaped oar in the ground, and make a fine sacrifice to the lord Poseidon: a ram, a bull, and a boar that mates with sows. And go home, and offer sacred hecatombs (great sacrifices, like 100 cattle) to the immortal gods, to all of them in their order.*

*And death will come to you far from the sea. A very gentle death. It will take you when you are worn down with a sleek old age, and your people will be prosperous around you. That is the truth I am telling you.*

*Teiresias — I suppose the gods themselves have spun all this out. But tell me this. I see the soul of my dead mother there. She sits by the blood without a word, and she cannot bring herself to look straight at her own son, or speak to him. Tell me, lord — how can she know who I am?*

*That is easily said. Any one of the dead you let come near the blood will speak the truth to you. Any one you refuse will go back again.*

And the soul of lord Teiresias went away into the house of Hades, having said his prophecy.

---

And I stayed where I was until my mother came up and drank the dark blood. And she knew me at once, and cried.

*My child. How did you come down here under the murky dark, and you alive? It is hard for the living to see these things. There are great rivers between, and terrible waters, and Ocean first of all, which nobody can cross on foot; you must have a well-built ship. Have you only now come here from Troy, wandering all this time with your ship and your men? Have you not been to Ithaca yet? Have you not seen your wife in your own house?*

*Mother — necessity brought me down to Hades, to consult the soul of Teiresias. I have not been near Greece yet. I have not set foot on my own country. I have been wandering, and there has been nothing but trouble, since the first day I followed Agamemnon to Ilium (Troy). But tell me this truly. What death took you? Was it a long illness? Or did Artemis (goddess of the hunt, whose arrows bring a quiet death to women) come and kill you with her gentle shafts? And tell me about my father, and about the son I left behind. Is my position still theirs? Or does some other man hold it now? And tell me about my wife — what she intends, and what is in her mind. Is she still with the boy, keeping everything safe? Or has one of the Greeks married her by now?*

*She is still in your halls, and her heart is steady, and she is enduring it. The nights waste away for her, wretchedly, and the days, and she is crying. Nobody holds your position. Telemachus keeps your lands undisturbed, and dines at the feasts a man who deals out judgements ought to dine at; everybody invites him.*

*But your father stays out on the farm and does not come into the town at all. He has no bedstead now, and no cloaks, and no bright coverlets. In the winter he sleeps in the house where the servants sleep, in the ashes by the fire, and wears bad clothes. And when the summer comes, and the rich autumn, there are beds of fallen leaves heaped low all over the slope of his vineyard, and he lies down on those, grieving, feeding a great sorrow in his heart and longing for you to come back. And a hard old age has come on him.*

*And that is how I died too. It was not the sharp-eyed archer goddess who came and killed me in my house with her gentle arrows, and it was not a disease, the sort that most often takes the spirit out of the limbs by a horrible wasting. It was missing you. It was longing for you, and for your good sense, glorious Odysseus, and for your kindness. That took my honey-sweet life away.*

And I wanted to take hold of the soul of my dead mother. Three times I went for her, and my heart urged me to hold her. Three times she went out of my hands like a shadow, or like a dream. And the grief in my heart got sharper every time.

*Mother — why do you not stay for me, when I want to hold you? Why can we not put our arms round each other, even here in Hades, and take our fill of cold grieving? Is this some phantom that dread Persephone has sent me, to make me cry even harder?*

*My child. Most unlucky of all men. Persephone is not deceiving you. This is simply the way it is with mortals when a person dies. The sinews no longer hold the flesh and the bones together. The great force of the blazing fire destroys all that, as soon as the life leaves the white bones. And the soul flutters off like a dream and goes to and fro.*

*Get back to the light, quickly. And take all of this in, so that you can tell your wife afterwards.*

---

That last instruction is the thing to keep. She does not say *remember me*, or *avenge me*, or *bury me properly*. She has one child in front of her whom she died of missing, and what she does with her few words is send him home to somebody else and give him something to say when he gets there. It is the only thing left that she can do about twenty years of waiting for a man to walk in at the door.

---

Then Persephone sent up the souls of the women, all of them the wives and daughters of great men, and they gathered in a crowd round the dark blood; and I drew the long sword off my thigh and would not let them all drink together, but let them come one at a time, and each of them told me her lineage.

Tyro came, who fell in love with a river and was loved by Poseidon in the river's shape, and bore Pelias and Neleus. Antiope came, who bore Amphion and Zethus, the two who first built the foundations of seven-gated Thebes and walled it, because for all their strength they could not live in Thebes without walls. And Alcmene came, who bore Heracles to Zeus; and Megara, the daughter of Creon, whom Heracles married.

And I saw the mother of Oedipus, the beautiful Epicaste, who did a monstrous thing without knowing what she was doing, and married her own son; and he had killed his own father and married her. And the gods made it known among men straight away. And he stayed on in lovely Thebes, ruling the Cadmeans, suffering, through the destructive plans of the gods. And she went down into the house of Hades, having tied a noose to a high roofbeam, in the grip of her own grief. And she left him pains past counting — everything a mother's avenging Furies bring about.[^2]

And Chloris came, and Leda who bore Castor and Polydeuces, whom the earth holds, and they are alive, and even under the earth they have honour from Zeus, and live on alternate days and are dead on alternate days. And Iphimedeia came, who bore Otus and Ephialtes, the two tallest and handsomest men the grain-giving earth ever fed after Orion: at nine years old they were nine cubits across and nine fathoms tall, and they threatened to raise war against the immortals themselves, and set about piling Ossa on Olympus and Pelion on Ossa so as to climb into the sky — and they would have brought it off, if they had reached the full measure of manhood, but Apollo killed them both before the down flowered on their cheeks.

And I saw Phaedra, and Procris, and beautiful Ariadne, the daughter of Minos, whom Theseus tried to bring from Crete to holy Athens and got no joy of, because Artemis killed her first on the island of Dia. And Maera, and Clymene, and hateful Eriphyle, who took gold as the price of her own husband.

I could not tell you or name all of them, all the wives and daughters of heroes that I saw; the immortal night would be used up first. And it is time to sleep, either on board the fast ship with my crew, or here in your house. My escort is a matter for the gods and for you.

---

That is the sound of a man putting his cup down. He has been talking since dinner; he has just walked a hall full of listeners along a wall of famous names; and he has stopped in the middle of it and said, in effect, *goodness, look at the time.*

---

And the whole room stayed silent, held in the spell of it, through the shadowy hall. And the first person to speak was the queen.

"Phaeacians. What do you make of this man — his looks, his size, and the balance of the mind inside him? He is my guest, but every one of you has a share in the honour of him. So do not be in a hurry to send him off, and do not be mean with your gifts to a man in such need. You have a great deal of treasure lying in your houses by the will of the gods."

And the old lord Echeneus said: "Friends, our wise queen has not spoken wide of the mark, and not wide of what we were all thinking. Do as she says — though the word and the deed rest with Alcinous here."

And Alcinous said: "What she says will stand, as surely as I am alive and rule over the Phaeacians who love the oar. Let our guest hold on, however much he wants to be off, until tomorrow — until I have made the gifts complete. The escort will be the men's business, and mine most of all, since the power in this country is mine."

And Odysseus said: "Lord Alcinous. If you told me to stay here for a year, and were getting my escort ready and giving me splendid gifts, I would take that too. It would be much better to reach my own country with a fuller hand; I would be more respected, and better loved, by every man who saw me come home to Ithaca."

And Alcinous said: "Odysseus — looking at you, we do not take you for a cheat or a liar, the sort the black earth breeds in great numbers, men who fashion falsehoods out of things nobody could ever check. There is a shapeliness on your words, and there is a good mind inside you. You have told your story with skill, the way a singer does.

"But tell me this. Did you see any of your godlike companions down there — men who went with you to Ilium (Troy) and met their fate? The night is long. Immeasurably long. It is not time to sleep in the hall yet. Tell me the marvellous things. I could hold out until the bright dawn, if you could bear to stay in this hall and tell me your troubles."

---

A king has just praised his guest for not being the sort of man who invents stories out of things nobody can verify, and has given as his reason that the man tells a story as well as a professional storyteller. The evening's material so far has included a one-eyed giant, a floating island, a woman who turns men into pigs and a conversation with the dead. I do not think Alcinous is a fool — he has been the most sensible man in four books — and I think the poem is grinning at us over its own frame. Note what else the interval does. The cups come back, and the queen says *do not be mean with your gifts*, and the guest replies that he would happily stay a year if the presents were good enough, because he would rather arrive home rich. He is not confessing at a dinner party. He is earning his fare.

---

Lord Alcinous — there is a time for many words and there is a time for sleep. But if you are still eager to listen, I would not grudge you things more pitiful still: the sufferings of my companions, who got out from under the war-cry of the Trojans and then died on the way home, through the will of an evil woman.

When Persephone had scattered the souls of the women, there came the soul of Agamemnon, grieving; and round him were gathered the others who had died with him in the house of Aegisthus. He knew me at once when he had drunk the dark blood. He wept shrilly and stretched his hands out towards me, wanting to hold me; but there was no strength left in him, and none of the force his supple limbs used to have.

I cried when I saw him. *Most glorious son of Atreus, lord of men. What death took you? Did Poseidon take you on your ships, raising a foul blast of cruel winds? Or did enemies do it on dry land, while you were cutting out their cattle and their fine flocks, or fighting for a city and its women?*

*It was not Poseidon on the ships, and it was not enemies on dry land. Aegisthus arranged my death and my fate, and killed me with the help of my accursed wife. He asked me to his house. He gave me dinner. And he killed me the way a man kills an ox at the manger.*

*So I died the most pitiful death. And round me my companions were slaughtered without a pause, like white-tusked pigs killed in the house of a rich and powerful man for a wedding or a feast day. You have been present at the killing of a great many men, cut down one at a time or in the press of a battle. But you would have been sorriest of all in your heart if you had seen that: how we lay in the hall among the mixing bowls and the loaded tables, and the whole floor swam with blood.*

*And the most pitiful thing I heard was the voice of Cassandra, whom treacherous Clytemnestra (Agememnon's wife, remarried to Aegisthus, and Helen's twin sister) killed beside me. And I lifted my hands and beat the ground with them as I died, with the sword in me. And that shameless woman turned her back and walked away. She would not even use her hands to close my eyes and shut my mouth, though I was going down to Hades.*

*So there is nothing more terrible, nothing more shameless, than a woman who can put a thing like that in her mind. I thought I was coming home welcome to my children and my household. And she has poured shame on herself, and on the women who come after her, and even on the decent ones.*

*Zeus has been terribly hard on the house of Atreus (Agememnon's father) from the beginning, through the plans of women. A great many of us died for Helen's sake, and Clytemnestra was laying her trap for you while you were far away.*

*So never be gentle to your own wife either. Do not tell her every plan you know. Tell her part, and keep part hidden. Though death will not come to you from your wife, Odysseus. She is far too sensible, and knows too well what good counsel is — Penelope, the daughter of Icarius. We left her behind a young bride when we went off to the war. There was a boy at her breast, an infant. He must be sitting among grown men by now, and lucky, because his father will see him when he comes, and he will put his arms round his father, which is how it should be. My wife did not let me have my fill of looking at my own son. She killed me first.*

*And here is one more thing; put it away in your mind. Bring your ship in to your own dear country secretly, not in the open. There is no trusting women.*

*But tell me — have you heard anything about my son? Is he alive? Because noble Orestes is not dead anywhere on this earth.*

*Son of Atreus, why ask me this? I have no idea whether he is alive or dead, and it is a bad thing to talk empty as the wind.*

So the two of us stood there exchanging these unhappy words, grieving, letting the tears fall.

---

And then came the soul of Achilles, and of Patroclus, and of faultless Antilochus, and of Ajax, who was the best of the Greeks in looks and in build after the son of Peleus. And the soul of Achilles knew me.

*Son of Laertes. You reckless man. What enormous thing will you think of next? How did you dare to come down to Hades, where the senseless dead live, the worn-out images of men?*

*Achilles, son of Peleus — far the strongest of the Greeks. I came for Teiresias, to see if he could tell me any plan for getting back to rugged Ithaca. Because I have not been near Greece yet, or set foot on my own country, and I have had nothing but trouble. But you, Achilles — no man was ever more blessed than you, and no man ever will be. When you were alive we Greeks honoured you like a god, and now you are down here you rule mightily among the dead. So do not grieve at being dead, Achilles.*

*Do not talk to me smoothly about death, glorious Odysseus. I would rather be alive and working the soil for another man — a landless man, with nothing much to live on — than be king over all the dead who are used up.*

That is Achilles, who was offered the choice in the older poem between a long quiet life at home and a short one ending under the walls of Troy with a name that would never die, and who took the short one on purpose, knowing the terms; and who is the reason that poem exists. He has had a while to think about it. The *Iliad* is a poem about a young man choosing glory over length of life, and the *Odyssey* puts that young man in a hole in the ground and asks him how it went.

*But tell me about my son. Did he follow you to the war, to be a leader? And tell me about noble Peleus, if you have heard anything. Do the Myrmidons still honour him, or do they treat him badly through Greece, because old age has him by the hands and feet? I am not up there under the sun's rays to help him — not the man I used to be, when I killed the best of their army on the wide plain of Troy. If I could come to my father's house like that for even a little while, I would make my strength and my untouchable hands something to be afraid of, for anyone doing him violence.*

And I told him about Neoptolemus: that I had brought him myself from Scyros; that in council he was always first to speak and never got it wrong, and only Nestor and I could beat him at it; that in the fighting he never stayed in the crowd but ran far out in front, and gave way to nobody, and killed a great many men. And I told him about the horse — how all the best of us sat inside it, and the leaders were weeping and their legs were shaking, and I never once saw that young man's fine skin go pale or saw him wipe a tear off his face; he kept begging me to let him out, handling his sword-hilt and his heavy bronze spear. And I told him that when we had sacked the steep city of Priam he went aboard his ship with his share and a fine prize, without a scratch on him.

And the soul of Achilles went away over the field of asphodel with long strides — glad, because I had told him his son was outstanding.

---

The other souls of the dead and gone stood about grieving, and each one asked me about the people they cared for. Only the soul of Ajax kept away, still furious about the victory I had won over him beside the ships, in the contest for the armour of Achilles.

I wish I had never won a contest like that. For the sake of that armour the earth closed over a head like his — Ajax, who was above all the other Greeks in his looks and in what he did, after the son of Peleus.

And I spoke to him gently. *Ajax, son of faultless Telamon. Were you not going to forget your anger with me, even in death, over that ruinous armour? The gods made it a disaster for the Greeks. Such a tower of a man we lost in you. We grieve for you constantly, the way we grieve for the life of Achilles; and nobody is to blame but Zeus, who hated the Greek army terribly and laid your fate on you. Come here, my lord. Hear what I am saying. Put down your anger and your lordly spirit.*

He did not answer me. He went off into the darkness after the other souls of the dead.

Even then he might have spoken to me, angry as he was, or I to him — but the heart in my breast wanted to see the souls of the others.

In a book whose entire machinery exists to make the dead speak, one man is addressed by name, apologised to, praised and begged, and turns round and walks away without a syllable. And notice what it costs the speaker: *I wish I had never won a contest like that* is the only place in twenty-four books where Odysseus wishes he had lost. He does not say it about the Cyclops's eye, or the horse, or the town at Ismarus. He says it about a prize he won fairly.

---

And I saw Minos, the son of Zeus, holding a golden staff, sitting and giving judgements to the dead; and they sat and stood about him through the wide-gated house of Hades, asking him to settle their cases. And after him I saw huge Orion, driving the wild beasts together over the field of asphodel, the same ones he killed himself on the lonely hills, with an unbreakable bronze club in his hands.

And I saw Tityos, the son of Earth, lying on the ground; he covered nine acres of it. Two vultures sat on either side of him tearing at his liver, going right into the flesh, and his hands could not push them off. He had laid hands on Leto as she was going to Delphi.

And I saw Tantalus, in hard pain, standing in a pool with the water up against his chin. He looked like a man dying of thirst and he could not get anything to drink: every time the old man stooped down, mad for a drink, the water was swallowed away and vanished, and the black earth showed round his feet. And there were tall trees over his head dropping their fruit down at him — pears and pomegranates and shining apples and sweet figs and ripe olives — and every time he reached up for them, the wind tossed them away to the shadowy clouds.

And I saw Sisyphus, in violent pain, wrestling a monstrous stone with both arms. He would brace his hands and feet and shove it up towards the top of a hill; and just as he was about to get it over the crest, the weight of it would turn him back, and the shameless stone would come rolling down to the flat ground again. And he would strain and shove it back up, and the sweat poured off his limbs, and the dust rose off his head.[^3]

And after him I saw the mighty Heracles — his phantom. He himself is among the immortal gods, enjoying their feasts, and he has Hebe for a wife. Round him there was a clamour from the dead like the noise of birds, and they scattered everywhere in terror; and he came on like the black night, with his bow bare and an arrow on the string, glaring terribly, like a man about to shoot. There was a fearful belt about his chest, a golden baldric, and marvellous things were worked on it: bears and wild boars and lions with their eyes flashing, and battles and fighting and killings and men being slaughtered. I hope whoever put his craft into that belt never made another one.

He knew me the moment he saw me. *Son of Laertes. Are you dragging out a bad destiny too, the same as the one I carried under the rays of the sun? I was the son of Zeus, and I had trouble past all measuring. I was made subject to a man far worse than I was, and he laid hard labours on me. He even sent me down here once, to bring up the hound — because he thought there could be no harder task; and I brought the hound up and led him out of Hades, and Hermes guided me, and Athena.*[^4]

And he went back into the house of Hades.

But I stayed there, in case some other one of the heroes who died long ago might come, and I might have seen the men I wanted to see, Theseus and Peirithous. But before that, the tribes of the dead came thronging up with an unearthly cry, and a pale fear took hold of me, in case dread Persephone should send me up the head of the Gorgon out of the house of Hades.

So I went back to the ship at once and told my men to get aboard and cast off the cables. They went aboard and sat down at the oars. And the current carried us down the stream of Ocean; oars first, and after that a fair wind.

## NOTES TO BOOK ELEVEN

[^1]: **The pit, the blood, and where this is.** Three things a modern reader needs, and none of them are what most people expect. — *First:* he does not go down. Circe's directions send him to the far edge of Ocean, to a beach, where he digs a small pit and the dead come *up*. There is no descent, no gate, no guide, no tour; he never sets foot in the house of Hades and never sees it. The full underworld journey — the crossing, the ferryman, the levels — is later machinery, mostly Virgil's and Dante's, and it is not here. — *Second:* the blood. The dead in this poem are not spirits in the modern sense but worn-out images, without strength, without voice, and without recognition; they cannot even *know* him until they have drunk. What the blood gives back, briefly, is not life but the power to remember and to speak, which is why he has to sit there rationing it with a sword, and why his own mother sits by the pit in silence and cannot look at her son. — *Third:* this is not hell. As promised in Book Four, note 5: there is no judgement at the door, no sorting, no reward for the good and no fire for the wicked. Everybody goes to the same grey place, Achilles and Agamemnon and Elpenor who fell off a roof, all together. Minos is down there judging, but look at what he is doing — settling disputes *among* the dead, the way a king settles disputes among the living. He is a magistrate, not St Peter.

[^2]: **Oedipus, three hundred years before Sophocles.** Here is the whole story as this poem has it: he killed his father and married his mother in ignorance; the gods made it known at once; his mother hanged herself; and *he went on ruling in Thebes*. He is not blinded. He does not put out his own eyes; there is no exile, no wandering, no daughter leading him by the hand to Colonus. He suffers, and he keeps the throne. Set that beside what happens to the material later, when the Athenian tragedians take hold of it and make the discovery and the self-blinding the whole point, and you have the same lesson as Book Three, note 4, on Orestes. These stories were not fixed. The versions we treat as *the* myth are almost always somebody's later, harder-edged rewrite. Homer knew a different one and told it in nine lines, on the way past.

[^3]: **What the three punishments have in common.** Tityos assaulted Leto. Tantalus and Sisyphus are here for offences the poem does not spell out, but the tradition is consistent that both personally cheated or insulted the gods — Tantalus by abusing their hospitality, Sisyphus by trying to outwit death itself. Every one of the three is being punished for an offence *against a god*, personally, and not for wickedness in general. This is the Elysium note from Book Four arriving from the other side, and worth stating plainly because it cuts against everything later readers brought with them: there is no ordinary moral accounting in this afterlife. Bad men who did not offend the gods are not punished, and good men who did not please them are not rewarded. There are three individuals in agony, and everybody else is simply dead, in the grey, forever. When Achilles says what he says thirty lines later, this is the place he is talking about.

[^4]: **Heracles in two places at once.** He is standing in front of Odysseus with an arrow on the string — and the poem stops to tell you that this is only his phantom, and that Heracles himself is up on Olympus at the feast, married to the goddess of youth. Both statements are in the text, one line apart. This is the plainest theological contradiction in Homer and there is no reconciling it: either a man's shade goes to the grey place or he becomes a god, and Heracles is doing both. Most scholars think the Olympus line is a later addition, patched in by somebody who could not bear to have the greatest hero of them all shuffling about in the dark with everybody else. I mention it because it is a useful reminder of what this text is. It is not one man's manuscript. It is a tradition that was sung and re-sung and repaired for centuries before anyone wrote it down, and occasionally you can see the join.
