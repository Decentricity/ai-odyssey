# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK FIFTEEN
### In Which a Young Man Comes Home by the Back Way, and an Old Slave Remembers Being a Prince

---

Athena went to Sparta, to remind the son of Odysseus about going home and to hurry him along.

She found him and Peisistratus lying in the porch of the house of Menelaus. Nestor's son was fast asleep, but no sweet sleep held Telemachus; he was awake all through the immortal night, worrying about his father. And the goddess stood over him.

"Telemachus, it is not right to go on wandering far from home, leaving your property behind with men as insolent as that in your house. Take care they do not divide it all up and eat it, and your journey turn out to have been for nothing.

"Rouse Menelaus and get him to send you off, so that you may still find your excellent mother at home. Her father and her brothers are already urging her to marry Eurymachus, who is outbidding all the other suitors in presents and keeps raising his offer. Take care she does not carry off something from your house against your will.

"You know how it is with a woman's heart: she wants to build up the house of the man who marries her, and of her earlier children and the husband who is dead she takes no more thought and asks no questions. So go home and hand everything over to whichever of the servant women seems best to you, until the gods show you a wife of your own.

"And there is another thing I want to tell you. The best of the suitors are lying in wait for you in the strait between Ithaca and rugged Samos, meaning to kill you before you can reach home. I do not think it will happen; sooner than that the earth will cover a few of the men who are eating up your living.

"Keep your ship well away from the islands and sail through the night. Whichever god is watching over you will send a wind behind you. And when you reach the nearest headland of Ithaca, send the ship and the crew on to the town, and go yourself first of all to the swineherd, who keeps your pigs and is loyal to you. Sleep there, and send him into the town to tell sensible Penelope that you are safe and have come back from Pylos."

And with that she went away to Olympus.

Telemachus woke Nestor's son with a touch of his heel. "Get up, Peisistratus. Bring out the horses and put them under the yoke, and let us be on our way."

"Telemachus, however much we want to go, there is no driving through a dark night. It will be dawn soon enough. Wait until the hero Menelaus brings the gifts out and puts them in the chariot and sends us off with a kind word. A guest remembers, all his days, the host who showed him friendship."

---

I shall not detain you over Athena's opinion of women, which is brisk, cynical and tactically shaped for a young man she wants on a ship by morning. It is worth noticing only because the poem puts it in the mouth of a goddess who knows perfectly well that it is not true of Penelope, and who has just finished telling Odysseus how faithfully his wife has held the house. She is not describing anybody. She is getting a boy to move.

---

When Dawn came up, Menelaus got out of bed and came to them; and Telemachus dressed quickly and asked to be sent home.

"Menelaus, send me back now to my own country. My heart is set on getting home."

"Telemachus, I shall not keep you here long when you want to be off. I disapprove of a host who is excessive in his kindness quite as much as one who is excessive the other way. Moderation is better in everything. It is as bad to press a guest to stay when he does not want to as it is to hold one back who is eager to go. One should look after a guest properly while he is there, and let him go when he wishes.

"But wait until I bring the gifts out and set them in the chariot, and until I tell the women to get a meal ready in the hall, out of the plenty we have. It is honour and glory and profit together to have dinner before setting out over a very wide stretch of country."

That is a small speech and I like it very much. Everything in this poem turns on the treatment of guests, and here is a man who has been thinking about the mechanics of it — who has worked out that the fault of holding somebody too long and the fault of pushing them out of the door are the same fault, and that the whole art of it is in reading what the other person actually wants. It is worth setting beside the household we visited at the end of the last book, where a slave with one spare cloak and no property at all arrived at precisely the same conclusion by himself.

Menelaus went down to the treasure chamber with Helen and his son Megapenthes, and chose a two-handled cup, and had the boy carry a silver mixing bowl. And Helen went to the chests where her embroidered robes lay, that she had made herself, and lifted out the largest and the most beautifully worked, which lay underneath all the others and shone like a star.

"Take this, dear child. Here is a gift from me, a keepsake from the hands of Menelaus, for you to use when you pour to the gods. Hephaestus made it, and Phaedimus the king of the Sidonians gave it to me when his house sheltered me on my way home."

And Helen put the robe into his hands. "I have a present for you too, dear child, a memory of Helen's hands — for your bride to wear on the day of your marriage, which you are longing for. Until then let it lie in your mother's keeping. And may you come with joy to your well-built house and your own country."

They poured the last of the wine and got into the chariot and drove out through the gateway. And as they went, Telemachus prayed to Athena; and while he was praying an eagle came over on the right with a great white goose in its talons, a tame bird out of the yard, and the men and women came running and shouting after it. And the eagle swung in close and darted away to the right, in front of the horses. And they were glad to see it, and their hearts warmed.

Peisistratus spoke first. "Consider, Menelaus, whether the god has shown this sign for the two of us, or for you."

And Menelaus began to think it over, so as to answer properly — and Helen got in before him.

"Listen to me. I shall prophesy, as the immortals put it in my heart and as I believe it will come out. That eagle came from the mountain where he was born and where his brood is, and took a goose that had been reared in the house. Just so will Odysseus come home after many hardships and much wandering, and take his revenge. Or he may be at home already, sowing trouble for every one of the suitors."

"May Zeus grant it," said Telemachus. "Then I would pray to you as I would to a god."

---

That is the last thing Helen says in the poem, and she is the only person in it who reads an omen without being asked and turns out to be exactly right.

She was extremely good at this in Book Four as well, when she looked at a boy who had just arrived and named his father before anybody else in the room had thought to. Her husband is still working out what to say — the poem is quite explicit that he is composing an answer — and she interrupts him and gets it right, and the reading she gives is one nobody else in that courtyard could have given, because it takes an eagle carrying off a domestic bird from a house full of shouting people and sees the point of view of the household. She has been the goose. She knows what it looks like from underneath.

---

They came to Pherae and slept at the house of Diocles, and drove on next morning to Pylos. And there Telemachus said: "Peisistratus, will you promise me something and do it? We are guest-friends from our fathers' time, and we are of an age, and this journey will make us closer still. Do not take me past my ship, but leave me there — or the old man will keep me in his house out of pure kindness, and I need to get home."

Nestor's son turned it over and decided how to do it properly, and swung the horses down to the shore, and unloaded the gifts into the stern of the ship, and told him to hurry aboard before the old man heard of it and came down to fetch him.

And as the crew were making ready, a stranger came up — a man from a long line of prophets, who had killed a man of his own tribe in Argos and was on the run with the dead man's brothers hunting him. His name was Theoclymenus, and he asked whose ship it was and begged to be taken aboard.

"Stranger, I shall not refuse you. Come with us, and you shall have what we have."[^1]

They took his bronze spear and laid it along the deck, and he came aboard and sat down beside Telemachus; and they cast off, and Athena sent them a fair wind that came on strong out of a clear sky, so that the ship might run through the salt water as fast as possible.

---

Meanwhile Odysseus and the swineherd were having their supper in the hut, and the other men with them; and when they had eaten enough, Odysseus tried him again, to see whether he would still be kind and tell him to stay on at the farm, or send him off to the city.

"Listen, Eumaeus, and all of you. In the morning I want to go into the town and beg, so as not to wear you and your men out here. Just give me your advice and a good guide to take me there; and once I am in the city I shall have to wander about on my own and see whether anybody will give me a cup of water and a piece of bread. I could go to the house of Odysseus and take my news to sensible Penelope, and mix with the arrogant suitors — they have plenty; they might give me a dinner. I would do whatever they wanted, and do it well. Because Hermes, who gives grace and fame to every kind of work, has given me a gift for service: nobody could match me at splitting kindling, or getting a dry fire going, or carving and roasting meat, or pouring the wine — all the things that lesser men do for their betters."

And the swineherd was extremely upset.

"Good heavens, stranger — how did a notion like that get into your head? You must be very keen to die on the spot, if you really want to go into that crowd of suitors, whose insolence and violence reach the iron sky.

"Their servants are nothing like you. They are young men in fine cloaks and tunics, with their hair oiled and their faces handsome, and the polished tables in there are loaded with bread and meat and wine.

"Stay here. Nobody finds you a nuisance — not I, and not one of my men. And when the dear son of Odysseus comes, he will give you a cloak and a tunic and send you wherever you want to go."

"Eumaeus, may you be as dear to father Zeus as you are to me, for stopping my wandering and my miserable homelessness. There is nothing worse for a man than a life on the road; but it is for the sake of the cursed belly that men put up with such misery.

"But since you are keeping me here and telling me to wait for him — tell me about the mother of Odysseus, and the father he left behind on the threshold of old age. Are they still alive and looking at the light of the sun, or are they dead and gone into the house of Hades?"

"Laertes is alive. But he prays to Zeus constantly that the life may waste out of his body in his own halls, because he grieves terribly for his son who is gone, and for the wife he married, and it was her death that hurt him most and brought him to an early old age.

"She died of grief for her famous son, a pitiful death — and may nobody die that way who lives here and is a friend of mine.

"While she was alive, sorry as she was, it was always a pleasure to me to ask after her and to talk to her, because she brought me up herself along with her own daughter Ctimene, the youngest of her children. I was brought up with her, and her mother honoured me only a little less. And when the two of us reached the age everybody wants to reach, they married her to a man in Same and got a great many presents for her; and me the lady dressed in a cloak and a tunic, very fine clothes, and gave me sandals for my feet, and sent me out to the farm. And she loved me more in her heart than ever.

"Now I go without all that. But the blessed gods make the work I stay at prosper, and out of it I have eaten and drunk and given to people who deserved it. From my mistress there is nothing pleasant to be had these days, not a word and not a kindness, since that trouble fell on the house — overbearing men. And servants very much need to talk to their mistress and ask her things, and eat and drink there, and carry something back to the fields with them. Those are the things that warm a servant's heart."

---

"Then you must have been a small child, Eumaeus, when you were brought so far from your own country and your parents. Tell me — was the city sacked where your father and your lady mother lived? Or were you left alone with the sheep or the cattle, and enemies took you in their ships and sold you into this man's house, and he paid a good price for you?"

"Stranger, since you ask me that, listen quietly and enjoy yourself and sit and drink your wine. These nights are enormous. There is time to sleep and time to enjoy listening, and you need not lie down before you have to; too much sleep is wearisome. Let the rest of these go outside and sleep, and at first light have their breakfast and go out with the master's pigs. But the two of us will stay in the hut and drink and eat and take a certain pleasure in going over each other's miseries — because afterwards a man does find pleasure even in his troubles, when he has suffered a great deal and wandered a great deal.

"There is an island called Syrie, if you have ever heard of it, above Ortygia, where the sun turns. It is not so very full of people, but it is a good country, rich in cattle and rich in sheep, full of vines and full of wheat. Famine never comes to those people, and no other hateful sickness comes upon them; but when the generations grow old in the town, Apollo comes with Artemis and kills them with his gentle arrows.

"There are two cities there, and everything is divided between them; and my father was king over both — Ctesius, the son of Ormenus, a man like the immortals.

"And Phoenicians came there, men famous for their ships, greedy rogues, with a great many trinkets in their black ship. Now there was a Phoenician woman in my father's house, tall and beautiful and skilled at fine handwork, and the crafty Phoenicians talked her round. One of them got her while she was washing clothes, lying with her beside the hollow ship; and afterwards he asked her who she was and where she came from, and she pointed at once to my father's high-roofed house.

"*I come from Sidon, rich in bronze, and I am the daughter of Arybas, who was very wealthy. Taphian pirates carried me off as I was coming in from the fields, and brought me here and sold me into this man's house, and he paid a good price for me.*

"And the man said: *Would you like to come home with us, then, and see the high-roofed house of your father and mother, and the two of them? Because they are still alive, and people say they are rich.*

"And the woman said: *That might be done, if you sailors would swear me an oath to bring me home unharmed.*

"So they all swore it. And when they had sworn and finished the oath, the woman said: *Now be quiet. Let none of your crew speak to me if he meets me in the street or at the well, in case somebody goes to the house and tells the old man, and he suspects and ties me up in cruel bonds and plans destruction for the lot of you. Keep it to yourselves, and get your cargo bought as fast as you can. And when the ship is loaded, send word to me quickly up at the house, because I shall bring gold as well, whatever comes to my hand. And I will tell you something else I would gladly give as my passage money. There is a child of my master's in the house that I nurse, a knowing little thing, who runs out of doors along with me. I would bring him aboard, and he would fetch you an enormous price wherever you sold him among people of another language.*

"And with that she went back up to the house.

"And they stayed there a whole year and got a great deal of goods into their hollow ship. And when the ship was loaded and it was time to go, they sent a man up to give the woman the word. He came to my father's house, a crafty fellow, with a gold chain strung with amber beads; and the maids in the hall and my lady mother handled it and looked at it and offered him a price — and he nodded to the woman without a word, and then went back to the hollow ship.

"And she took me by the hand and led me out of the house. In the front hall she found the cups and the tables of the men who had been dining with my father and had just gone out to the assembly, and she hid three goblets in the fold of her dress and carried them off; and I went along after her, in complete innocence.

"The sun went down and all the ways grew dark, and we came quickly to the harbour, where the Phoenicians' fast ship was. They put us aboard and sailed, and Zeus sent a following wind. Six days we sailed, night and day alike; and when Zeus brought on the seventh, Artemis of the arrows struck the woman, and she dropped into the bilge like a sea-bird going down.

"And they threw her overboard, to be a meal for the seals and the fishes; and I was left there with my heart aching. And the wind and the water carried them to Ithaca, and there Laertes bought me with his own property.

"And that is how I came to see this country with my eyes."

So the two of them talked, and slept only a little, because Dawn came up quickly on her golden throne.

---

I do not think I need to say very much about that, but I will say two things.

The first is that this man is a king's son. He was born a prince on an island with two cities in it, and his father ruled both, and he was stolen at about five years old by a woman who had herself been stolen, and sold, and was trying to get home — and who used him as her fare. She died six days out and was thrown into the sea, and the child went on to Ithaca and was bought by an old man who was kind to him. He has kept pigs for that family for fifty years and calls his absent owner *honoured sir* and will not say his name.

The second is what he does with the story. Look at how he begins it: *these nights are enormous; there is time to sleep and time to enjoy listening.* He settles his guest with a drink, sends the other men off to bed so as not to bore them, and observes, quite deliberately, that going over old griefs together is a pleasure once the griefs are old enough. He is not complaining and he is not asking for sympathy. He is doing the thing a good host does on a long night, which is to offer a story in return for a story — and the man he is offering it to has just told him a magnificent pack of lies and is sitting there in disguise in his own farmhouse listening to the true one.[^2]

---

And meanwhile Telemachus and his crew were coming in to the shore. They furled the sails and unstepped the mast and rowed her in to the anchorage and moored her, and got out and had their meal on the beach.

"Take the ship on to the town now," said Telemachus. "I am going out to the fields and the herdsmen. In the evening, when I have looked over my land, I shall come down to the town; and in the morning I shall give you a good dinner of meat and sweet wine as your wages for the journey."

And Theoclymenus said: "And where am I to go, dear child? Whose house shall I make for, among all the men who rule in rugged Ithaca? Or shall I go straight to your mother's house, and yours?"

"At any other time I would tell you to go to my own house; there is no lack of hospitality there. But it would be the worse for you now, because I shall be away and my mother will not see you. She does not appear often before the suitors in the hall; she keeps apart from them, upstairs, at her loom. But I can name you another man to go to: Eurymachus, the splendid son of Polybus, whom the people of Ithaca look at nowadays as though he were a god. He is much the best of them, and the keenest to marry my mother and have the honour that was Odysseus's.

"But Zeus, who lives in the sky, knows whether he will not bring the day of doom down on all of them before any wedding."

And as he said it a bird flew over on the right: a hawk, the swift messenger of Apollo, with a dove in his talons, plucking her; and the feathers came drifting down to the ground between Telemachus and the ship.

And Theoclymenus called him away from his men and took him by the hand.

"Telemachus, that bird did not fly on your right without a god behind it. I knew it for an omen the moment I looked at it. There is no family in Ithaca more kingly than yours. You will have the power here, always."

"Stranger, I only wish that word may be fulfilled. Then you would very soon know my friendship, and get so many gifts from me that anybody meeting you would call you a lucky man."

And he turned to Peiraeus, whom he trusted more than any of his crew, and asked him to take the stranger home and look after him and honour him until he came himself; and Peiraeus said he would, and would keep him as long as he stayed, and there would be no want of hospitality.

Then he went aboard and told the crew to embark and cast off the cables, and they went aboard and sat down at the oars.

And Telemachus bound his good sandals under his feet and picked up his heavy spear with the sharp bronze point off the deck of the ship. And they cast off the cables, and pushed her out, and sailed for the town, as the son of Odysseus had told them.

And his feet carried him on, fast, until he came to the yard where his pigs were, thousands of them, and where the good swineherd slept beside them, always loyal to his masters.[^3]

## NOTES TO BOOK FIFTEEN

[^1]: **Theoclymenus, and the long pedigree.** The poem introduces this man by giving five generations of his family before it tells you what he wants, which is a passage most readers find heavy going, and it is worth knowing why it is there. He is a seer, from the most famous seer-family in Greek legend — the line of Melampus, who understood the speech of birds, and of Amphiaraus, who went to the war at Thebes knowing it would kill him because his wife had been bribed with a necklace to send him. To name that ancestry was to establish, in the only way available, that this stranger's prophecies are worth listening to. He is also a murderer on the run, taken aboard without hesitation and without any questions asked about the killing — which tells you something about how blood-guilt worked. A man who had killed within his own tribe had to leave, immediately and permanently, and the family of the dead man would pursue him; but exile discharged the obligation, and elsewhere he was simply a stranger, and strangers come from Zeus.

[^2]: **What Eumaeus was, and what he is.** He is the son of a king, stolen as a small child and sold; and it is worth being plain that his story is not offered as an outrage. It is offered as a piece of ordinary bad luck of the kind that happened constantly on that sea. What the poem does with it is quieter and stranger: it gives the finest manners in the book to a slave, and lets him tell a prince's biography in a hut, at night, to a beggar who is really a king. Everybody in that room is in disguise, one way or another. — Two details reward a second look. The woman who steals him has herself been stolen — taken by pirates from her own country, sold, and now trying to buy her way home with somebody else's child — and the poem gives her a name, a father and a city, which it did not have to do. And Eumaeus, who has been a slave since he was five, has himself bought a slave, Mesaulius, with his own savings, as he mentioned in passing at the end of the last book. He is not outside the system. Nobody in this poem is outside it.

[^3]: **Two birds, one book.** The poem frames this book with omens and it is doing it deliberately. At the start, an eagle takes a tame goose out of the yard of a great house, and Helen reads it: the wild thing comes down from the mountain and carries off the creature that has been fattening at home. At the end, a hawk strips a dove in flight and the feathers fall between the young man and his ship, and the seer reads that one. Both mean the same thing and neither of them needs to be spelled out to the reader, which is why the poem does not spell it out. Notice, though, who is allowed to interpret. A woman at a gate and a fugitive murderer picked up at a beach — the two people in this book with the least standing of anybody in it — are the two who can see what is coming. The kings say nothing.
