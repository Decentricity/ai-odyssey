# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK EIGHT
### In Which a Blind Man Sings, a Young Man Is Rude About Sport, and a Stranger Cries Into His Cloak

---

When Dawn came up, rosy-fingered, Alcinous got out of bed, and so did Odysseus, the sacker of cities; and the king led the way down to the Phaeacian meeting-ground, which is laid out beside the ships.

Athena went through the town in the likeness of the king's own herald, stopping beside one man after another. "Come along, leaders and counsellors of the Phaeacians — come to the assembly and hear about the stranger who has arrived at the house of Alcinous. He has been driven over the sea, and he looks like one of the immortals."

And she poured a supernatural grace over Odysseus's head and shoulders and made him taller to look at, and broader, because she wanted the Phaeacians to find him formidable and worth respecting — and because he was going to have to get through a great many tests before the day was out. It looks like a party, this day. It is an examination.

The seats filled up, and the crowd stared at him, and Alcinous stood and spoke.

"A stranger — I do not know who — has come to my house, wandering, out of the east or out of the west, and he is asking for an escort. Let us get one ready, the way we always do; nobody who comes to my house sits about here for long, grieving over his send-off.

"So drag a black ship down to the bright sea, a new one on her first voyage, and pick out fifty-two young men from the whole country, the best there are. Lash the oars in place and then come up to my house and get a meal inside you. That is my instruction to the young men. As for the rest of you — the sceptred kings — come to my house, so that we can entertain our guest properly indoors. Nobody refuse.

"And send for Demodocus, the singer whom heaven has favoured. The god gave him song beyond anybody else, to delight us however his heart moves him to sing."

So the ship was hauled down and the mast stepped and the oars fitted into their leather loops, and they moored her out in deep water and went up to the great house. The colonnades and the courtyards and the halls filled with men, young and old, and there were a great many of them; and Alcinous killed twelve sheep for them, and eight white-tusked boars, and two shambling oxen, and they dressed them and got a very fine dinner ready.

And the herald came bringing the singer, whom the Muse loved above other men, and to whom she had given good and evil together: she took his eyes away, and she gave him sweet song.[^1]

He was given a silver-studded chair in the middle of the diners, backed against a tall pillar; and the herald hung the clear-toned lyre on a peg above his head and showed him how to reach up and take it down, and put a basket beside him and a good table and a cup of wine to drink when he wanted it.

And when they had all eaten and drunk as much as they wanted, the Muse stirred the singer to sing about the famous deeds of men — out of a song whose fame had gone right up into the wide sky in those days.

He sang about the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles: how the two of them had once fallen out at a rich feast of the gods, with terrible words, and how Agamemnon had been secretly delighted to see the best of the Greeks quarrelling, because Apollo (god of prophecy, and of the bow) had told him at Delphi that this would be the sign. And it was the beginning of the disaster that rolled over the Trojans and the Greeks alike, by the will of Zeus.

And Odysseus took the great purple cloak in his strong hands and drew it down over his head and hid his handsome face, because he was ashamed to be seen crying by the Phaeacians. Every time the singer stopped he would push the cloak back and wipe the tears away and take his two-handled cup and pour an offering to the gods; and every time the singer started again — because the Phaeacian nobles kept urging him on, they were enjoying the story so much — he would cover his head and cry.

He got away with it. Nobody noticed except Alcinous, who was sitting next to him and could hear him breathing.

Consider what has just happened, because as far as anybody can tell it had never happened in a story before. A man is sitting in disguise at a foreign dinner table listening to a professional performer sing a well-known number about the Trojan war, and he is in it — not as a veteran in the audience but as one of the two men the song is about; and the crowd, who have heard it before and like it, are calling for more. That is what undying fame turns into. It becomes something a stranger performs at parties, in a town you have never visited, to people who will applaud and then ask for the one about the horse; and the man it belongs to sits under his cloak and waits for it to stop so that he can wipe his face and pour his wine like everybody else.

---

Alcinous, who was quick, said that they had all had enough of dinner and the lyre and had better go out to the games — so that the stranger could tell his friends at home how far the Phaeacians outdid everybody at boxing and wrestling and jumping and running.

Out they went, with a great crowd behind them, and a number of fine young men stood up to compete. Their names, and I give them because they are worth having, were Acroneus and Ocyalus and Elatreus, Nauteus and Prymneus and Anchialus, Eretmeus and Ponteus and Proreus, Thoon and Anabesineus and Amphialus; and Euryalus, who was as good as murderous Ares to look at, the best-built of all the Phaeacians after the faultless Laodamas; and the three sons of Alcinous, Laodamas and Halius and Clytoneus.[^2]

Clytoneus won the footrace by a distance. Euryalus won the wrestling, Amphialus the jump, Elatreus the discus, and Laodamas the boxing.

And when they had enjoyed the games, Laodamas said: "Friends, let us ask the stranger whether he knows any sport. He is not badly built — look at his thighs and his calves, and those arms, and the thick neck on him. There is a great deal of strength there, and he is not past it either; he has just been broken up by hard usage. There is nothing on earth like the sea for finishing a man off, however strong he is."

So he went and stood in the middle and said, politely: "Come and try, sir, if you know any games. You look as if you would. There is no greater glory for a man, as long as he lives, than what he can do with his hands and his feet. Come and have a try, and shake the cares off your heart. You will not be kept waiting for your journey much longer; the ship is launched and the crew are ready."

"Laodamas, why are you all pressing me and making fun of me? I have more grief in my mind than games. I have been through a great deal and worked very hard, and I am sitting here in your assembly wanting to go home, and asking your king and your people for it."

And Euryalus answered him to his face, and insulted him.

"No. I did not think you looked like a man who knows about contests — not the sort men hold everywhere. You look more like a man who goes up and down in a ship with a lot of oars, in charge of a crew of traders, keeping an eye on his cargo and watching his freight charges and his greedy profits. You do not look like an athlete."

That is not an insult about sport, and I hope you heard what it actually was. He is saying: you are in business; you are the sort of man who counts things. To a young nobleman of this world there was no deeper cut available, since the whole value system he was raised in held that a gentleman shows his quality with his body, in public, for honour — and that a man who works for gain is a lesser article. It also happens to be aimed at somebody who has spent ten years calculating, weighing, counting his men and getting what he could out of every place he landed in, and who is at this moment working a room in order to extract a passage home. Which is very likely why it lands.

And Odysseus looked at him from under his brows.

"That was ugly, sir, and you talk like a reckless man. The gods do not give every good thing to every man — not beauty and brains and eloquence together. There is a man whose looks are nothing much, and the god crowns his words with a shape, and people watch him with pleasure; he speaks steadily on, with a winning modesty, and he stands out in any gathering, and when he walks through the town men look at him as if he were a god. And there is another man who is as handsome as the immortals, and there is no grace in anything he says.

"You, for instance. You are outstanding to look at; a god could not improve you. And your mind is empty.

"You have stirred me up. I am not a beginner at games, whatever you say; I was among the best of them while I could still trust my youth and my hands. Now I am held down by hardship and pain, because I have got through a good deal of war and a good deal of heavy water. Even so — I will try. Your words bit me, and you have provoked me."

And he got up, still in his cloak, and picked up a discus: a big one, bigger and thicker and a great deal heavier than the ones the Phaeacians had been throwing at each other. He swung it and let it go, and the stone hummed as it went; and the Phaeacians, the long-oared men, the famous sailors, ducked at the noise of it going over.

It went past every one of their marks. It flew light out of his hand.

And Athena, in the shape of a man, set the marker down where it fell and called out: "Even a blind man could find your mark, stranger, feeling for it — because it is not mixed in with the crowd of them at all. It is out in front, well out. Take heart about this event. There is not a Phaeacian here who will reach it, let alone pass it."

And Odysseus was delighted, because there was somebody in that crowd who was on his side; and being thoroughly wound up by now, he challenged the lot of them.

"Reach that, young men. And when you have, I shall throw another one as far or further. Anybody else who feels moved to it — since you have made me angry — come out. Boxing, wrestling, running, I do not care. Any Phaeacian you like, except Laodamas, because he is my host, and only a lunatic quarrels with the man who is feeding him.

"I am not bad with any of the usual weapons. I know how to handle a polished bow. I would be first to hit my man in a crowd of enemies, even with a lot of my own friends standing beside me shooting at the same target. There was only one man at Troy who beat me with a bow, and that was Philoctetes. Beyond him I say I am far the best of everyone alive now who eats bread on this earth — though I would not set myself against the men of former times, not against Heracles, and not against Eurytus of Oechalia, who used to shoot against the immortals themselves, which is why Eurytus did not live to be old; Apollo was angry with him and killed him, because he had challenged him to a shooting match.

"And I can throw a spear further than another man can shoot an arrow. It is only in the footrace that I am afraid one of you might beat me. I have been badly knocked about in a great deal of water, with no steady food on board, and my legs have gone."

Everybody went very quiet. And of all the things this man might have boasted about — he is an eloquent liar with twenty years of material — he chose the bow, which nobody had asked about and on which he was not going to be tested. It is the first time in eight books that a bow has been mentioned. I should keep it, if I were you.

Alcinous stepped in and smoothed the whole thing over, and did it beautifully.

"Stranger, nothing you have said is ungracious. You wanted to show what is in you, because you were angry, and because this man stood up in front of everybody and insulted you. No one who knew how to talk sense would find fault with your quality.

"But listen now, so that you can tell your own heroes about it later, when you are at dinner in your own house with your wife and children and you happen to think of us. We are not perfect boxers, and we are not wrestlers. But we run fast, and we are the best seamen there are. And we love our dinner, and the lyre, and dancing, and clean clothes, and hot baths, and bed.

"Come on then — the best dancers among the Phaeacians. Dance. So that the stranger can tell everyone at home how far we are ahead of the rest of the world at sailing and running and dancing and song. And somebody fetch Demodocus his clear-toned lyre."

He does not scold Euryalus in public, you notice; he accepts the visitor's anger as reasonable, changes the subject, and changes it to the thing his people are honestly best at — telling the truth about what they are not good at while he is about it. There is no shame in him at all.

---

So they cleared a dancing floor and widened the ring, and the boys who were best at it, in the first bloom of youth, struck up and beat out the holy dance with their feet; and Odysseus stood watching the twinkle of the feet, and was amazed.

And the singer struck the lyre and began a song, and it was a rude one.

He sang about Ares (god of war) and Aphrodite (goddess of love): how they first went to bed together in the house of Hephaestus (the lame smith of the gods, and her husband), secretly, and how Ares gave her a great many presents and disgraced the marriage bed.

And the Sun, who sees everything, saw them, and went and told him.

And when Hephaestus heard the story that goes to the heart, he went off to his forge with murder in his mind and put the great anvil on the block, and hammered out chains that could not be broken and could not be got out of, so that the two of them would stay exactly where they were put. And when the trap was made he went to the bedroom where his own bed stood and threw the chains all round the bedposts in a circle, and hung a great many more of them down from the roof beam, fine as spider silk, so fine that not one of the blessed gods could see them. It was very cunning work. And when he had got the whole thing round the bed he pretended to leave for Lemnos, which was his favourite place in the world.

Ares was not keeping a blind watch. He saw the great craftsman go, and came to the house wanting Aphrodite, and found her just back from her father's, and took her hand. "Come to bed, love. Hephaestus is not at home; he has gone off to Lemnos."

And she thought it was a very good idea.

So the two of them went to bed and lay down — and the chains came down round them, and they could not move a limb or lift anything, and then they understood that there was no getting out of it.

And back came the great lame god, having turned round before he ever reached Lemnos, because the Sun had been keeping watch for him. He stood in the doorway of his own house with a savage rage on him and gave a dreadful shout, and called out to all the gods:

"Father Zeus, and the rest of you blessed immortals — come here and look at this. It is a joke, and it is not to be borne. Because I am lame, Aphrodite is always insulting me, and she loves destructive Ares because he is handsome and sound in the legs, and I was born crooked. And nobody is to blame for that but my two parents, who should have thought twice.

"Come and see where those two are sleeping — in my bed — and how it hurts me to watch it. I do not think they will want to lie like that much longer, however much they are in love. But my trap and my chain will hold them until her father gives me back every one of the courtship gifts I paid over for that shameless girl. His daughter is beautiful, and she has no self-control."

And the gods came crowding to the house with the bronze floor. Poseidon (god of the sea) came, and Hermes the runner came, and lord Apollo who works from far off came. The goddesses stayed at home, every one of them, out of modesty.

And the gods stood in the doorway, and unquenchable laughter went up among the blessed gods when they saw the cleverness of it. And one would look at the man next to him and say: "Bad deeds do not prosper. The slow one catches the quick one. Look at Hephaestus — slow as he is, and lame — catching Ares, who is the fastest of all the gods on Olympus. And now he owes the fine for adultery."

And Apollo said to Hermes: "Would you be willing to sleep in bed beside golden Aphrodite, even held down by those heavy chains?"

And Hermes said: "I only wish it, lord Apollo. Let there be three times as many chains, and no way out of them, and let all of you gods look on, and all the goddesses too — I would still sleep beside golden Aphrodite."

And the immortals roared with laughter.

Only Poseidon did not laugh. He kept asking Hephaestus to let Ares go, and in the end offered to stand surety and pay the fine himself if Ares defaulted; and Hephaestus said, well, if Ares runs off owing me, I shall be chaining *you* up instead — and released them. And the moment the chains were off the two of them jumped up and went: Ares straight to Thrace, and Aphrodite to Cyprus, to Paphos, where she has her sanctuary and her fragrant altar; and there the Graces bathed her and rubbed her with the immortal oil that shines on the gods who live forever, and dressed her in beautiful clothes, a wonder to look at.[^3]

That was the singer's song, and Odysseus enjoyed it as much as anybody.

I hope you enjoyed it too, and I hope you also noticed what the Phaeacians did not, which is that they have just been told the plot. A wife; a husband who finds out; a lie about where he is going; a trap built in secret out of the one thing he is better at than anybody alive; and everybody caught in it at once, in the hall, with the whole household looking on. The only reason it is a comedy is that nobody in it can die. Take the immortality away and it is Agamemnon at his own homecoming dinner with twenty men behind the door — the same story exactly, and human beings stay dead. Which is why this poem can put its funniest scene in the middle of its saddest evening and not think itself frivolous.

---

Then Halius and Laodamas danced on their own, because nobody could match those two; and they took a beautiful purple ball, and one would lean back and throw it up towards the shadowy clouds and the other would spring off the ground and catch it easily before his feet came down. And when they had finished with throwing it straight up they began passing it back and forth, dancing all the while, and the other young men stood round the ring beating time, and a great noise of it went up.

"My lord," said Odysseus, "you said your dancers were the best. There it is, exactly as you said. I am astonished, looking at it."

Alcinous was very pleased, and turned to the Phaeacians. "The stranger seems to me a man of excellent sense. Let us give him a proper guest-gift. Twelve kings rule in this country as leaders, and I am the thirteenth. Let every one of us bring him a clean cloak and a tunic and a talent of gold, and let us bring it all together now, so that the stranger can go in to dinner with the gifts in his hands and a glad heart. And Euryalus is to make it up with him, in words and with a present, because what he said was not decent."

They all agreed, and Euryalus did it, and did it well. He gave him a bronze sword with a silver hilt and a scabbard of new-sawn ivory round it, worth a great deal. "Sir, and father — if anything was said here that was out of order, let the storm winds pick it up and carry it off. And may the gods let you see your wife, and get you home to your own country; you have been away from your friends far too long, and suffering."

"And to you, my friend — good luck, and may the gods give you a good life, and may you never miss this sword you have just given me, having made it right with words." And he slung it over his shoulder.

The sun went down, and the splendid gifts were carried up to the house, and Alcinous told Arete to bring out the best chest there was and put a fresh cloak and tunic in it herself; and he added a beautiful gold cup of his own, so that the stranger would remember him every time he poured a drink for the gods at home. And Arete told her women to set the great cauldron on the fire and heat a bath; and she packed the chest with the clothes and the gold, and said:

"Look at the lid yourself now, and tie it up quickly with a knot of your own — so that nobody robs you on the voyage, while you are lying asleep in the black ship."

And Odysseus heard her, and fitted the lid down at once, and put a cunning knot on it that lady Circe had taught him, long ago.

You have not met her yet. He does not explain. He simply ties the knot, and the poem tells you where he learned it and moves on — and there is a name sitting in the middle of that sentence which means nothing to anybody in the room and everything four books from now.

Then the housekeeper told him to get into the bath, and he was glad to see the warm water, because he had not been looked after like that since he left Calypso's house.

---

And when the women had bathed him and rubbed him with oil and put a fine cloak and tunic on him, he came out and went towards the men who were drinking their wine.

And Nausicaa was standing by the doorpost of the strong-built hall.

She looked at him, and she was amazed, and she said: "Goodbye, stranger. When you are back in your own country, remember me sometimes — because it is to me, first of anybody, that you owe your life."

"Nausicaa, daughter of great-hearted Alcinous — if Zeus grants that I get home and see my day of homecoming, then there, every day of my life, I will pray to you as to a god. Because you gave me my life, girl."

And he went and sat down beside the king.

That is the last we see of her. She does not weep, and she does not ask him to stay, and she asks for the smallest thing she could possibly have asked for, on the strongest grounds there are — which is that he would be dead in the reeds without her.

---

They were carving the meat and mixing the wine, and the herald came up leading Demodocus and set him down among them, propped against a tall pillar. And Odysseus cut a piece off the chine of a white-tusked boar, a rich piece with plenty of fat along both sides, and there was more left on it than he kept for himself; and he called the herald over.

"Give this to Demodocus, so that he can eat it; I want to say something civil to him, sorry as I am. Singers get honour and respect from every man on earth, because the Muse has taught them the paths of song and she loves the whole tribe of them."

And the herald carried it over and put it in the hands of the great man, and he took it and was glad. And when they had eaten, Odysseus said to him:

"Demodocus, I praise you above every mortal man. Either the Muse taught you or Apollo did, because you sing the fate of the Greeks so very rightly — everything they did and suffered and worked through — as if you had been there yourself, or heard it from a man who was.

"So change to another part of it now, and sing about the making of the wooden horse: the one Epeius built with Athena's help, and which Odysseus got up into the citadel by a trick, filled with the men who sacked Troy. If you can tell me that part of it in the right order, I shall say to every man alive that a god was generous to you and gave you divine song."

He asked for it, you notice. Nobody made him. He has spent the whole day pulling a cloak over his head to get through this man's songs, and now he puts down his cup and asks, politely, by name, for the one that is entirely about him.

The singer began, and took it up from where the Greeks burned their huts and went aboard and sailed away, and the men with Odysseus were already sitting inside the horse in the Trojans' own meeting-place, because the Trojans had dragged it up there themselves. And it stood there while they sat round it arguing, and they could not decide, and there were three ways it might have gone: split the hollow wood open with the pitiless bronze; or drag it to the top of the rock and push it over; or leave it standing as a great offering to please the gods. And that was the way it was going to end, because it was laid down that they would be destroyed when their city closed round that great wooden horse with all the best of the Greeks inside it.

And he sang how the sons of the Greeks poured out of the horse and sacked the city; how they went through it, this way and that, wrecking the steep town; and how Odysseus went like Ares to the house of Deiphobus with Menelaus beside him, and dared the most terrible fight of all there, and won it through great-hearted Athena.

That is what the famous singer sang. And Odysseus melted, and the tears ran down his cheeks out of his eyes and wetted them.

And the way a woman cries, falling on the body of her own husband, who has gone down in front of his city and his people trying to hold the day of destruction away from his town and his children; and she sees him dying, gasping for breath, and winds herself round him and shrieks aloud; and behind her men are hitting her back and her shoulders with the butts of their spears, driving her off into slavery, to have hard work and misery, and her cheeks waste with the most pitiful grief —

that is how Odysseus cried, and the tears were pitiful under his eyelids.

---

The poem has just compared him to a woman being dragged away from her husband's body on the night her city fell, and there are women in exactly that position, at Troy, on that specific night, because of the wooden horse. Which he built; and which he has just asked, by name, to hear a song about; and which he is weeping at.

I have read a good many attempts to soften that and I do not believe any of them. It has been coming since Book Three, when Nestor mentioned in passing that Athena turned against her own side over what the Greeks did in that city; the whole homeward voyage is the invoice. And here, at the moment of maximum self-pity, the poem reaches out without a word of comment and hands him the grief of the people he did it to. It does not say he deserves it. It says: this is what that felt like, and now it is what you feel like. And then it goes straight back to the story.

---

Nobody noticed him crying except Alcinous, who was sitting next to him and could hear him. And Alcinous stopped the music.

"Let Demodocus put the lyre down. Not everybody here is enjoying this. Ever since we sat down to dinner and the singer started, our guest has not once stopped grieving; some very heavy sorrow has got into him. Let the singer stop, so that all of us can enjoy ourselves together, hosts and guest alike. Everything we are doing is being done for our honoured visitor: the escort, the gifts, the friendship. Any man with the least sense treats a stranger and a suppliant like a brother.

"So do not go on hiding what I am about to ask you behind some clever evasion. Tell me the name they call you at home — your mother and your father, and the people in your town. Nobody in the world is nameless, good or bad; parents give a name to everyone once they are born.

"And tell me your country and your people and your city, so that the ships can carry you there by thinking about it. Because our ships have no steersmen and no steering oars, the way other ships do. They understand by themselves what men are thinking and what they want; they know every city and every rich field there is, and they cross the open water at enormous speed, wrapped in mist and cloud, and there is never the least fear of damage or of going down.

"Though I did hear my father Nausithous say, once, that Poseidon was angry with us for giving everybody safe passage home. He said that one day a well-built Phaeacian ship, coming back from an escort, would be smashed on the misty sea, and a great mountain would be set down over our city. That is what the old man said. The god may bring it about, or leave it undone, whichever pleases him.[^4]

"But come — where have you been driven to? What countries did you reach, what people? Were they cruel and wild, with no sense of right, or did they take strangers in, and have they god-fearing minds?

"And tell me why you cry. Why does your heart go, inside you, when you hear about the fate of the Greeks and of Troy? The gods made that. They spun destruction for those men, so that there would be a song in it for people who have not been born yet.

"Did some relation of yours die in front of Troy? A good man — a son-in-law, or a father-in-law? They are the nearest to us after our own blood. Or was it a companion? A man who understood you? Because a companion with an understanding mind is no worse a thing than a brother."

## NOTES TO BOOK EIGHT

[^1]: **The songs we have lost, and what a bard actually was.** Demodocus sings three times in this book, and every one of his songs is a summary of a story that existed and is now gone. The quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles comes from no poem we possess; nor does the wooden horse, which — and this surprises people — does not appear in the *Iliad* at all. That poem ends with Hector's funeral, before the horse, before the fall, before any of it. What survives to us are two poems out of a whole library: there was once a cycle of epics covering the entire war from its causes to the last homecoming, and they were known and quoted and loved, and they have all vanished, leaving titles, summaries, and quotations in other men's books. When Demodocus takes requests he is thumbing through a shelf that no longer exists. — Which also tells you what a working singer was. Not a man who recited one long poem, but a man with an enormous repertoire who could start anywhere in it, at a customer's request, and take it up in the right order. Note too what the Muse gave and took: the blindness and the song arrive together, in the same line, as a single transaction. Later ages built a legend that Homer himself was blind, and this passage is very likely where they got it.

[^2]: **The Phaeacian athletes' names.** Read them again. Acroneus is roughly Topship; Ocyalus is Swiftsea; Nauteus is Sailor; Prymneus is Sternman; Eretmeus is Oarman; Ponteus is Seaman; Proreus is Prowman; Thoon is Speedy; Anchialus is Seaside; Amphialus is Sea-all-round. Even the king is Alcinous, "strong of mind," his father was Nausithous, "swift with ships," and his daughter Nausicaa has ships in her name as well. An entire nation of sailors is walking about with sailing for a surname, and a Greek audience got the joke instantly and for nothing — which is exactly the sort of pleasure that evaporates in translation. It is the kind of thing an English reader would meet if a village of blacksmiths turned out to be called Forge, Hammer, Smith, Anvil and Sparks.

[^3]: **The fine for adultery.** There is law in the joke, and the law is the point of the ending. Hephaestus does not want revenge; he wants to be paid. He demands that Aphrodite's father hand back the courtship gifts — the substantial property a suitor transferred to a bride's family to make a marriage — on the grounds that the goods were paid over for a wife who has not behaved like one. And Poseidon settles the matter by standing surety, like a man guaranteeing a neighbour's debt. That is not divine justice; it is property, and a bad debt, and every man listening had seen something of the kind settled in his own village. Which makes the joke sharper than it looks: the gods on Olympus are not living in some other moral universe. They are living in a Greek village with no funerals.

[^4]: **The prophecy hanging over the Phaeacians.** Alcinous mentions it in passing, in the middle of a boast, and then shrugs — the god may do it or not. It is worth pinning down, because it is the one dark thing in this bright country and it is stated plainly. These people ferry everybody home; it is their national vocation, and their pride, and the reason the gods sit down to dinner with them. And Poseidon resents it, being the god who keeps men *from* getting home, and there is an old warning in the family that the price will eventually be a wrecked ship and a mountain dropped on the harbour. Hold on to it. The poem has not raised it for atmosphere.

*Book Nine: In Which the Stranger Gives His Name at Last, and Tells About a Cave.*
