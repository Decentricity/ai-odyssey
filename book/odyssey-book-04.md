# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK FOUR
### In Which There Is a Wedding Going On, Nobody Can Stop Crying, and a Man Wrestles the Sea

---

They (Telemachus and crew) came down into Sparta, into the hollow of the hills, and drove up to the house of Menelaus; and they found him giving a wedding feast for a great crowd of his people, for a son and a daughter both at once.

The daughter he was sending away to the son of Achilles, who had been promised her years before at Troy, in the middle of the fighting, in the way such promises get made — and the gods were bringing it off at last, and she was going out that day with horses and chariots to the famous city where her husband was king. And for his son he was bringing home a girl from Sparta: a good match. The son's name was Megapenthes, and he was strong and well thought of, and he was the child of a slave woman, because after Hermione (his daughter) the gods gave Helen no more children.[^1]

So they were feasting in the great high-roofed house, the neighbours and the family of Menelaus, enjoying themselves; and a singer was performing among them with his lyre, and two acrobats were going round and round in the middle of the floor, tumbling in time with the music.

And two young men and a chariot stopped in the gateway, and nobody noticed at all.

I want to point out, before anything else happens, that this enormous wedding is never mentioned again. Two marriages, a house full of guests, a girl leaving home for good — and the poem sets it all up like scenery and then walks past it and does not look back. It is there for one reason: so that you can see, straight away, the difference between this house and the house our story belongs to. Both are full of people eating. In one of them, it is a wedding.

---

Eteoneus, one of Menelaus's men, saw them and went in to ask what was to be done.

"Menelaus — there are two strangers at the gate, and they look like men of the line of great Zeus himself. Tell me: shall we unyoke their horses, or send them on to somebody else who will make them welcome?"

And Menelaus was disgusted with him.

"You were not a fool before this, Eteoneus. You are talking like a child. You and I ate other people's bread all the way home — the whole way, on the kindness of strangers, and only got here at all because they took us in. Unyoke their horses. Bring the men in to eat."

So the man went, at a run, calling others to come with him; and they led the sweating horses out from under the yoke and tied them at the mangers and threw down spelt (wheat) and white barley mixed for them, and tipped the chariot up against the shining wall of the gateway. And they brought the two young men into the house.

And they stared.

There was a brightness like the sun and the moon through that high-roofed house — the gleam of bronze and gold and amber and silver and ivory going away in every direction — and Telemachus said quietly to Nestor's son, with his head close so nobody would hear:

"Look at it. This is what the court of Zeus on Olympus must be like inside. The quantity of it. I am staggered."

And Menelaus heard him anyway, and answered:

"Dear children, no mortal man competes with Zeus. His house and his belongings last forever. Men — well. There may be a man who could match me, and there may not.

"I brought all this home in my ships after a great deal of suffering. Eight years I was wandering: Cyprus, Phoenicia, Egypt; I got as far as the Ethiopians, and the Sidonians (modern Lebanon), and the Libyans, where the lambs are born with horns on their heads and the ewes lamb three times in the year, and no man there, master or shepherd, ever goes short of cheese or meat or sweet milk, because they milk all year round.

"And while I was going up and down out there collecting all this, another man killed my brother, behind my back, and never gave him a moment's warning — by the cunning of his own cursed wife. So I take no pleasure in being lord of all this.

"I would rather live here with a third of it, and have the men back alive who died out there on the wide plain of Troy, far from the grazing lands of Argos (Greece).

"I cry for all of them. Often enough I sit here in the house and let my heart out in grief, and then I stop, because a man gets his fill of cold grief soon enough. But there is one man I miss more than all of them together, and when I think of him I cannot eat and I cannot sleep — because no Greek took on the work Odysseus took on, and he got nothing for it, and I got a lifetime of sorrow that will not go away. He has been gone so long that we do not even know whether he is alive. Old Laertes must be mourning him, and Penelope, and Telemachus, who was a baby in the house when he sailed."

And that was too much for Telemachus, hearing his father named. He pulled his purple cloak up over his eyes with both hands and cried.

Menelaus saw it, and knew, and hesitated — should he leave the boy to bring the subject up himself, or should he ask him first and go through it all with him? — and while he was still turning it over, Helen came down out of her high scented room.

---

She (Helen) came down like Artemis (god of the hunt) with her golden arrows, and her women came with her: Adraste, who set the chair for her; Alcippe, who brought the soft wool rug; and Phylo with the silver work-basket that Alcandra had given her in Egyptian Thebes, out of a house with more treasure in it than anywhere on earth. Menelaus had had two silver bathtubs from that house, and two tripods and ten talents of gold; and separately, to Helen, the woman of the house had given a golden spindle and this basket, silver, on wheels, with a gold rim round the top.

Her woman set it down beside her, full of yarn already spun, with the spindle laid across it wound with dark violet wool.

And she sat down in her chair with a footstool under her feet, and looked at the strangers, and said at once:

"Menelaus — do we know who these men say they are? I have never in my life — no, never — seen a likeness like it, in a man or in a woman. I am astonished, looking at him. That is Odysseus's son. That has to be Telemachus, the child that man left behind in his house, still a baby, when you Greeks came to Troy on my account. Shameless creature that I was."

"Wife, I think so too, now you say it," said Menelaus. "Those are his feet and his hands, and the way his eyes move, and the head, and the hair on it. And just now, when I was speaking about Odysseus — about everything he took on, for me — the boy put his cloak over his face and wept."

And then Nestor's son Peisistratus said: "Menelaus, you are right. He is that man's son. But he is a modest young man and it seems wrong to him to come here and start talking loudly the first time he is in your presence, when we both listen to your voice as if it were a god's. My father Nestor sent me to bring him, because Telemachus wanted to see you, and hoped you might advise him, or do something for him. A son has a very hard time in a house when his father is gone and there is nobody to take his side — which is where he stands now: his father gone, and nobody in the country to keep the trouble off him."

"Well," said Menelaus, "if the son of a man I loved that much is in my house — a man who took on so much for me — I had meant to give him a welcome above all the other Greeks, if only Zeus had let the two of us come home over the sea. I would have moved him out of Ithaca altogether, with his goods and his child and all his people. I would have cleared a city for him here in Argos (Greece) and built him a house, and we would have been together, and nothing would have come between us or spoiled it, until the black cloud of death came over us.

"And the god must have begrudged it. He alone got no homecoming."

And with that he set them all off, and every person in the room cried.

Helen cried, and Telemachus cried, and Menelaus cried; and Nestor's son cried too — but he was not crying for Odysseus. He was crying for his own brother, Antilochus, who had been killed at Troy by the splendid son of the shining Dawn (Memnon, ally of Troy), and whom he had never seen, and whom Nestor still spoke of. And he (Peisistratus )said, when he could:

"Menelaus — my father (Nestor) always said, whenever we talked of you at home, that there was nobody with sense like yours. So bear with me now. I do not enjoy crying after dinner, and there will be a new Dawn tomorrow morning. Not that I grudge anyone their tears for a man who is dead and has met his fate; it is the only honour we can pay the poor dead — cut off our hair, and let the tears come down our faces. I lost a brother out there myself. He was not the worst of the Greeks, either. You will have known him; I never did."

---

So Helen put a drug in the wine.

She had thought of something, and she did it while they were talking, quietly, as a hostess sees to the cups: she dropped into the bowl they were drinking from a drug that kills pain and anger and takes away the memory of everything bad. A man who swallowed that, once it was mixed in the bowl, would not let a tear down his cheek for a whole day — not if his mother died, not if his father died, not if his brother or his own son were cut down with a bronze blade in front of him and he watched it happen with his own eyes.

The daughter of Zeus had subtle drugs, and good ones, that Polydamna had given her in Egypt, the wife of Thon; because there the fertile earth grows the greatest number of drugs, many of them healing when they are mixed, and many of them deadly; and every man there is a doctor, and knows more about it than anyone else alive.[^2]

And when she had put it in, and told them to pour, she said: "Now let us sit and enjoy ourselves and tell stories. I will tell you one that fits.

"I could not begin to tell you everything Odysseus did and endured. But here is one thing that man brought off, in the country of the Trojans, where you Greeks suffered.

"He took a beating — his own, deliberately: he laid degrading marks all over his own body, and threw a bad cloth round his shoulders like a servant, and went into the enemy's wide streets looking like a beggar, which was nothing at all like what he looked like beside the Greek ships. He went into Troy like that, disguised, and every one of them was taken in.

"I was the only one who knew him. And I questioned him, and he slipped away from my questions, cleverly. But when I had bathed him and rubbed him with oil and put clothes on him, and sworn a great oath not to give him away to the Trojans until he was back at the ships — then he told me the whole plan of the Greeks.

"And he killed a great many Trojans with the long bronze on his way out, and went back with a great deal of information.

"The other Trojan women wailed and wailed. My own heart was glad — because by then it had already turned round, and I wanted to go home, and I was sick over the madness Aphrodite gave me when she led me away out of my own dear country, and made me leave my daughter, and my bedroom, and a husband who was not lacking in anything, not in mind and not in looks."

And Menelaus said: "Yes. All of that is true, my wife, exactly as you tell it.

"I have known a great many men's minds and a great many men's ways, and I have been about the world; but I never yet saw with my own eyes another heart like patient Odysseus's.

"Here is another thing that strong man did and endured — inside the wooden horse, where all the best of us were sitting, carrying murder to the Trojans.

"And you came out to us, wife. Some god must have put you up to it, a god who wanted to hand the Trojans the victory; and Deiphobus (prince of Troy) walked out there beside you.

"Three times you went round the hollow trap, feeling it, stroking it with your hands. And you called out to the Greek captains, every one of us, by name — and you did each man's wife's voice.

"Diomedes and I were sitting in the middle of it with Odysseus, and we heard you calling. The two of us started up, mad to get out, or else to answer you from inside, right then. And Odysseus held us. He held us down and stopped us, both of us, with the whole thing hanging on it.

"And every other Greek in there stayed quiet, except Anticlus, who wanted to answer you. And Odysseus put his hands over the man's mouth and clamped them there, and held on — and saved every Greek in that horse — and did not let go until Athena had led you away."

---

I am going to stop here, because two people have just told two stories that cannot both be true, in the same room, ten minutes apart, over a bowl of drugged wine, and everyone in the house is going to go to bed perfectly happy.

Helen's story: Odysseus came into Troy in disguise, and I alone recognised him, and I bathed him and hid him and swore an oath and let him go, and I had already turned back to the Greek side by then, and my heart was glad when Trojans died.

Menelaus's story: the Greeks were sitting inside the horse in the dark, minutes from being discovered and burned alive, and Helen came out and walked round it three times, and called every man in it by name in the voice of his own wife, and had Deiphobus of Troy on her arm while she did it.

He does not say she was trying to kill them. He says a god must have been prompting her, a god who wanted Troy to win. That is a husband being extremely careful at his own dinner table.

And the second story is worse than it looks, because of the detail everybody skims: *she did the voices*. She stood in the dark outside a wooden box containing the fifty men who had wrecked her life and the life of everyone she had lived among for ten years, and she used their wives against them. She knew what to say. Diomedes and Menelaus nearly answered her; a man named Anticlus opened his mouth and would have, and Odysseus had to hold his jaw shut in the dark. Some tellings say he suffocated him. Ours does not say, and I think ours would rather you did not ask.

So which Helen is at the table? The one who was secretly loyal all along and helped the Greeks in disguise, or the one who nearly killed them all with a party trick?

I do not think the poem knows. I think the poem is showing us something it understands better than it can explain, which is a woman who has come back — who is *home*, in a house made of Egyptian silver, with a husband who worships her and a daughter going out to be married — and who has to live in the same room as everything she did and everything that was done to her; and who has, by the way, the only pharmaceutical in the ancient world that makes it possible to sit in that room.

Look again at the order of events. She has the drug in the bowl *before* the storytelling starts. She is not soothing anyone's grief; she is anaesthetising a room so that the past can be discussed in it safely. And then the very first story told under the influence of that drug is a story in which Helen behaved well.

She is the hostess. She is the historian. She is the pharmacist. It is her house, and it is her version.

I will only add this, and then we can go to bed. She says she was sick over "the madness Aphrodite gave me." A very great deal rides on whether you believe that — whether Helen went to Troy or was taken there, whether a goddess made her do it or she wanted to go, and whether "a goddess made me" was ever anything more than a way of putting it. The Greeks argued about it for a thousand years and never once agreed.[^3]

---

They went to bed. Helen told the women to put out bedding in the porch, good purple rugs and blankets over them and thick cloaks on top; and the two young men slept there. And Menelaus slept in an inner room of the high house, and Helen of the long robes lay beside him.

And when Dawn came up, rosy-fingered, Menelaus got out of bed and dressed and slung his sharp sword over his shoulder and tied good sandals on his feet and came out looking like a god, and sat down beside Telemachus and said:

"Well, Telemachus. What has brought you here to bright Sparta over the broad back of the sea? Public business, or your own? Tell me the truth."

And Telemachus told him. The suitors. A hundred and eight of them in the house, killing the flocks and the shambling cattle, courting his mother, insolent past bearing. And: *I have come to your knees, to ask you to tell me how my father died. Say you saw it, or say you heard it from somebody wandering. Do not soften it out of pity. Tell me exactly what you saw.*

Menelaus was disgusted, and let it out.

"Cowards. Weaklings. They want to lie down in the bed of a man with a heart like that — and they are like a deer that puts her two newborn fawns to sleep in a lion's den, and goes off to graze the mountain slopes and the grassy valleys, and then the lion comes home to his own bed and kills them both, and it is ugly. That is what Odysseus is going to be to those men.

"Father Zeus, and Athena, and Apollo — if he is still the man he was that day on Lesbos, when he stood up to wrestle Philomeleides (king of Lesbos, who wrestled visitors) and threw him flat, and every Greek there cheered — if Odysseus walks in among those suitors as he was that day, they will find their marriages short and their weddings bitter.

"Now: what you have asked me, I will not talk round, and I will not tell you anything untrue. Everything the Old Man of the Sea told me — the unerring one — I will pass on to you, and I will not hide a word of it."

---

And here is the story he told, and it is the best story in the first quarter of this poem, and it is the story that finally answers the question we have been asking since page one.

"The gods held me in Egypt. I wanted to come home, and they kept me there, because I had not made them the offerings I owed them. The gods always want us to remember what we owe.

"There is an island in the surf off Egypt — they call it Pharos — a day's clear sail out from the coast with a good following wind, with a harbour where ships can put in and take on dark (drinking) water. And there the gods kept me twenty days, and no wind ever got up. Every scrap of food was gone, and the spirit was going out of my men.

"And a goddess took pity on me. Eidothea, the daughter of Proteus, the mighty Old Man of the Sea. I had got at her heart somehow. She came on me wandering by myself, away from my men, who were forever going round the island fishing with bent hooks, because their bellies were driving them.

"And she stood beside me and said: *Are you a complete fool, stranger? Or a weakling? Or is this what you like — suffering? You have been shut up on this island a long time and you cannot find a way out, and your men are running out of heart.*

"And I said: *Whichever goddess you are, I am not stuck here because I want to be. I must have done something wrong to the gods who hold the wide sky. So tell me — you gods know everything — which of them has me tied up here? And how do I get home over the fish-filled sea?*

"And she said: *I will tell you exactly. There is a certain unerring Old Man of the Sea who comes here — deathless Proteus of Egypt, who knows the depths of the whole sea, and who serves Poseidon. They say he is my father. If you could somehow lie in wait for him and take hold of him, he would tell you the way, and the distances, and how to get home over the fish-filled sea. And he would tell you, if you wanted, what has been done in your own house, good and bad, while you have been away on this long miserable journey.*

"And I asked her how to trap the old god, because he would see me coming and slip out.

"And she said: *At the exact hour the sun stands over the middle of the sky, the unerring Old Man comes up out of the water under a dark ripple raised by the west wind, and lies down to sleep in a hollow cave; and the seals come up out of the grey water round him and sleep in a herd, and they smell bitterly of the deep sea. I will bring you there at first light and lay you out in a row. Choose three of your men, the best you have on your ships.*

"*And here is what the old man will do. He will count the seals over, going along the fives, and look them over; and when he has counted them all he will lie down in the middle of them, like a shepherd among sheep. The moment you see him asleep — that is the moment for strength and for holding on. Hold him there, however hard he fights to get away. And he will try. He will turn himself into every creature that moves on the earth, and into water, and into a blazing fire. Hold him. Grip harder. And when at last he asks you questions in his own shape — the shape he was in when you saw him lie down to sleep — then let go, and let the old man be, and ask him which god is angry with you, and how you get home over the fish-filled sea.*

"And with that she went down into the swell of the sea.

"She came back at dawn with four sealskins, freshly flayed, and she had scooped out four lying-places in the sand; and she laid us down in a row and put a skin over each of us.

"That was the most disgusting ambush ever laid. The stink of those sea-fed seals was murderous — nobody lies down happily beside a monster of the deep — but she saved us. She had thought of it, and it was the best of remedies: she brought ambrosia, and put it under each man's nose, sweet-smelling, and it killed the stench of the animals.[^4]

"We waited all morning with our hearts steady, and the seals came up out of the sea in a crowd and lay down in rows along the edge of the water. And at midday the old man came up out of the sea and found his fat seals, and went along them and counted them; and he counted us in among the animals first of all, and never suspected a thing. And then he lay down himself.

"And we came at him shouting, and threw our arms round him. And the old man did not forget his cunning.

"He turned into a lion with a great mane. And then a snake, and a leopard, and a huge boar. And then he was running water. And then he was a tree in leaf with the branches going up.

"And we held on. We held on, steady, and we did not let go.

"And when at last the old man got tired of it, he asked me a question. *Which of the gods, son of Atreus (Menaleus), put you up to this? Ambushing me and taking hold of me against my will? What do you want?*

"And I said: *You know. Why put me off with questions? I have been stuck on this island a long time and I cannot find the way out and there is no heart left in me. Tell me — you gods know everything — which of the immortals has me tied up here, and how do I get home over the fish-filled sea?*

"And he said: *You should have made the proper offerings to Zeus and the rest of them before you sailed, if you wanted to get home quickly over the wine-dark water. It is not your fate to see your own people and your well-built house and your own country until you have gone back to the water of Egypt — the rain-fed river — and made holy offerings to the everlasting gods who hold the wide sky. And then they will give you the road you want.*

"It broke my heart to hear it. He was telling me to go back over the misty sea to Egypt, a long hard way. But I answered him: *Old man, I will do it. All of it, exactly as you say. But tell me this as well, and tell me straight. Did all the Greeks come home safe in their ships — all the ones Nestor and I left behind when we sailed from Troy? Or did some of them die on their ships, or in their friends' arms, with the war finished?*

"And he said: *Son of Atreus, why ask me that? You would be better off not knowing, and not looking into my mind, and I do not think you will get through the answer without crying.*"

---

He was right, of course. And I will let Menelaus report the three answers as he got them, because they are the whole business of the poem so far, arriving at last, on a beach, out of the mouth of a shape-changing sea god who did not want to give them.

**The first** was Ajax — not the great Ajax, but the other one, the son of Oileus, and you have met him already in this book, though not by name. His ships were wrecked by Poseidon on the great rocks; and Poseidon saved him, actually saved him, and set him down alive out of the water. And he would have got away with it, hated by Athena as he was — except that he opened his mouth. He said, out loud, that he had escaped the great gulf of the sea *in spite of the gods*. And Poseidon heard him say it, and took his trident in both hands and struck the rock, and split it, and half of it stayed standing and half went down into the sea with the man on it; and it carried him under, and he drank the salt water and died.

*(That is the man who dragged Cassandra out of Athena's sanctuary on the night Troy fell. See Book Three, note 3. Everything in this poem eventually gets billed to somebody.)*

**The second** was Agamemnon. Proteus told it, and this is the fourth telling in four books, and it is at last the thing itself: he got past the great headland in his ships, hard as it was, and thought he was home. And then a bad wind took him and carried him to the edge of his own country — the very edge, the place where Aegisthus was living — and the wind turned kind again, and he came in and set foot on his own land, and touched it, and kissed it, and the warm tears came down his face because he was seeing his own country. And a watchman saw him. Aegisthus had put that watchman on the headland a year before and promised him two talents of gold to keep looking. And the man ran with the news. And Aegisthus set the trap at once: twenty men hidden in the hall, a feast laid on the other side of the room, horses and chariots sent down to bring the king up in honour. And he brought Agamemnon in, thinking of murder, and fed him, and killed him at dinner, the way a man kills an ox at the feeding trough. And not one of the king's companions was left alive, and not one of Aegisthus's either. They died on top of each other in that hall.

**And the third**, when Menelaus asked whether any of them was still alive:

*"Son of Laertes. Odysseus. I saw him on an island, crying hard, in the house of the nymph Calypso, who is keeping him there against his will. And he cannot get back to his own country, because he has no ships with oars and no companions to carry him over the wide back of the sea."*

That is it. That is the answer. Four books, three kingdoms, an assembly, a night crossing, two long dinners, an old man's memory and a sea god's arm — and what comes back is: *he is alive, he is on an island, he is crying, and he has no way off.*

And the boy who came all this way for it will not hear it for another sixteen books, because Menelaus is telling this to Telemachus, and Telemachus is not going to reach his father until much later and by a very different road. Everyone in the poem now knows where Odysseus is except the people who need to.

---

Proteus told Menelaus one thing more, about himself, and I include it because it is strange and lovely and slightly outrageous.

*It is not your fate to die in Argos (Greece). The immortals are going to send you to the Elysian plain, at the ends of the earth, where fair-haired Rhadamanthus is, and where living is easiest for men: no snow, no hard winter, no rain at all, and the ocean always sending up a clear west wind to blow cool on the people there. Because you have Helen, and because you are the son-in-law of Zeus.*

Read that again. Menelaus is not going to die and go down into the dark like everybody else in this poem. He is going to a garden at the end of the world with perfect weather.

Not for courage, not for justice, ot for anything he did, but because of who he married.[^5]

---

Then Menelaus wanted to give presents, and Telemachus wanted to go home.

"Do not keep me long here," he said. "I could happily sit in this house a whole year and never miss my parents, I am enjoying your stories so much. But my crew are already restless in sacred Pylos, and you are keeping me. Whatever gift you give me, let it be a keepsake. And no horses for Ithaca, please — leave them here, where you can enjoy them. You have wide plains here, with clover and reeds and wheat and rye and white barley. There are no wide runs on Ithaca and no meadows. It is goat country, and I like it better than horse country. None of the islands are good for driving or have good grass, and Ithaca least of all."

And Menelaus laughed, and stroked his hand, and said his name, and said: "Good blood, my boy. That is well said. Very well — I will change the gift; that is easy enough. Of everything stored in my house I will give you the finest and most valuable thing there is. I will give you a mixing bowl, worked all over. It is solid silver with a gold rim, and Hephaestus made it, and the king of the Sidonians gave it to me when his house sheltered me on the way home. That is what I want you to have."[^6]

---

And now we must go back to Ithaca, where it is the same morning, and where something has gone wrong.

The suitors were in front of the great hall, amusing themselves throwing the discus and the spear on the levelled ground where they always did it, in their usual style. Antinous and Eurymachus were sitting there, the leaders, easily the best of them.

And Noemon came up — the man who had given the ship — and asked Antinous a question.

"Antinous, do we know at all when Telemachus is coming back from sandy Pylos? He has my ship, and I need it. I want to cross to Elis, where I keep twelve mares with hard-working mules under them, unbroken. I want to drive one off and break it in."

They were astonished. They had had no idea he had gone anywhere. They thought he was out on the farm with the sheep, or with the swineherd.

"Tell me the truth," said Antinous. "When did he go? And who went with him? Picked men of Ithaca, or his own hired men and servants? Could he even manage that? And tell me this too, so I am clear: did he take the black ship from you by force, against your will — or did you hand it over because he asked you nicely?"

"I gave it to him," said Noemon. "What else would anybody do, when a man like that, with all that trouble on him, asks a favour? It would be hard to refuse.

"And the young men who went with him are the best in the country after us. And I saw Mentor go aboard as their captain — or a god who looked exactly like him.

"But that is the strange thing. I saw Mentor here myself, yesterday morning, at dawn. And he had already sailed for Pylos."

And he went off to his father's house.

And the two of them were left with their hearts full of it. They made the rest of the suitors sit down and stop their games, and Antinous stood up and spoke, black with anger, his eyes like fire burning.

"This is a disgrace. Telemachus has brought off a journey — arrogantly, in style, and we all said it would never happen. A boy. Against every one of us. He has launched a ship and picked the best men in the country and gone.

"He is going to be a menace. May Zeus break his strength before he grows into it.

"Give me a fast ship and twenty men, and I will lie in wait for him in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos, and he will find out what sailing after his father costs."

They all approved, and told him to do it, and got up and went into the house.

---

Now Penelope was not long finding out what they were plotting, because Medon the herald heard it. He was outside the courtyard while they were weaving it together inside, and he went straight through the house to tell her; and she met him on the threshold.

"Herald — what have those men sent you for now?"

"They mean to kill your son," he said. "They are going to ambush him on his way home and kill him with the sharp bronze. He has gone after news of his father, to sacred Pylos and to bright Sparta."

And her knees gave under her, and her heart, and she could not speak for a long time; her eyes filled with tears and her voice would not come. And at last she said:

"Herald — why has my son gone? He had no need to go on board any ship. Ships are the horses of the sea for men; they carry them over the water. Was it so that even his *name* would not be left among men?"

"I do not know," said Medon, "whether a god started him or whether it was his own heart. He has gone to Pylos to ask about his father — how he died, or whether he is coming home."

And he went back through the house.

And a grief that eats the heart came down over her, and she could not bring herself to sit on any of the chairs in the room, though there were plenty; she sat down on the threshold of her own richly-made bedroom, crying pitifully, and all the women of the house cried round her, young and old.

"Listen to me," she said, sobbing. "Of all the women brought up in my generation the Olympian has given me the most grief. First I lost a husband with a lion's heart, a man better in every way than any of the Greeks, whose name goes right across the country. And now the storm winds have snatched my beloved son out of the house with no word said, and I never even heard he was going.

"You are hard women. Not one of you thought to get me out of my bed — and you knew perfectly well, every one of you, the night he went down to the hollow black ship. If I had known he meant to make that journey he would have stayed, however much he wanted to go, or he would have left me dead in this house.

"Somebody call old Dolius to me — the servant my father gave me when I came here, who keeps my orchard — and let him go to Laertes (father of Odysseus) and sit down beside him and tell him all of it. Laertes may think of something. He may go out and appeal to the people, and tell them what these men are doing — these men who want to wipe out his line, and the line of godlike Odysseus."

And Eurycleia the nurse said: "Dear child, kill me with the pitiless bronze or let me live in the house, whichever you like, but I will not hide it from you.

"I knew. I gave him all of it, the bread and the sweet wine. And he made me swear a great oath not to tell you until twelve days had gone, or until you missed him yourself and heard that he had gone, so that you would not spoil your lovely skin with crying.

"Now wash, and put clean clothes on, and go upstairs with your women, and pray to Athena, the daughter of Zeus who holds the storm cloud. She can save him even out of death. And do not trouble the old man; he is troubled enough. I do not believe the family of Arceisius is altogether hated by the gods. Someone will be left to hold this high-roofed house and the rich fields all round it."

So she quieted her, and stopped her crying; and Penelope washed and put clean clothes on and went up with her women and put the barley grains in a basket, and prayed to Athena.

"Hear me, Unwearied One, daughter of Zeus. If ever Odysseus burned you the rich thigh pieces of an ox or a sheep in his house, remember it now for me, and save my son, and keep these suitors and their arrogance away from him."

And she cried out in prayer, and the goddess heard her.

And the suitors made a noise in the shadowy hall, and one of them said: "The queen must be arranging her wedding. She has no idea that her son has been marked for killing." That is what they said. They had no idea what had been arranged for *them*.

Then Antinous told them to stop talking. And they took a fast ship down to the sea and stepped the mast and fitted the oars in their leather straps and hauled up the white sail, and their servants brought their armour; and they moored her well out in the water and got out and had their dinner and waited for the evening.

---

And Penelope lay upstairs without eating, without touching food or drink, turning over and over the one question — whether her decent son would get away, or be beaten down by the arrogant men. And she turned it as a lion turns, cornered in a crowd, frightened, when the men close their treacherous ring round him. And in the middle of it, sweet sleep came over her and she lay back and slept, and all her joints let go.

And Athena thought of something else.

She made a phantom. She made it in the shape of a woman — of Iphthime, another of the daughters of great Icarius (Penelope's father), whom Eumelus had married and who lived far away in Pherae — and she sent it into the house of Odysseus to stop Penelope's crying and her sobbing and her grief.

It came into the bedroom past the strap of the door-bolt, and stood over her head, and said:

"Penelope. Are you asleep, with your heart in pain? The gods who live easy will not let you cry or be distressed. Your son is coming home. He has done nothing to offend them."

And Penelope answered her, sleeping very sweetly at the gate of dreams:

"Sister — why have you come here? You never used to; you live so far away. And you tell me to stop grieving, and stop all the pain going round and round in my mind, when I have already lost a husband with a lion's heart, better than any of the Greeks, whose name goes right across the country — and now my boy has gone away in a hollow ship, and he is only a child, and he knows nothing about hardship or about talking to men. I cry for him even more than for the other one. I shake for him, in case something happens to him out there in the country he has gone to, or on the sea. He has enemies, and they are plotting against him, and they want to kill him before he can get home."

"Take heart," said the dim phantom, "and do not be too frightened. He has a companion going with him that other men would pray to have standing beside them, because she has the power: Pallas Athena. She pities you in your grief. It is she who sent me here to tell you this."

And Penelope said: "If you are a god, and you have heard a god's voice — then tell me about that other unhappy one as well. Is he alive somewhere? Is he still looking at the light of the sun? Or is he dead already and in the house of the dead?"

"I will not tell you about him," said the dim phantom. "Whether he is alive or dead. It is a bad thing to talk on the wind."

And it slipped out past the door-bolt into a breath of wind.

And Penelope woke, and her heart was warm in her, because a clear dream had come to her out of the dark of the night.

---

And the suitors went aboard and sailed the watery ways, planning murder in their hearts.

There is a rocky island in the middle of the sea, halfway between Ithaca and rugged Samos — not a large one — called Asteris, with a double harbour in it where ships can lie hidden.

There the Greeks lay in wait for him.

## NOTES TO BOOK FOUR

[^1]: **Megapenthes.** The poem gives you this in a single clause and moves on: Menelaus's only son is the child of an enslaved woman in his household, because Helen bore him no children after Hermione. It is stated the way one might state a man's age. There is no scandal in it, no concealment, and no suggestion that the boy's position is awkward — he is well thought of and is being married off at a great public feast on the same day as his half-sister. This is simply how great households worked, and how a man in this world made sure of an heir: the wife's children inherited first, the others were acknowledged and provided for, and the mother's own view of the arrangement is not recorded anywhere and was not thought worth recording. I mention it because it goes past in half a line, and because a reader who does not stop will not notice that they have just been told something quite large about the women of these houses. (See Book One, note 10, on Eurycleia.)

[^2]: **The drug.** The Greek names it *nepenthes* — literally "no-grief" — and describes its effects with unusual precision: not sleep, not intoxication, but a chemical suspension of mourning lasting a day. Egypt was where such things came from, in the Greek imagination and probably in fact; Egyptian medicine was the most advanced in the eastern Mediterranean and its practitioners were famous abroad, which is why the poem says every man there is a doctor. What the drug actually was, if it was anything, has been guessed at for two thousand years — opium is the usual suspect. What matters more is that our poem, which is not otherwise interested in pharmacology, pauses to give it four lines, and gives it to *Helen*, and has her administer it to a room full of men without asking them.

[^3]: **Did Helen go, or was she taken?** The single most argued-over question in Greek literature, and there was never an answer. In the *Iliad* she loathes herself and speaks of Aphrodite compelling her, and it is left unresolved. The lyric poet Stesichorus is said to have been struck blind for slandering her, and to have recovered his sight only after writing a second poem taking it all back — in which she never went to Troy at all, and a phantom went in her place while the real Helen sat out the war in Egypt. The historian Herodotus, who visited Egypt, reported that the Egyptian priests told him much the same thing, and believed them, on the grounds that the Trojans would obviously have handed her over rather than lose their city. Euripides wrote a whole play on that version. — Notice what all of these have in common: they are attempts to make the story bearable. Ours does not take that way out. It puts her in a room with her husband, with the drug, and with two incompatible accounts of her own conduct, and lets her be a person rather than a verdict.

[^4]: **Ambrosia.** The food of the gods; the word means, roughly, "the not-mortal stuff." It turns up here as a perfume smeared under the nose, and elsewhere as something eaten, and elsewhere again as a preservative rubbed on a dead body to keep it from decaying. It is best understood not as a substance with fixed properties but as *the divine version of a thing* — divine food, divine ointment, divine scent — and its one constant property is that it stops decay and death from doing their work. Which is exactly what it is doing on that beach: four men are lying under rotting sealskins, and the ambrosia keeps the smell of mortality off them.

[^5]: **Elysium.** Almost everyone else in this poem is going to the same place when they die, and it is not a nice one — a grey, sunless, memoryless holding-pen for the dead, which we will visit in person, at length, in Book Eleven, and where the greatest hero of the age will tell us to our faces what he thinks of it. Elysium is the exception, and at this early stage of Greek belief it is not a reward for virtue at all. There is no heaven for good men here, no judgement, no scales. Elysium is for a tiny number of people connected to the gods by blood or by marriage, and Menelaus qualifies on a technicality: he is married to a daughter of Zeus. That is the whole reason, and Proteus states it as a reason. Later Greeks, who found this unsatisfactory — as you may — quietly rebuilt the afterlife into something with a moral filter on the door. The version in front of us has none. It is worth knowing that when you read what happens to the people in this poem who behave well and die anyway.

[^6]: **"No horses for Ithaca."** A small speech, easy to skip, doing three things at once. It is good manners: refusing an unusable gift gracefully, and Menelaus laughs with pleasure because the boy has handled it beautifully — *good blood* — which is a compliment about breeding standing in for a compliment about upbringing, and Telemachus has had no father to give him one. It is geography: Ithaca is a poor, steep, goat-grazed rock, and the kingdom everyone is murdering each other over is worth strikingly little. And it is character: given the chance to pretend to be richer than he is, in the most gilded house he has ever stood in, the boy says plainly that his country has no meadows and that he prefers it. — The bowl, meanwhile, is a *gift*, in the technical sense (Book One, note 7): an object whose value is largely the relationship it records. Keep an eye on it. Objects like that do not stay where they are put in this poem.
