# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK SIXTEEN
### In Which a Father and a Son Are Alone in a Room, and Only One of Them Knows It

---

At first light, in the hut, Odysseus and the good swineherd lit a fire and got their breakfast ready and sent the herdsmen out with the droves of pigs.

And the dogs, who bark at everybody, did not bark. They went fawning round somebody coming up the path.

Odysseus noticed it, and heard the sound of feet.

"Eumaeus — somebody is coming. A friend of yours, or somebody the dogs know, because they are not barking; they are making a fuss of him. And I can hear footsteps."

He had not finished saying it when his own son stood in the doorway.

The swineherd jumped up in amazement, and the bowls he had been mixing the bright wine in fell out of his hands. And he went to meet his master and kissed his head and both his beautiful eyes and both his hands, and the tears came pouring down.

The way a loving father greets a son who comes back in the tenth year out of a far country — an only son, a late child, for whom he has suffered a great deal — that is how the swineherd put his arms round godlike Telemachus and kissed him all over, as though he had got out from under his own death.

"You have come, Telemachus. Sweet light. I thought I should never see you again once you had gone off in the ship to Pylos. Come in, dear child, and let me have the pleasure of looking at you, newly here from somewhere else. You do not often come out to the farm and the herdsmen; you stay in the town — as though it were any pleasure to you to look at that destructive crowd of suitors."

"So be it, old friend. I have come out here for your sake — to see you with my own eyes, and to hear from you whether my mother is still at home, or whether some other man has married her by now, and the bed of Odysseus is lying empty, with nothing on it, and the cobwebs gathering."

"She is in your halls, with a steady heart, enduring; and the nights and the days waste away for her, wretchedly, while she cries."

He took the bronze spear from him, and Telemachus stepped in over the stone threshold. And as he came in, his father got up from his seat to make room for him.

And Telemachus stopped him. "Sit down, stranger. We shall find a seat somewhere else in our own farmyard; here is a man who will put one out."

So Odysseus sat down again, and the swineherd heaped green brushwood for the young man and threw a fleece on top; and Telemachus sat down there, and the swineherd set out platters of roast meat left over from the night before, and heaped bread into a basket, and mixed honey-sweet wine in an ivy bowl, and sat down opposite. And they put out their hands to the good things in front of them.

"Old friend, where does this stranger come from? How did the sailors bring him to Ithaca? Who did they say they were? Because I do not suppose he came here on foot."

And Eumaeus told him what he had been told: a Cretan, who had wandered through a great many cities of men, with a god driving him about; who had run away from a Thesprotian ship and come to the farm. "And now I put him in your hands. Do as you like. He says he is your suppliant."

"Eumaeus, that is a hard thing to say to me. How am I to take this stranger into my house? I am young, and I cannot yet trust my own hands to defend a man if somebody starts on him. My mother's heart is divided two ways: whether to stay here with me and keep the house, respecting her husband's bed and what the people say, or to go now with whichever of the Greeks courting her in the halls is the best man and offers most.

"But since this stranger has come to *your* house, I shall clothe him in a cloak and tunic, good clothes, and give him a two-edged sword and sandals for his feet, and send him on wherever his heart tells him to go. Or if you like, keep him here on the farm and look after him, and I shall send the clothes out here and all his food, so that he is not a drain on you and your men.

"But I will not have him go up there among the suitors. They are far too full of insolence and violence, and they will make a mockery of him, and that would be a bitter grief to me. It is hard for one man to get anything done against a crowd, however strong he is; they are a great deal stronger."

---

And the much-enduring Odysseus said:

"Friend, since it is right that I should answer — my heart is torn open, listening to you tell what these suitors get up to in your halls, against the will of a man like you.

"Tell me: do you put up with it willingly? Or do the people of your country hate you, following some voice from a god? Or do you blame your brothers, who are the men a fighter relies on when a serious quarrel comes?

"I wish I were as young as I am angry, and were the son of Odysseus, or Odysseus himself — then let some stranger cut my head off my neck if I did not walk into the halls of that man and make myself a plague to every one of them. And if they beat me down by weight of numbers, one against so many, I would rather be killed in my own house than have to watch these disgraceful things going on and on: guests knocked about, servant women dragged through the fine halls, wine drawn off and wasted, men eating up food idly and endlessly, for a thing that is never going to be brought off."

"Stranger, I shall tell you exactly. The people are not hostile to me and there is no quarrel with them. And there are no brothers to blame, either. Zeus made this a single line: Arceisius had one son, Laertes; Laertes had one son, Odysseus; and Odysseus left one son — me — in his halls, and had no joy of me.

"So the house is full of enemies. All the chief men of the islands — Dulichium, Same, wooded Zacynthus — and everyone who is anybody in rocky Ithaca, they are all courting my mother and wearing the house away. And she neither refuses the hateful marriage nor can bring herself to end it; and they eat my living away, and before long they will tear me to pieces too."

Then he turned to the swineherd. "Eumaeus, go quickly and tell my mother that I am back safe from Pylos. And come straight back here; tell nobody else. There are a great many Greeks planning trouble for me."

"Shall I take the same message to Laertes as I go? The old man used to look after his farm, whatever grief he had for Odysseus, and eat and drink with the servants when he felt like it. But they say that since you went off in the ship he has not eaten or drunk, and has not looked at the work of the farm, but sits grieving and crying, and the flesh is wasting off his bones."

"Sad as it is, we shall let him alone. Tell my mother, and have her send the housekeeper out to him quietly, and she can tell the old man."

And he sent the swineherd off; and Eumaeus tied his sandals on and went to the town.

---

And Athena did not fail to notice that the swineherd had gone. She came up to the hut in the likeness of a woman, tall and beautiful and skilled at fine handwork, and stood in the doorway where Odysseus could see her.

Telemachus did not see her at all; the gods do not appear plainly to everybody. But Odysseus saw her, and so did the dogs, and they did not bark; they whimpered and shrank away across the yard.

She signalled with her eyebrows. Odysseus understood and came out of the hall, past the great wall of the yard, and stood in front of her.

"Son of Laertes. Speak to your son now and hide it no longer, so that the two of you can plan the killing of the suitors and go into the town. And I shall not be far away; I am ready for the fight."

And she touched him with her golden wand. She put a freshly washed cloak and tunic about him first, and made him taller and younger; the brown came back into his skin, his cheeks filled out, and his beard turned dark again.

Then she went away, and Odysseus went back into the hut. And his own son stared at him and was frightened, and turned his eyes away, in case this was a god.

"Stranger — you look different than you did a moment ago. Your clothes are not the same, and your skin is not the same. You must be one of the gods who hold the wide sky. Be gracious to us. We will give you acceptable offerings and worked gold. Spare us."

"I am no god. Why do you compare me to the immortals?

"I am your father. The one you have been grieving for, and suffering for, and putting up with other men's violence for."

And he kissed his son, and let a tear fall to the ground, which until then he had held back, every one of them.

And Telemachus did not believe him.

"You are not Odysseus. You are not my father. Some god is deceiving me, to make me cry harder. No mortal man could arrange this by his own devising — not unless a god came himself and easily made him young or old, whichever he wanted. Just now you were an old man in filthy clothes; now you look like one of the gods."

"Telemachus, it is not right for you to be so amazed that your father is in the house, or to stand there wondering. No other Odysseus is ever going to come here. I am he. I have suffered a great deal and wandered a great deal, and I have come in the twentieth year to my own country.

"This is Athena's doing. She makes me whatever she likes, because she can; one moment a beggar, the next a young man with fine clothes on his body. It is an easy thing for the gods who hold the wide sky to glorify a mortal man or to bring him low."

And he sat down. And Telemachus threw his arms round his good father and wept.

And in both of them the longing to cry rose up, and they cried out loud, harder than birds — sea-eagles, or vultures with hooked claws, whose young the country people have taken out of the nest before they could fly. That is how pitifully the tears fell from under their eyelids.

And the light of the sun would have gone down on the two of them crying, if Telemachus had not suddenly said: "But what ship brought you here? What sailors? Who did they say they were? Because I do not suppose you came to Ithaca on foot."

---

There is the reunion, and I want to point at one thing in it and then leave it alone.

He does not prove anything.

Every other recognition in this poem has a token attached: there will be a scar in a few books' time, and a piece of furniture at the very end, and each of them is a hard fact produced under pressure to satisfy somebody who has decided not to be fooled. Here there is nothing of the kind. His father simply says *I am your father*, and the boy says he does not believe it, and the man says it again and adds that Athena did the rest — and that is the whole of the evidence, and it is accepted.

Which makes sense, when you think about it, because there is nothing available. Telemachus was an infant in arms when this man sailed. He has no memory of a face, no voice to compare, no scar he was ever shown. He cannot recognise his father; he can only be told, and decide. And what settles it is not proof at all: it is the crying, which goes on for so long that the poem says the sun would have set on it.

He is the only person in the book who has to take it on trust.

---

Odysseus told him the truth: that the Phaeacians had brought him and put him ashore asleep, and given him bronze and gold and woven cloth, which was now stored in a cave by the will of the gods; and that Athena had sent him here to plan how the suitors might be killed.

"Now tell me the number of them, so that I know how many there are and what sort of men. Then I shall turn it over in my own mind and decide whether the two of us can take them on alone, or whether we should look for help."

"Father, I have always heard of your great name — that you were a mighty spearman and a wise head in council. But this is a very large thing you have said. Two men cannot fight a crowd of strong ones.

"The suitors are not ten, and not twice ten. They are a great many more than that. Count them here, and now. From Dulichium there are fifty-two picked young men, with six servants; from Same, twenty-four; from Zacynthus, twenty; and from Ithaca itself, twelve, all of them the best. And with them are Medon the herald and the divine singer, and two attendants who are skilled at carving meat.

"If we go against all of that inside the hall, I am afraid your revenge would turn out bitter and terrible when you came to take it. Think whether you can find a helper — somebody who would stand up for us both with a ready heart."[^1]

"Then I shall tell you, and you turn it over. Do you think Athena and father Zeus will be enough for us, or shall I think about another helper?"

"Those are excellent helpers you name, though they sit up high among the clouds and hold power over all men and all immortals."

---

Then Odysseus gave him the plan, and it is worth having in full, because everything from here to the end of the poem runs on it.

"In the morning, go home and join that overbearing crowd. The swineherd will bring me to the town later, looking like an old beggar in a bad way. And if they insult me in the house, let the heart in your breast put up with it while I am badly treated. Even if they drag me by the feet through the hall and out of the door, or throw things at me — watch it and endure it. Tell them to stop being foolish, certainly, and coax them with gentle words. They will not listen; their day is standing beside them.

"And I shall tell you something else; keep it in your mind. When Athena, who thinks of everything, puts it into my head, I shall nod to you. And the moment you see it, take all the weapons of war that are lying in the hall and carry them up and store them in the corner of the high chamber — every one of them.

"And when the suitors miss them and ask you, put them off with soft words. Say: *I have laid them away out of the smoke, because they are not what they were when Odysseus left them and went to Troy; they are spoiled, as far as the breath of the fire has reached them. And besides, the son of Cronos (Zeus) has put a bigger fear in my heart — that you may quarrel over your wine and wound each other and disgrace the feast and the courtship. Iron by itself draws a man on.*[^2]

"But leave two swords behind for the two of us, and two spears, and two oxhide shields, where our hands can snatch them up.

"And nobody is to know that Odysseus is in the house. Not Laertes, not the swineherd, not any of the servants, and not Penelope herself. You and I alone will find out how the women stand; and let us test some of the men as well, and see who honours us and fears us in his heart, and who does not care and slights you, young as you are and worthy as you are."

"Father, I think you will find out what sort of spirit I have in due course; I am not soft in the head. But I do not think this particular plan will profit the two of us, and I ask you to consider it. You will be a very long time going round the farms testing every man, while at their ease those others go on eating your goods in the halls, without sparing anything.

"The women I do agree you should find out about — which of them dishonour you and which are innocent. But I would rather not go round the farms testing the men now. Let us see to that later, if you really know some sign from Zeus."

---

Meanwhile the ship that had brought Telemachus from Pylos came in to the town, and they hauled her up and carried the gifts to the house of Clytius, and sent a herald to the house of Odysseus to tell Penelope that her son was out in the country and had ordered the ship into the city, so that she should not be frightened and cry.

And the herald and the good swineherd met, both of them carrying the same message to the lady. And when they reached the house, the herald said it out loud in the middle of the servant women: "Your dear son, my queen, has come back from Pylos." And the swineherd went up close to Penelope and told her quietly everything her son had told him to say; and when he had said it all he went out through the courtyard and back to his pigs.

And the suitors were dismayed, and went out and sat down in front of the wall of the yard, outside the gates.

Eurymachus spoke first. "Friends, this is a large thing that Telemachus has brought off in the most insolent way — this journey. And we said it would never happen. Come, let us launch a black ship, the best there is, and get a crew of rowers together, to go quickly and tell the others to come home."

He had not finished when Amphinomus turned in his place and saw a ship inside the deep harbour, with men taking the sails down and oars in their hands. And he laughed pleasantly and said: "There is no need to send any message. They are here. Either some god told them, or they saw her going past themselves and could not catch her."

So the men from the ambush came up from the shore and went to the assembly, and would not let anybody else, young or old, sit down with them.

And Antinous said:

"So the gods have got the man out of trouble. Day after day our lookouts sat on the windy heights, one relieving another; and at sunset we never slept on shore but stayed at sea in the fast ship waiting for the bright Dawn, lying in wait for Telemachus so as to catch him and kill him. And meanwhile some god has brought him home.

"So let us think here about a miserable end for him. He must not get away. I do not believe our business will ever be brought off while he is alive. He is shrewd himself in judgement and planning, and the people are not showing us any particular favour any more.

"Come — before he calls the Greeks to assembly. Because I do not think he will be slack about it. He will be full of anger, and he will stand up in front of everybody and tell them how we plotted his death and failed to catch him; and they will not approve when they hear an ugly thing like that. Take care they do not do us harm, and drive us out of the country, and we go off to somebody else's.

"Let us get in first and take him out in the fields, well away from the town, or on the road. And let us keep his living and his goods, dividing them fairly among ourselves, and give the house to his mother to keep, and to whichever of us marries her.

"But if that does not please you, and you want him to live and keep everything his father left him — then let us stop gathering here and eating up his treasure, and let each man court her from his own house with wedding gifts. And she can marry the one who gives most and comes as her destined husband."

Nobody said anything at all.

Then Amphinomus stood up and spoke among them — the son of Nisus, and the leading man from Dulichium, and the one whose conversation pleased Penelope most, because he had a decent mind.

"Friends, I would not want to kill Telemachus. It is a dreadful thing to kill a man of royal blood. Let us first find out what the gods think about it. If the decrees of great Zeus approve, I shall kill him myself and tell everybody else to. But if the gods turn us away from it, then I say we stop."

They all thought that was well said, and got up and went back into the house of Odysseus and sat down on the polished chairs.

---

Hold on to Amphinomus. He is the only one of the hundred and eight who is ever given a good motive for anything, and he has just talked the others out of a murder; and Penelope likes him, and the poem says so plainly and tells you why. Nothing more will be made of him for two books. He is not going to be spared.

---

But Penelope had heard. The herald Medon had been standing outside the courtyard while they wove the plot inside, and he came and told her; and she came down the long stairs to the hall with her women, and stood by the doorpost with her bright veil in front of her face, and spoke to Antinous by name.

"Antinous. Insolent man. Contriver of wickedness. They say you are the best of all the men of your age in Ithaca for judgement and for speech. You were never any such thing.

"Madman — why do you spin out death and doom for Telemachus, and take no notice of suppliants, whose witness is Zeus? It is an unholy thing to plot evil against one another.

"Do you not know that your own father came here as a fugitive, in terror of the people? They were furious with him, because he had joined up with Taphian pirates and raided the Thesprotians, who were at peace with us. They wanted to destroy him, and tear the heart out of him, and eat up all that great living of his.

"And Odysseus held them off and stopped them, angry as they were.

"And now you eat up his house without paying for it, and court his wife, and try to kill his son, and grieve me terribly. I tell you to stop, and to make the others stop."

And Eurymachus answered her — smoothly.

"Daughter of Icarius, wise Penelope. Take heart. Do not let these things trouble your mind. That man does not exist, and never will, who will lay hands on Telemachus your son while I am alive and looking at the light of the sun.

"Because I tell you this, and it will be brought to pass: his black blood would run round my spear at once. Odysseus himself used to sit me on his knees, often, and put roast meat in my hands and hold the red wine to my lips. So Telemachus is far the dearest of all men to me, and I tell him to have no fear of death — not from the suitors. From the gods it cannot be avoided."

So he said, cheering her up; and he was arranging Telemachus's death in his own mind while he said it.

And she went up to her bright upper room and cried for Odysseus, her own husband, until Athena cast sweet sleep on her eyelids.

---

And in the evening the good swineherd came back to Odysseus and his son. They were getting supper ready, having sacrificed a yearling pig; and Athena had come first and struck Odysseus with her wand and made him an old man again and put the shabby clothes back on him — in case the swineherd looked him in the face and knew him, and went off and told Penelope and did not keep it in his heart.

Telemachus spoke first. "You are back, Eumaeus. What is the news in the town? Are the lordly suitors home from their ambush yet, or are they still out there watching for me?"

"I did not care to go about the city asking. My heart told me to give my message and come straight back. But a fast messenger from your crew met me, a herald, and he got to your mother first. And there is one thing I do know, because I saw it with my own eyes. I was above the town, where the hill of Hermes is, when I saw a fast ship coming into our harbour, with a great many men in her, and she was loaded with shields and double-edged spears. I thought it was them. I do not know."

And Telemachus smiled, and looked across at his father — and took care not to catch the swineherd's eye.

Then they finished the work and laid out the meal and ate; and when they had had enough of eating and drinking they thought about bed, and took the gift of sleep.

## NOTES TO BOOK SIXTEEN

[^1]: **A hundred and eight men.** This is the only place the poem does the arithmetic, and it is worth writing down, because the vagueness of "the suitors" can make them feel like a crowd of extras. Fifty-two from Dulichium with six servants; twenty-four from Same; twenty from Zacynthus; twelve from Ithaca itself, and those the best men on the island. A herald, a singer, and two carvers. That is a hundred and eight armed young men of good family, plus staff, against two — and shortly against four. Telemachus gives the figures because he is trying to talk his father out of it, and his objection is entirely sound. Note also what the numbers say about Ithaca: the twelve local men are outnumbered eight to one by outsiders. This is not a village quarrel. It is most of the aristocracy of four islands, camped in one house, waiting for a kingdom to fall vacant.

[^2]: **"Iron by itself draws a man on."** The excuse Telemachus is coached to give for clearing the weapons out of the hall has two halves, and the second half is a proverb: the mere presence of a weapon pulls a man towards using it. It is offered here as a piece of ordinary wisdom that no one would think to argue with — of course you do not leave spears standing about a room where men are drinking — and it is doing double work, because it is also a perfectly accurate description of what is about to happen in that room. The plan depends on the suitors accepting a commonplace about human nature and failing to notice that it is being used against them.
