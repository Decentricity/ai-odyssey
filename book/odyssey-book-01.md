# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK ONE
### In Which the Gods Hold a Meeting, and a Young Man Discovers He Has a Voice

---

Before we begin, a word about beginnings.

The old poets never simply *started*. That would have seemed to them rather like sitting down to dinner without saying anything at all, or building a house without laying a foundation, or (to use a comparison they would have understood better than either) setting out on a long sea voyage without first pouring a little wine into the water for the gods and hoping they were in a good humour. A poet always began by asking for help. Not from his listeners — from a Muse.[^1]

So the old poem begins with a request, and since we are going to be together for some while, you and I, I shall begin with one too, and then get on with it.

*Tell me about the man of many turnings — the one who was clever in a hundred ways and got very little good out of any of them; who wandered a long, long way after he had knocked down the holy towers of Troy; who saw the cities of many peoples and learned what was in their minds; who suffered a great deal at sea, in his own heart, struggling to keep his life and to bring his companions home. He never did bring them home, though. They died of their own foolishness, the idiots — they ate the cattle of the Sun, and the Sun took away their day of homecoming. Begin wherever you like, goddess. Begin anywhere, and tell us too.*

There. That is more or less how it went, and I have kept it, because it tells you three things straight off, and it is polite to tell people what a story is about before they are three hundred pages into it.

The first thing is that this is a story about a man who was extremely clever and it did not save him. The second is that he lost every single one of his men, and that it was not his fault, which is a thing the poem is at some pains to say early and say plainly. And the third — well, the third is the one that matters most. All of that business with the sea and the monsters and the cattle of the Sun: it is not the point of the story. It is the part everybody remembers, the way everybody remembers the dragon and not the will. The point of the story is a house. A particular house, on a small stony island, with a woman in it and a boy who has grown up in it without a father, and a great many uninvited guests eating everything in it. The sea monsters are on the way to the house. Do not lose sight of the house.

Now then.

---

By the time our story opens, the war at Troy had been over for ten years, and every Greek[^2] who was going to get home had got home. They had straggled back one by one, out of the storms and the shipwrecks and the long detours, and their wives had wept over them, and they had hung up their shields and settled down to complain about the weather and the young. The rest were dead — buried under the walls of Troy, or drowned on the way back, or (in one particularly nasty case, which we will come to) murdered on their own doorsteps by people who ought to have been glad to see them.

Only one man was still out.

His name was Odysseus, he was the king of a small rough island called Ithaca off the west coast of Greece, and he was, at this moment, sitting on a beach a very long way from it, crying.

He was being kept there by a goddess called Calypso, who lived alone on an island in the middle of nowhere, who was beautiful, who was in love with him, and who had told him — quite sincerely, and more than once — that if he would only agree to stay with her she would make him immortal, and he would never grow old, and they would live together forever in a cave with vines growing over the mouth of it and four springs running clear water in four directions. She was not being cruel. She thought she was offering him the best thing anyone had ever been offered, and by most reckonings she was.

He said no. He said no for seven years. He spent his days sitting on the rocks looking east over the water, hoping to see so much as the smoke going up from the chimneys of his own house, and wishing he were dead; and at night he went back to the cave and lay down beside her because there was nowhere else on the island to lie down. That is the situation, and I want you to sit with how uncomfortable it is for a moment, because a great many people have retold this part as though it were a holiday. It was not a holiday. It was seven years of being loved by somebody you have not chosen, on an island you cannot leave.

And the gods, on the whole, were sorry for him. All of them except one.

The exception was Poseidon, who was god of the sea and of earthquakes and of horses (an odd combination until you have seen surf coming in), and who had a grudge against Odysseus of the deepest and most personal kind, which we shall come to in its proper place. It is enough for now to say that Odysseus had blinded Poseidon's son, and that Poseidon was not the sort to let that go. He could not quite kill the man outright — that was not permitted him — but he could keep him from home, and he had been keeping him from home for a decade, with the patience of water working on stone.

It happened, however, that just now Poseidon was away. He had gone off to the far south to a feast among the Ethiopians, who lived at the ends of the earth where the sun rises and sets, and who were famous for two things: being the most distant people in the world, and being unusually good hosts.[^3] He would be gone some while, and he had left no one minding the door.

Which was exactly what Athena had been waiting for.

---

The gods lived on Mount Olympus, in the north, on a peak so high that it goes up out of the weather altogether; and while people down below imagined all sorts of things about their arrangements up there, the honest truth is that the arrangements were embarrassingly like a family. They ate together. They argued at the table. They took sides, and remembered slights, and worked round one another, and the whole enormous machinery of the world went grinding along beneath them as a sort of by-product of who happened to be annoyed with whom that week.

At the head of the table was Zeus, who was king, and who had been thinking about a murder.

"It really is extraordinary," he said, to no one in particular, in the tone of a man returning to a grievance he has aired before, "the way they blame us. Listen to them. Every disaster that comes to a mortal, and it's the gods, the gods, the gods — as if we sat up here inventing miseries for them. When the plain fact is that they manufacture the greater part of it themselves, out of their own recklessness, and then send up the bill to Heaven.

"Take Aegisthus. We *told* him. We sent Hermes down specially — went to some trouble about it — and said: do not murder that man, and do not marry that man's wife, because there is a son, and the son will grow up, and when he is grown he will come for you. We could not have been plainer. And he did it anyway, and the boy grew up, and came for him, exactly as advertised. And now I suppose we shall be told that was our doing as well."[^4]

There was a small pause of the kind that falls when everyone at a table knows that somebody is about to use an opening.

"Father," said Athena.

Now I must tell you about Athena, since she is going to be in this story more than anyone else except the two people it belongs to. She was Zeus's daughter, and his favourite, and the goddess of exactly three things, which were: war (but only the kind that is planned and won, not the kind that is enjoyed), crafts and weaving and everything made carefully with the hands, and cleverness. That last is the important one. She was the patron goddess of good ideas, quick answers, and lies told well in a tight corner — and you can see at once why Odysseus was her particular favourite among all the men alive, because he was the finest liar in the world and she admired him for it the way a master carpenter admires a joint.

"Father," she said, "Aegisthus got what was coming to him, and so may everyone else who does what he did. But my heart is broken over Odysseus. Poor wretched man — he has been away from his own people all this time, on an island in the navel of the sea, in the house of Calypso, who keeps him there with soft words and coaxing so that he will forget Ithaca. And he cannot forget it. He would be glad to see the smoke going up from his own roof and then die. And it does not move you at all. Did he never sacrifice to you, out there beside the ships at Troy? Why are you so hard on him?"

"Child, what a thing to say." Zeus looked genuinely startled. "How could I ever forget Odysseus? There is no man alive with better sense, or who gave the gods their due more generously. It is my brother Poseidon, who is in a rage about the Cyclops — his son, whose eye Odysseus put out. He will not kill him. He simply moves him about the sea and will not let him come home. Well. Let us all put our heads together and settle it. Poseidon will have to give way. He can hardly hold out against every one of us at once."

"Then send Hermes," said Athena, who had had the plan ready for some time and was not going to waste the moment, "to Calypso's island, to tell her plainly that it is decided, and that Odysseus goes home. And I will go to Ithaca — to his son. I will put some spirit into the boy. I'll have him call the Greeks to assembly and speak out to the whole island against these men who are eating him out of house and home. And then I'll send him off to Pylos and to Sparta to ask after his father. He may hear something. And if he hears nothing, he will at least come back a man with a good name, which is worth having."

Zeus agreed, because he always did in the end, and because it was a good plan.

And Athena went down.

---

She went down from the peaks of Olympus and came to Ithaca, and stood at the outer gate of the courtyard of Odysseus's house, holding a bronze spear; and she had taken on the appearance of a man — a stranger, a sea-captain named Mentes, chief of a people called the Taphians, who were traders and part-time pirates, there being less difference between the two professions than either would have admitted.[^5]

And what she saw, standing at that gate, was this.

The courtyard was full of young men. They were sprawled on the hides of oxen — oxen they had killed themselves, out of the herds of the house — and they were playing at dice, and shouting, and enjoying themselves enormously. Servants were running about among them at a half-trot, the particular half-trot of people who have been working too hard for too long with no end in sight: some mixing wine and water in the great bowls, some wiping down the tables with sponges and setting them out again, some carving up more meat than any household could afford. There were a hundred and eight of these young men. They were the sons of the best families of Ithaca and the islands round about. They had been living in that house, at that house's expense, for something over three years.

They were there to marry the queen.

Here I had better stop and explain, because this is the part that a modern reader stumbles over, and if you stumble over it now the whole book will limp.

Odysseus had been gone twenty years. There was no news of him, no body, no grave — nothing. Under the customs of the time a woman in that position was in an impossible middle place: not a wife, because her husband was almost certainly dead; not a widow, because nobody could prove it. And the kingdom was in the same middle place. Ithaca had no king, and its heir, Telemachus, had been an infant in arms when his father sailed and was now barely twenty — old enough to be angry, not yet old enough to be obeyed.

So the young men of the district had worked it out among themselves, as young men will. Whoever married Penelope would in all likelihood become king. Therefore they would all court her at once. Therefore they would all move into her house, and stay there, and eat and drink at her expense until she gave in and chose one — and the longer she held out, the more of the estate they would get through, which they did not mind in the least, because the whole point of the exercise was that the estate would eventually belong to one of them.

You will want to know why she did not simply throw them out. She could not. A woman could not order armed men off the property, and the law of that country provided her no help she could call on. You will want to know why she did not simply *say no*. Then her father would have taken her home and married her to somebody of his choosing, and the house would have passed out of Telemachus's hands entirely; and there would still have been a wedding, only now she would have no say in it at all. You will want to know why Telemachus did not throw them out, and the answer is arithmetic: one young man and a handful of loyal servants against a hundred and eight armed sons of powerful families, every one of whom had a father and brothers who would want blood if he were harmed.

They were, in short, not besieging the house. They were doing something cleverer and much crueller. They were being *guests*. And that is the joke at the black heart of this entire book, because in that world there was no law more sacred than the law of guests — and these men had turned it inside out and were wearing it as armour.[^6]

---

Telemachus saw the stranger at the gate before anyone else did, because he was not enjoying the party.

He was sitting a little apart from the rest, sick at heart, doing the thing he did most days, which was to imagine his father walking in through that gate: coming in out of nowhere, scattering the suitors like poultry, taking back his own house and his own honour and his own place at the head of his own table. It is a daydream that will be familiar to anyone who has ever been young and powerless in a house full of people behaving badly. It had the particular quality of all such daydreams, that it did not make him feel better afterwards.

And then he looked up, and there was a stranger standing at the gate — waiting, being kept waiting — and Telemachus was up and across that courtyard like a shot, appalled.

He took the stranger's right hand in his own and took the bronze spear with the other, and he said: "Welcome, sir. Sit down and eat something, and afterwards you shall tell us what you need."

Notice the order of that sentence. He did not ask the man's name. He did not ask his business, or where he was from, or how long he intended to stay. That was not merely good manners; it was the law, and Telemachus was following it to the letter in a house where nobody else had followed it for three years. First you take the stranger in, and feed him, and give him a bath and clean clothes and a good chair. *Then* you ask who he is. And when he leaves you give him a present, and if he ever comes back, or his children come, or his children's children, your house and his are friends forever and neither may lift a hand against the other. It was called *xenia* — guest-friendship — and it was under the personal protection of Zeus himself, who kept a special title for the purpose: Zeus of the Strangers, god of anybody who turns up at your door with nothing.[^7]

Which is why it is quite an important detail that the young man who kept the law that day, alone in that house, was giving a chair and a footstool and the best cuts of the meat to a god in disguise.

He led her in and set her spear in the rack against the great column, where his father's spears were standing — still standing, still there, and I do not think you need me to tell you what that rack of spears was doing to him every day of his life. He gave her a fine carved chair set a little apart from the crowd, with a linen cloth over it and a footstool, and drew up a seat for himself beside it, so that his guest would not have to eat within earshot of the noise and would not have to listen to men bellowing over dice while she ate.

A servant girl brought water in a golden jug over a silver basin and poured it over their hands, and set out a polished table, and the housekeeper brought bread and a great many small dishes, and the carver brought platters of meats and set gold cups beside them, and the steward went up and down keeping them filled.

And then the suitors came in.

They came in and took their places and set to, with a great deal of noise, and when they had eaten and drunk as much as they wanted they began to want other things — dancing and singing, which is the crown of a feast — and a herald put a beautiful lyre into the hands of a man named Phemius, who sang for those men because they made him, and who began to sing.[^8]

And under the cover of the music, Telemachus put his head close to his guest's, so that no one else would hear, and said:

"Friend — I hope you won't take this badly. It's easy for *them*: they eat another man's living and pay nothing for it, a man whose white bones are lying out in the rain somewhere on land, or rolling in the sea's surf. If they saw him walk in through that door there is not one of them who would pray to be faster on his feet — they'd pray to be richer in clothes and gold, and much good it would do them. As it is, he has died badly, and there's no comfort anywhere, not even if somebody told me he was coming. His homecoming is finished.

"But tell me — who are you? Where do you come from? What ship? And this is what I really want to know: is this the first time you have been here, or were you a friend of my father's? A great many men used to come to this house. He got about, in his day."

And Athena — Mentes — said: "I'll tell you everything. My name is Mentes; my father was Anchialus, and I'm chief of the Taphians. I'm here with my own ship and crew, sailing the wine-dark sea to trade iron for copper. My ship's lying out of town under Mount Neion. My family and yours have been guest-friends since your grandfather's time; ask old Laertes if you doubt me. And I came because they told me your father was home. Apparently the gods have him tangled up somewhere still.

"He isn't dead, though. Odysseus is alive, and on some island, held by hard men who won't let him go. I'm no prophet and I don't read birds, but I'll tell you what I think: he will not be away much longer. He'd get out of an iron cage. He'll find a way.

"But tell me this. You're — what, Odysseus's son? You must be. That head, and those eyes; it's uncanny."

"My mother says so," said Telemachus, "and I suppose she would know. Though for my own part I sometimes wish I were the son of a man who had grown old on his own farm among his own things. As it is, I'm the son of the unluckiest man ever born. That's your answer."

Then Mentes said, "Well, the gods have not left your house without a name, whatever else they've done, if Penelope has a son like you. But now tell me about *this*." And the stranger looked out across the hall — at the shouting, and the meat going round, and the dogs, and the wreckage of it. "What is all this? A wedding? A public dinner? Because it certainly isn't people paying for their own food. They look to me like men eating another man's house down around his ears, and behaving very insolently about it. Any decent man walking in here would be disgusted."

And that opened the door, and out it all came.

"Stranger — since you ask — there was a time when this house was rich and respectable, back when *he* was still on the island. But the gods have decided otherwise, and they have made him vanish more completely than any man who ever lived. I could bear it better if he were dead. If he had gone down at Troy with his friends around him, or died in his own bed with the work of the war behind him, the Greeks would have built him a barrow, and I'd have had that at least to hand on. As it is the storm winds have carried him off with no name and no notice, and left me nothing but grief.

"And he isn't the only thing I have to cry about. Look at them. Every man of rank in Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus, and every man of rank on this rock of an island, is courting my mother and wrecking my house. And she cannot refuse them — it would be the ruin of her — and she cannot bring herself to choose. And meanwhile they eat, and drink, and in a little while they will have eaten me down to nothing, and I expect me with it."

Athena was very angry when she heard that, and she said:

"By heaven, you need your father. If Odysseus were to come and stand in that doorway now, with a shield and two spears and a helmet on his head, the way I first saw him — if he came in on those men the way he was that day, they would find their marriages short and bitter. But that lies with the gods, whether he comes home and pays them out in that hall or not. So think about this instead: how are you going to get them out of the house *yourself*?

"Listen to me and do as I say. Tomorrow morning call the Greeks to assembly — all of them — and speak, and let the gods be your witnesses. Tell the suitors to go home. As for your mother, if she has a mind to marry, let her go back to her father's house, and let *them* deal with the dowry and the bride-gifts, as is proper for a daughter they think well of.

"And here is my advice for you. Take a ship — the best you can get — and twenty rowers, and go and ask after your father. Go to Pylos first, to old Nestor; and then to Sparta, to Menelaus, because he came home last of all the Greeks and he will have picked up more news than anybody.[^9] If you hear your father is alive and on his way, then grit your teeth and put up with this for one more year. And if you hear he is dead, then come home, and heap up a barrow for him, and give him the funeral rites in full, and give your mother to a husband. And when that is done and finished — *then* think very carefully about how you are going to kill these men in your house, by a trick or in the open.

"You are too old to be a child any longer. Have you not heard what a name young Orestes got for himself among men, when he came home and killed the man who murdered his father? You are tall, my friend, and you are handsome, and men will be talking about you for a long time yet. Be brave.

"And now I must go down to my ship. My crew will be getting restless. See to this yourself, and remember what I've said."

"Friend," said Telemachus, "you have spoken to me like a father to his son, and I will not forget it. But stay a little longer — bathe, and rest, and let me give you a present to take home with you, something fine, something worth keeping, the way friends give to friends."

"No — don't hold me now. I am in a hurry. Whatever gift you feel moved to give me, give it to me when I come back, and let it be a good one, and you shall have as good in return."

And with that Athena went out of the hall, and up — like a bird going up, straight up out of sight.

And she left something in the young man's chest that had not been there that morning. He stood a moment where she had left him, and then he understood, in the way one does understand such things, without being told: and he went across the hall towards the suitors, and he was not the same person who had been sitting in the corner an hour before.

---

The bard was still singing. He was singing about the Greeks coming home from Troy, and about all the ways it had gone wrong for them, and the suitors were sitting quiet for once and listening.

And upstairs, Penelope was listening too. She heard it from her room above the hall, and she could not bear it, and she came down the long stairs with two maids behind her and stood by the pillar at the edge of the room with her veil across her face; and she was crying, and she said:

"Phemius — you know a hundred songs. Sing them one of those. Let them sit here and drink their wine in peace and listen to anything else at all. Only stop singing *that* one, because it breaks my heart in my breast every time, and I have a grief in this house that will not be lifted, and a man I remember every hour of every day, whose name is spoken across the whole of Greece."

And Telemachus said: "Mother — why shouldn't he sing what he likes?"

I want you to notice that this is the first thing Telemachus says in the whole poem to his own mother, and that he has just come from a conversation with a goddess.

"It isn't the poets who cause these things," he said. "Zeus causes them; he gives out what he likes to each of us, and there's no arguing with it. There's nothing wrong in singing about the Greeks' bad end. People always praise the newest song loudest, whatever it's about. Set your heart to listen. My father was not the only man who never came home from Troy; a great many others were lost as well.

"Go back upstairs, and see to your own work — your loom, and your spinning, and keep the maids at theirs. Talking is men's business, and mine most of all, since I am the master of this house."

And Penelope went back upstairs, astonished, and turned that over in her mind for a long while, and cried herself to sleep. And I confess I have never been quite sure how to take that speech — whether it is a boy trying on his father's voice and finding it a size too large, or a young man discovering that being brave and being kind are two different accomplishments and that you can manage the first before you have learned the second. Probably it is both. Most people are unbearable for a little while just after they find their courage.

The suitors, meanwhile, broke into an uproar in the shadowy hall, every one of them praying to be the man who got into her bed. But Telemachus stood up in front of the lot of them and said:

"Suitors of my mother — you insolent, overbearing men — let us enjoy our dinner, and let there be no shouting, because it is a fine thing to listen to a singer like this one. But in the morning let us all take our places in assembly, all of us, and I will make my speech to you plainly. Get out of my house. Feast somewhere else; eat your own food; go round from house to house and use up your own inheritances. Or if you think it better and more profitable to eat one man's living and pay him nothing for it — then go on doing it, and I will call on the gods, and Zeus may yet see that you are paid what you are owed; and you will die in this house, and there will be no vengeance taken for any one of you."

That silenced them. They bit their lips and stared at him, because they had never in three years heard him speak like that.

Then Antinous said — and Antinous is a man you may as well start disliking now, because you will be doing a great deal of it later — "Telemachus. The gods themselves must be teaching you to make speeches, and to make them so *boldly*. I hope Zeus never makes you king of Ithaca, even though it is yours by birth."

"Antinous," said Telemachus, "I'll say this even if it annoys you. I should be quite happy to take the kingship, if Zeus offered it. Do you think it's the worst thing that can happen to a man? There's nothing wrong with being a king: your house grows rich and you are held in honour. But there are plenty of other Greek princes on this island, young and old, and one of them can have the title, now that Odysseus is dead. What I *will* be master of is my own house, and my own servants, whom my father won for me."

And Eurymachus said, smoothly — for Eurymachus was the one who always spoke smoothly, and you should distrust him rather more than Antinous, not less — "Telemachus, which of us is to be king of Ithaca lies with the gods; but nobody is going to take your property from you while there are men living on this island. Now. Tell me about your visitor. Where was he from? What was his family? Did he bring news of your father, or was he here about some debt of his own? He got up and went off very quickly; he didn't wait to be introduced. He didn't look like a man of no account, either."

"Eurymachus," said Telemachus, "my father's homecoming is over and done with. I don't believe any news now, whoever brings it, and I have no time for prophecies. That man was an old guest-friend of my father's from Taphos: Mentes, son of Anchialus, chief of the seafaring Taphians."

So he said. And in his heart he knew perfectly well that he had been talking to a god.

Then they turned to dancing and singing again and enjoyed themselves until evening came on; and when it did, they went off to their own homes to sleep, which is the one thing that could be said in their favour.

And Telemachus went up to his own high room in the fine walled courtyard, in a place with a view all round it, and went to bed with his mind turning over and over.

Going with him, carrying blazing torches, went Eurycleia — daughter of Ops, son of Peisenor — who had been bought by his grandfather Laertes long ago, when she was a girl, for the price of twenty oxen; and who had nursed Telemachus as a baby, and loved him more than any of the servants in that house did.[^10]

She opened the door for him, and he sat down on the bed and pulled off his soft tunic and put it into the old woman's hands, and she folded it and smoothed it and hung it on a peg beside the bedstead and went out of the room, drawing the door to by its silver handle and pulling the bolt home with the strap.

And there he lay all night, wrapped in a fleece, thinking about the journey Athena had told him to make.

## NOTES TO BOOK ONE

[^1]: **The Muses.** Nine goddesses, daughters of Zeus and of Memory — which is exactly the right parentage, as you will see. This poem was composed before writing was in general use in Greece, and it was not read; it was *performed*, from memory, by professionals, over several evenings, probably with a stringed instrument going underneath it. A poet who could hold twelve thousand lines in his head and produce them on demand did not feel that this was a thing he was doing entirely by himself, and he did not think he was being modest when he said so. When he asked a Muse to sing *through* him, he meant it fairly literally. The daughters of Memory were the ones who kept the words safe. Whether one man called Homer made this poem, or several, or a tradition of hundreds over centuries, is a question people have been arguing about for two thousand years, and it has a name — the Homeric Question — and no answer.

[^2]: **"Greeks."** The poem never uses the word. It calls them Achaeans, or Argives, or Danaans, more or less interchangeably, according to what fits the rhythm of the line. None of these means quite what "Greek" means today, and there was no country called Greece: there were many small kingdoms speaking related dialects who thought of themselves as one people only in certain moods — such as when sailing to Troy together. Throughout this book I say "Greeks", because saying "Achaeans" makes it sound like a story about strangers, and it is not.

[^3]: **The Ethiopians.** Not the people of the modern country, though the name survives there. To this poet the word meant, roughly, "the people at the far edge of the world where the sun is close," and they were understood to be exceptionally pious and to give exceptionally good dinners, for which reason the gods went to visit them and were reliably unavailable. It is the ancient equivalent of a character being out of mobile reception, and the plot needs it.

[^4]: **The murder Zeus is talking about.** Agamemnon was the commander-in-chief of the whole Greek army at Troy, the greatest king of his day. While he was away, his cousin Aegisthus seduced his wife Clytemnestra; and when Agamemnon came home in triumph after ten years of war, the two of them killed him at his own homecoming feast. Seven years later Agamemnon's son Orestes came back from exile and killed Aegisthus. — Now: keep that story in your hand for the whole of this book, because the poem is going to hold it up beside our story again and again, like a tailor holding cloth against cloth. A king comes home from Troy. There is a wife who has waited, and a son who has grown up, and men in the house who should not be there. It happened one way for Agamemnon. Everyone in this poem, gods included, is watching to see which way it will happen for Odysseus. Penelope is being measured against Clytemnestra every time she comes down the stairs. Telemachus is being measured against Orestes, and knows it, and it terrifies him.

[^5]: **Mentes, and a word you already know.** Athena spends most of this poem in disguise, and this is the first of many. Later she will borrow the shape of another of Odysseus's old friends, an Ithacan called Mentor, to guide and advise Telemachus on his travels — which is where our word *mentor* comes from. The word entered English by a roundabout route (a French novel of 1699 that retold these events for the education of a prince), and it means what it means because of a goddess pretending to be somebody's uncle.

[^6]: **The suitors, and why this is a horror story.** It is worth saying plainly what these men are doing, because our word "suitors" makes it sound like a Jane Austen problem. They have occupied a house whose master is missing and whose heir is a boy. They are consuming the estate deliberately — the estate they intend to inherit — as a way of applying pressure to a woman who has no legal means of removing them. They are doing this under the protection of a sacred custom, in numbers that make resistance suicide, and they are enjoying it. That is the situation, and everything else in this poem — the monsters, the shipwrecks, the twenty years — is the long way round to the door of that hall.

[^7]: **Guest-friendship (*xenia*).** The most important social institution in this world and the hardest for us to feel from the inside, because we have police, and hotels, and passports. In a world with none of those, a traveller far from home was a man with no protection of any kind, and the only thing standing between him and being robbed and killed on any road in Greece was a religious obligation — that the stranger at your door is sacred, and that Zeus himself watches how you treat him. Feed him first; ask his name afterwards; send him away with a gift. In return, the guest owes the host courtesy, honesty, and restraint. It goes both ways, and it binds the two families permanently. Once you have this in your head, you have the moral spine of the entire poem: nearly every episode in it is a test of hospitality, passed or failed. Odysseus's whole voyage is a series of doors he knocks on. The Cyclops fails the test at one end of the scale. The suitors fail it at the other.

[^8]: **Phemius.** Remember him. He is a professional bard in a hall he cannot leave, singing to men he did not choose to entertain — a fact that will matter very much indeed, several hundred pages from now, on a day when it is dangerous to be found in that hall. There is also a fine joke buried here, and I do not want you to miss it: this poem is being performed by a bard, in a hall, for a company of noblemen at dinner. Its audience were looking at a real Phemius while they listened. And the song he sings is *the Greeks coming home from Troy*, which is the song we are currently inside.

[^9]: **Nestor and Menelaus.** Nestor was king of Pylos, the oldest of the Greek commanders at Troy, famous for talking a very great deal and being, on balance, worth listening to. Menelaus was king of Sparta and the husband of Helen — whose departure to Troy, willing or otherwise, started the war in the first place. He got her back, and got home last of anybody, after being blown all the way to Egypt. They are the two surviving men most likely to know something, and Telemachus's journey to them takes up the next three books.

[^10]: **Eurycleia, bought for twenty oxen.** She is a slave. So are most of the servants in this house, and in every great house in the poem, and the text says so without embarrassment, because it did not occur to anyone that it required one. I am not going to smooth this over, and I am not going to let it pass without comment either. Hold two things at once, as the poem does: Eurycleia was purchased, like livestock, at a price the poem is happy to quote you; and Eurycleia loves this boy, and he loves her, and she is going to be one of the two or three people in the entire story who can be relied upon absolutely when everything comes apart. Both of those are true, and the second does not excuse the first. It is a very old book. Twenty oxen, and a lifetime, and a lamp in a doorway at the end of a bad day.
