# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK TWELVE
### In Which There Is Singing, a Choice Between Two Deaths, and the Last of the Ships

---

This is the last of the four books he tells himself, and it is the one where everything runs out: the ship, the men, the food, all of it, until there is one man in the water holding two pieces of wood. I mention it in advance because the poem will not stop to mark it. He simply gets to the end, and stops, and turns back into a guest who has talked rather too long.

---

When our ship had left the stream of Ocean and come to the wave of the wide sea, and to the island of Aeaea, where the house of early Dawn is and her dancing floors and the risings of the sun — we ran her ashore on the sand and went out onto the beach, and there we slept and waited for the bright morning.

And when Dawn came up I sent my companions to Circe's house to fetch the body of dead Elpenor. We cut logs quickly and buried him on the furthest point of the headland, grieving, letting the big tears fall. And when the corpse and the dead man's armour had burned, we heaped up a mound and dragged a standing stone onto it, and on the very top of the mound we planted his oar.

And Circe knew perfectly well that we had come back from the house of Hades, and she dressed and came down quickly, and her women came with her carrying bread and a great deal of meat and sparkling red wine. And the shining goddess stood among us.

*You reckless men. You have gone down alive into the house of Hades. You will be twice dead, when other men die only once. Come — eat here, and drink wine, all day long. And at first light you shall sail; and I will show you the way and make every part of it plain, so that you do not come to grief through some miserable piece of bad planning, on the sea or on the land.*

And our proud hearts agreed to that. So all day until sunset we sat and feasted on meat past counting and sweet wine; and when the sun went down and the darkness came, they lay down to sleep beside the ship's cables. But she took me by the hand and sat me down away from the others, and lay down beside me and asked about every single thing; and I told her all of it, in the right order. And then she said this.

---

*All of that is finished, then. Now listen to what I tell you — and a god himself will remind you of it.*

*You will come first to the Sirens, who bewitch every man who comes near them. Whoever comes on them without knowing, and hears the Sirens' voice, his wife and his little children never stand round him and welcome him home again. The Sirens bewitch him with their clear singing, sitting in a meadow; and there is a great heap of men's bones round about them, rotting, with the skin shrivelling on them.*

*Drive past. And knead sweet wax and stop up your companions' ears, so that none of them can hear. But if you want to hear it yourself, then have them tie you hand and foot in the fast ship, standing upright against the mast-step, with the ropes made fast to the mast itself — so that you can listen to the voices of the two Sirens and enjoy it. And if you beg them and order them to let you go, then let them tie you down with more ropes still.*

*And when your companions have driven the ship past them, then I am not going to tell you which of two roads you should take. You must work that out in your own mind. I shall describe them both.*

*On one side there are overhanging rocks, and the great wave of dark-eyed Amphitrite (Poseidon's wife, who breeds the monsters of the sea) roars against them. The blessed gods call them the Wandering Rocks. Nothing that flies gets past — not even the timid doves that carry ambrosia to father Zeus; the smooth rock always takes one of them, and the father sends another up to make the number right. And no ship of men that ever came there has got away; the waves of the sea and the blasts of destroying fire carry off ships' timbers and men's bodies together. One seagoing ship alone ever sailed past — the Argo, that everybody sings about — on her way home from Aeetes; and even her the wave would have thrown against the great rocks, but Hera (queen of the gods) sent her through, because Jason was dear to her.*[^1]

*On the other side there are two cliffs. One of them reaches the wide sky with its sharp peak, and a dark cloud stands round it and never streams away, and there is never clear air about the top of it, in summer or in autumn. No mortal man could climb it, not if he had twenty hands and twenty feet, because the rock is smooth, like something polished. And halfway up that cliff there is a misty cave, facing west, towards the darkness. That is where you will steer your hollow ship past, glorious Odysseus. A strong man shooting from a ship could not reach that cave with an arrow.*

*And Scylla lives in it, yelping horribly. Her voice is no bigger than the voice of a new-born puppy, but she herself is a dreadful monster; nobody would be glad to look at her, not even a god. She has twelve feet, all of them dangling, and six necks, immensely long, and on each of them an appalling head with three rows of teeth in it, thick and close together, full of black death. She is sunk to her middle inside the hollow cave, but she holds her heads out beyond the terrible gulf and fishes there, sweeping round the rock for dolphins and sea-dogs and whatever bigger thing she can catch. No sailors have ever yet got past her and been able to boast that their ship came through untouched. She takes a man in each head, and carries him off out of the dark-prowed ship.*

*The other cliff is lower — you will see; they are close together, and you could shoot an arrow across. On that one there is a great fig tree covered in leaves, and underneath it divine Charybdis sucks the black water down. Three times a day she throws it up, and three times she sucks it down, terribly. Do not be there when she sucks it down. Nobody could save you from destruction then, not even the Earthshaker.*

*Go close along Scylla's cliff instead, and drive the ship past fast. It is far better to grieve for six of your companions than for all of them together.*

*Goddess, tell me this truly. Could I somehow get clear of deadly Charybdis and still fight the other one off, when she goes for my men?*

*You obstinate man. So your mind is on fighting again, and on hard work. Will you not give way even to the immortal gods? She is not mortal. She is an immortal evil — terrible, savage, impossible to fight. There is no defending yourself. Running is best. If you stop to put your armour on beside that rock, I am afraid she will come out at you again with as many heads as before, and take as many men. Drive past hard, and shout for Crataiis, who is Scylla's mother, and who bore her as a curse to men. She will stop her coming a second time.*

*Then you will reach the island of Thrinacia. And there a great many cattle of Helios (the Sun, who sees everything and hears everything) graze, and his fat flocks: seven herds of cattle, and as many fine flocks of sheep, and fifty in each. They bear no young and they never die, and goddesses are their shepherds. If you leave those alone, and keep your mind on getting home, then you may still all reach Ithaca, badly off as you will be. But if you do them any harm, then I am telling you now: destruction for your ship and your men. And if you get out of it yourself, you will come home late and in a bad way, having lost every one of them.*

And the Dawn came up while she was still speaking; and the shining goddess went away up the island.

---

Almost everybody arrives at the Sirens carrying the wrong picture, and it is not the reader's fault but two and a half thousand years of paintings. Homer never describes them. Not their faces, not their bodies, not their number beyond Circe's passing *the two Sirens*; and there is no suggestion anywhere of the bedroom about them. The bird-bodies came later out of vase-painting, the fish-tails out of a medieval muddle with mermaids, and the seductress with the comb and the mirror is largely the nineteenth century. What the poem gives is a meadow, a heap of men with the skin drying on their bones, and a song — and when we hear the song, in a page or two, it will turn out to be an offer of *knowledge*. We know what happened at Troy, they sing, and everything that happens anywhere on the earth. Come and be told; you will go away knowing more.

Which means the hook is baited with precision for exactly one man's weakness, and it is not lust. It is the thing that took him into a stranger's cave to see what gift he might be given, and up every lookout on every island, and down to a pit at the edge of the world to ask the dead questions after he already had his answer.

---

So I went to the ship and roused my men, and Circe sent us a following wind behind the dark-prowed ship, a good companion, filling the sail. And then I spoke to my companions, sick at heart.

*Friends. It is not right that only one or two of us should know the prophecies Circe told me. I shall tell you, so that we may all know them, and then either die, or get away from death and out from under our fate. First of all she told us to keep away from the voices of the marvellous Singers, and from their flowering meadow. She said I alone might listen. But you must tie me with hard bonds, standing upright against the mast-step, with the ropes made fast to the mast itself. And if I beg you and order you to let me go, then you must tie me with more ropes still.*

And meanwhile the ship came quickly to the island of the Sirens, because a fair wind drove her. And then the wind dropped, all at once, and there was a windless calm, and some god put the swell to sleep. My companions stood up and furled the sail and stowed it, and sat down to the oars and made the water white with the polished pine.

And I took a great round cake of wax and cut it up small with the sharp bronze and worked it in my strong hands; and the wax grew warm quickly, because of the great pressure and the rays of the lord Sun; and I went along the line and stopped the ears of every man of them. And they tied me hand and foot in the ship, upright against the mast-step, with the ropes made fast to the mast; and then they sat down and struck the grey sea with their oars.

And when we were within calling distance, driving hard, the Sirens saw the fast ship coming close, and they raised their clear song.

*Come here, famous Odysseus, great glory of the Greeks. Stop your ship, so that you can listen to the two of us. No man has ever yet driven his black ship past this place without listening to the honey-sweet voice out of our mouths. Every man has gone away delighted with it, and knowing more than he did. Because we know everything — everything the Greeks and the Trojans suffered on the wide plain of Troy, by the will of the gods. And we know everything that happens on the fruitful earth.*

That is what they sang, in beautiful voices, and my heart wanted to listen. And I made signs with my eyebrows to my companions to untie me. They fell forward on the oars and rowed; and Perimedes and Eurylochus got up at once and tied me with more ropes, and pulled them tighter.

And when they had rowed past, and we could no longer hear the Sirens' voices or their song, my trusty companions took the wax out of their ears and untied me.[^2]

---

Odysseus could have stopped his own ears, of course. There was wax enough, and nothing in Circe's instructions obliged him to listen; she offered it as an option, and it is the option nobody sensible would take. So he deafened every man aboard and had himself made helpless instead, and arranged to be the only person in the world who has heard that song and lived — and, as it happens, the only man on that ship who would ever be in a position to tell anybody about it. Which he is doing, at this moment, at dinner.

---

And the moment we had left that island behind, I saw smoke, and a great wave, and I heard a booming. And the oars flew out of their hands in terror, and all the oars splashed down along the current, and the ship stopped dead where she was.

And I went along the ship and stood over every man and heartened him with soft words. *Friends. We are not exactly ignorant of trouble. This is no worse than when the Cyclops shut us in his hollow cave by main force. Even out of that we got away, by my courage and my planning and my wits; and I think we shall remember this one too, one day. So come — hit the deep surf of the sea with your oars, and see whether Zeus will let us get out from under our destruction. And you, steersman — here are your orders, and keep them in your mind. Hold the ship out beyond that smoke and that surf, and hug the cliff on the other side, and do not let her drift off the other way without your noticing, or you will throw us all into disaster.*

And they did as I said, quickly.

I did not say a word to them about Scylla — a trouble there was no dealing with — in case my companions were frightened and stopped rowing and huddled together down inside the ship. And at that point I let Circe's grim instructions go clean out of my head, when she told me not to arm at all. I put my famous armour on, and took two long spears in my hands, and went up onto the fore-deck, because that was where I expected rocky Scylla to appear from first. And I could not make her out anywhere. My eyes got tired with staring, everywhere, up at that misty rock.

So we sailed up the narrow strait, and we were crying: Scylla on one side, and on the other divine Charybdis sucking the salt sea water down horribly. When she threw it up, she seethed and bubbled all over in a churning mess, like a cauldron on a great fire, and the spray flew high overhead and fell on the tops of both cliffs. And when she sucked the salt water down, the whole inside of her showed, churning, and the rock roared terribly all round, and the sea floor showed underneath, black with sand. And a green fear took hold of my men.

We were looking at *her*, frightened of dying — and Scylla took six of my companions out of the hollow ship, the six best of them in strength and in their hands.

I turned my eyes back to the fast ship, looking for my men, and I saw their feet and their hands already above me as they were lifted up. And they screamed and called out to me by name, that one last time, with their hearts in agony.

The way a fisherman on a jutting rock, with a long pole, throws his bait down as a trap for the little fishes, and when he catches one he pulls it up and out and throws it on the shore, writhing: that is how they were pulled up towards the rocks, writhing. And there in her doorway she ate them, while they screamed and stretched their hands out towards me, in the most terrible struggle of all.

That was the most pitiful thing my eyes ever saw, out of everything I suffered searching out the ways of the sea.

---

That is the only place in the poem where somebody is told the exact price of a course of action in advance and pays it deliberately. Circe does not hint and she does not speak in riddles: one way loses the whole ship, the other loses six men, and six is better than all. He does the arithmetic and takes the six. It is the correct decision, and a commander who refused it would kill everybody — but it is worth saying plainly that this is what command is, in a way the older poem never quite has to face, since in the *Iliad* men die in battle facing an enemy, and here six men who have done nothing are given away in advance on a goddess's recommendation.

And they are not told. He says so, and gives his reason, and the reason is professional and almost certainly right: had they known there was something overhead that could not be fought, they would have dropped the oars, and then the ship would have drifted, and Charybdis would have had all of them. Nevertheless he stood in the bows in full armour with two spears, watching for her, having been told plainly that arming was useless and would only cost him a second six. He did it anyway; he could not help it. So the picture to carry out of this book is of a man in armour at the front of a ship, staring up at a cliff for something he has been assured he cannot fight, while behind him the men he did not warn pull at their oars in ignorance and six of them go up off the deck like fish on a line.

---

So we got clear of the rocks and of Charybdis and of Scylla; and then we came to the island of the god, where the fine broad-browed cattle of the Sun were, and his many fat flocks. And while I was still out at sea in the black ship I could hear the lowing of cattle being penned, and sheep bleating. And into my mind came the words of the blind prophet Teiresias, and of Circe, who had both told me very strictly to keep away from that island.

So I spoke to my companions, sick at heart, and told them both prophecies and named both sources, and said: drive the black ship past the island.

And their spirits broke in them. And Eurylochus answered me straight away, and there was hate in it.

*You are a hard man, Odysseus. There is more strength in you than in other men and your limbs never get tired. You must be made entirely of iron, if you will not let your companions — worn out with hard work and lack of sleep — set foot on dry land, where we might get a decent supper ready on an island in the sea. Instead you tell us to go blundering off through the fast-coming night, driven away from the island over the misty water.*

*It is out of the night that the hard winds come, the ones that break ships up. How is any man to get out from under a sheer destruction, if a squall comes suddenly out of the South Wind or the blustering West — the ones that most often smash a ship apart, whatever the gods who rule us may want? No. Let us give way to the black night now and get our supper, and stay beside the fast ship. In the morning we will go aboard and put out into the wide sea.*

That is the best speech Eurylochus ever makes and nearly all of it is true. The men are exhausted; they have just watched six friends lifted off the deck and eaten; night is coming; and everything he says about the weather is not merely reasonable but professionally right. He is a seaman making a seaman's case. He is also entirely wrong, for the oldest of reasons: he does not know what his captain knows. He has not stood beside a pit in the dark and heard a dead prophet name that island. He is arguing about the weather with a man who is not arguing about the weather.

And all the rest of them agreed with him. And then I knew that some god was arranging trouble for us.

*Eurylochus, you force my hand; I am one man against all of you. Very well. But all of you swear me a great oath: that if we find a herd of cattle or a big flock of sheep, no man will kill a single cow or a single sheep out of the wicked folly of his heart. Sit quietly and eat the food that immortal Circe gave us.*

And they swore it, exactly as I told them to. And we moored the well-built ship in a hollow harbour beside a spring of sweet water, and my companions went ashore and made supper skilfully. And when they had put away all desire for food and drink, they began to weep, remembering the companions Scylla had taken and eaten. And a sweet sleep came over them while they were crying.

---

Then in the third watch of the night Zeus got up a screaming wind and an extraordinary storm, and hid the land and the sea alike in cloud, and night came rushing down out of the sky. And when Dawn came up we hauled the ship in and made her fast in a hollow cave, where the nymphs had their dancing floors and their seats. And I called them all together and said: friends, there is food and drink in the fast ship; let us keep our hands off these cattle, in case we come to harm; these belong to a dreadful god, the Sun, who sees everything and hears everything.

And their proud hearts agreed.

And then the South Wind blew for a whole month, and no other wind came at all, except the East and the South. Now as long as my men had food and red wine, they kept their hands off the cattle, because they wanted to live. But when all the provisions in the ship were used up, they had to go roving after game, whatever they could get: fish, and birds, anything that came to their hands, with bent hooks — because hunger was wearing away their stomachs.

And I went up the island to pray to the gods, hoping one of them would show me a way home. And when I had got away from my companions I washed my hands somewhere out of the wind and prayed to all the gods who hold Olympus.

And they poured a sweet sleep over my eyelids.

And Eurylochus began to put an evil plan to my companions.

*Listen to me, my companions, for all the bad things you have suffered. Every kind of death is hateful to us miserable men. But dying of hunger, and meeting your fate that way, is the most pitiful of all. Come on. Let us drive off the best of the Sun's cattle and sacrifice to the immortals who hold the wide sky. And if we ever get back to Ithaca, our own country, we will build a rich temple to the Sun straight away and put a great many fine offerings in it. And if he is angry about his straight-horned cattle, and wants to wreck our ship, and the other gods agree with him — then I would rather lose my life all at once, with one gulp at the wave, than be squeezed slowly to death on a desert island.*

And all the rest of them agreed with him. And at once they drove off the best of the Sun's cattle from close by. And they stood round them and prayed to the gods, pulling the soft leaves off a tall oak, because they had no white barley on the ship. And when they had prayed and cut the throats and flayed them, they cut out the thigh pieces and covered them with a double fold of fat and laid raw meat on top; and they had no wine to pour over the burning offerings, so they poured water instead, and roasted all the entrails.

And at that moment the sweet sleep went off my eyelids, and I went down to the fast ship and the shore of the sea. And when I was close to the ship the warm smell of the fat came round me, and I cried out to the immortal gods: *Father Zeus, and the rest of you blessed gods who live forever — you lulled me into a pitiless sleep, to destroy me. And my companions, left to themselves, have planned a monstrous thing.*

---

There is the pattern this poem has been assembling since the beach at Ismarus, complete at last. He warned them, in full, with both sources named. He lost the argument. He took the only step left and made them swear an oath, and they swore it and meant it. And then the wind blew for a month, and the food ran out, and he was up a hill asleep.

---

And Lampetie went as a messenger to the Sun to tell him we had killed his cattle. And he spoke among the immortals, furious.

*Father Zeus, and the rest of you blessed gods — take vengeance on the companions of Odysseus, who have insolently killed my cattle, the cattle I always took pleasure in, going up into the starry sky and turning back down again to earth. If they do not pay me a proper compensation, I shall go down into the house of Hades and shine among the dead.*

*Sun — go on shining among the immortals and among mortal men on the grain-giving earth. I shall very soon strike their fast ship with a bright thunderbolt and smash it to pieces in the middle of the wine-dark sea.*

(I had that from Calypso of the beautiful hair. She told me she had heard it herself from Hermes the messenger.)[^3]

And when I got down to the ship I rebuked them, going from one to another. And we could find no remedy: the cattle were already dead. And then the gods began showing us portents. The hides crawled. The meat on the spits bellowed, both the roasted and the raw. And there was a noise like the voice of cattle.

For six days my trusty companions feasted on the best of the Sun's cattle. And when Zeus brought on the seventh day, then the wind stopped raging in a storm, and we went aboard at once and put out into the wide sea, and set up the mast and hoisted the white sail.

---

But when we had left that island behind, and no other land showed, nothing but sky and sea, then Zeus set a black cloud over the hollow ship, and the sea went dark underneath it.

She did not run for very long after that. The West Wind came at once, shrieking, blowing in a great tempest, and the blast of it snapped both the forestays of the mast; and the mast fell backwards, and all the gear spilled down into the bilge; and the mast caught the steersman on the head in the stern of the ship and smashed every bone in his skull together, and he went down off the deck like a diver, and his proud spirit left his bones.

And Zeus thundered at the same moment and threw his bolt into the ship. And she shuddered from end to end, struck by the bolt of Zeus, and filled with the smell of sulphur; and my men fell out of her. They were carried on the waves round the black ship like sea-crows, and the god took away their homecoming.

And I kept going up and down the ship until the sea tore the sides away from the keel, and the wave carried the bare hull along. And it broke the mast off level with the keel — and the backstay had been thrown over it, a rope made of oxhide — and with that I lashed the keel and the mast together, and sat on the two of them, and was carried along by the ruinous winds.

Then the West Wind stopped raging. And the South Wind came on quickly, bringing pain to my heart, because it meant I would have to measure out the road back to deadly Charybdis all over again. All night I was carried, and at sunrise I came to Scylla's cliff and to terrible Charybdis. And she was sucking the salt sea water down.

And I sprang up towards the tall fig tree and got hold of it and hung on like a bat. There was nowhere I could get a footing or climb up: the roots were far below and the branches were out of reach above, long and heavy, and they overshadowed Charybdis. And I held on grimly and waited for her to spit the mast and the keel up again. And they came at last, to my great relief. At the hour when a man gets up from the assembly and goes to his supper — a man who has been settling a great many quarrels among young men who want a judgement — at that hour the timbers came out of Charybdis.

And I let go with my hands and feet and dropped, and came down with a crash in the water beside those long timbers; and I got up on them and rowed along with my hands. And the father of gods and men did not let Scylla catch sight of me. Otherwise I would not have got out from under a sheer destruction.

Nine days I was carried, and on the tenth night the gods brought me to Ogygia, where Calypso lives, who took me in and looked after me.

But why should I tell you all this? I told it to you and your good wife yesterday, in this house, and I hate telling a story twice when it has already been told properly.

---

And he stops there, which is a very fine last line: not a flourish, not a summary and certainly not a moral, but a small piece of good manners. *You have heard the rest. It would be rude to repeat myself.* It closes the frame, too. From here to the end there is no more first person and no more monsters. The next time we see him he will be asleep in the bottom of somebody else's boat, and when he wakes he will be on a beach he does not recognise, in his own country, and he will start lying again for quite different reasons.

I shall take it back from here.

## NOTES TO BOOK TWELVE

[^1]: **The Argo, and a poem that is not this one.** Circe drops the name of another ship and another voyage as though everybody knew all about it, which they did — the story of Jason and the golden fleece was old and famous when this poem was young. Notice how the reference is made: not *there was a ship called Argo*, but *the Argo that everybody sings about*, which is one poet acknowledging a rival hit. It is the clearest single sign that the two poems we have are survivors from a whole crowded literature. There were epics about Thebes, about the war before the war at Troy, about the homecomings of all the other Greek captains, about Heracles, about the Argo; there were men who could sing every one of them; and all of it is gone, except the odd line quoted by a later writer, and except for moments like this, where one poem waves at another across the water as it goes past.

[^2]: **The Sirens, and what they are not.** Homer never describes them: not their faces, not their bodies, not their number beyond Circe's passing *the two Sirens* — nothing but a meadow, a heap of rotting men, and a voice. The bird-bodies come from Greek vase-painting and funerary sculpture; the fish-tails come from a medieval muddle with mermaids in the north; and the seductress with the comb and the mirror is largely the nineteenth century, which had its own reasons for wanting the danger at sea to be a woman. What the poem does give, and gives precisely, is the content of the song, and it is a promise of knowledge: we know what happened at Troy, and we know everything that happens on the earth. That is what is written on the hook. The bones in that meadow belong to men who stopped rowing because they wanted to be told something.

[^3]: **How he knows what Zeus said.** This is one of my favourite lines in the poem and it is nearly always skipped. Odysseus has just reported a private conversation on Olympus between the Sun and the king of the gods — and he stops dead, mid-story, to tell his audience where he got it. Calypso told him; and she said Hermes had told her. Think what that means: somewhere on that island, in the middle of those seven years, a goddess and a man sat down and she passed on a piece of divine gossip about the day his crew died, which she had from the messenger of the gods, and he has carried it ever since and is retailing it now at a dinner party. The poem makes nothing of it. It simply notes the chain of custody, the way a careful witness does — and it is the only place in four books where the teller stops to explain how he came by something he could not have seen. It is exactly the sort of thing an honest man does, and exactly the sort of thing a very good liar does, and there is no way on earth to tell which from the outside.

[^4]: **The cattle of the Sun, and a promise kept.** Turn back to the first page of Book One and look at what the poem chose to say about the crew in its opening breath: that they died through their own recklessness, the fools, because they ate the cattle of the Sun, and the Sun took away their day of homecoming. That is the only specific event in the entire twelve-year voyage that the poem announces in advance. Not the Cyclops, not the witch, not the descent to the dead. This. Which tells you what the poem thinks the story of the crew actually is; and it has now delivered exactly what it promised, eleven books later, with the reasons attached — they were starving, they had sworn an oath, their captain was asleep on a hillside, and the man who talked them into it made a speech about how a quick death is better than a slow one.

[^5]: **Between Scylla and Charybdis.** The phrase has been in English for centuries and most people who use it do not know it means something quite specific: not merely two dangers, but two dangers of *unequal and known* cost, where the whole art lies in choosing the smaller deliberately and paying it. The rock will certainly take six men; the whirlpool may take everybody. Circe's advice is not to be brave and not to be clever — it is to steer close to the thing that will definitely maim you in order to stay clear of the thing that might destroy you altogether. People have been trying to put the pair on a map since antiquity, the usual candidate being the Strait of Messina, which has a real tidal race in it; I would not lean on it. Charting this poem is a very old amusement that has produced a large library and no agreement whatever, and the poem is doing something more interesting than describing a coastline. It is describing a decision, of a kind anybody who has had to make one will recognise, and it has been lending people the words for it ever since.

*Book Thirteen: In Which a Man Is Carried Home Asleep, and Does Not Know Where He Is.*
