# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK NINE
### In Which the Stranger Gives His Name at Last, and Tells About a Cave

---

Before he answers, I am going to get out of the way, because for the next four books the story is not mine to tell.

He is about to say his own name aloud for the first time in this poem, and then he is going to keep talking until the end of Book Twelve — the giant, the witch, the singers on the rock, the country of the dead, all of it. It is the most famous stretch of storytelling in Europe, and almost none of it happens where people think it does. It happens in a dining hall on Scheria, after supper, in the mouth of a shipwrecked man describing himself to a king whose ships he needs. There are no witnesses left; every man who sailed with him is dead. His audience are a people who packed up and emigrated because they could not go on living next to the Cyclopes. And there is a ship in the harbour outside that has not sailed yet.

So when the prose says *I* from here on, it is Odysseus and not me. Where a line breaks across the page like the one below, that will be me coming back for a moment, and then going away again.

---

Lord Alcinous, most distinguished of all your people — it is a fine thing to listen to a singer like this one, with a voice like a god's. I do not think there is any better end than this: good cheer through a whole people, and men sitting in rows down a hall listening to a singer, and the tables beside them full of bread and meat, and a steward drawing wine out of the bowl and carrying it round and filling the cups. That seems to me about the most beautiful thing there is.

But your heart has turned to asking me about my miseries, so that I shall cry even harder than I have been. What shall I tell you first, then? What last? The gods have given me a great many.

My name first, so that you know it, and so that if I get out from under the day of my death I shall be your guest-friend afterwards, even though I live a long way off.

I am Odysseus, the son of Laertes. Men know me for every kind of trick, and my fame has gone up to the sky.[^1]

I live in Ithaca, where you can see for miles. There is a mountain on it, Neriton, with the leaves shaking on it, and it stands out. There are islands round about, close in to each other — Dulichium, and Same, and wooded Zacynthus. Mine lies low, and furthest out in the sea towards the dark; the others lie away towards the dawn and the sun. It is rough country, but it raises good young men, and I have never seen anything sweeter than a man's own land.

Calypso kept me in her hollow cave, a goddess, and she wanted me for her husband. Circe kept me in her house on Aeaea, and she wanted the same. Neither of them ever persuaded my heart. There is nothing sweeter than your own country and your own parents — not if you live in a rich house somewhere else, in a foreign country, away from them.

Very well. I shall tell you about the miserable voyage home that Zeus gave me out of Troy.

---

The wind carried me from Troy to the Cicones, at Ismarus. I sacked the town and killed the men. We took their wives out of it, and a great deal of property, and divided it up so that nobody went short of a fair share.

Then I told my men to run for it, quickly. The fools would not listen. There was a great deal of wine drunk, and they slaughtered a great many sheep on the beach, and cattle with their shambling walk and their bent horns.

And meanwhile the Cicones went and called the other Cicones, their neighbours inland, who were more numerous and better men, and knew how to fight from a chariot and on foot as well. They came at first light, as thick as the leaves and the flowers come in spring, and a bad fate from Zeus stood beside us, and we suffered a great deal. They set the battle up beside the ships and we threw our bronze spears at each other; and all morning, while the sacred day was still growing, we held them off, though there were more of them. But when the sun moved round to the hour when a man unyokes his oxen, then the Cicones broke us, and the Greeks gave way.

Six armed men off each ship were killed. The rest of us got away, and got out from under our death.

---

That is the first adventure, and it is the one most people have never heard of, and I should not like you to go past it. Before there is a single monster in this poem the hero stops at a town on the way home from a war that is already over and takes it — and says so himself, in his opening sentence, with no provocation offered and none expected. *We took their wives out of it and divided them up so that nobody went short.* One book ago the poem compared this man, at length and with no comment whatever, to a woman being beaten away from her husband's body and driven off into slavery. Here is the same sentence from the other side, in his own mouth, at dinner, as background. He is not confessing. It has not occurred to him that there is anything to confess, and that is precisely why it is worth a moment of your time.

The other thing in it is the shape of the disaster, which you will meet again: he told them to go, and they would not, and it cost seventy-two men. Remember the form of words, because he is scrupulous about it, and there is a place further on where he cannot use it.

---

Zeus who gathers the clouds set the North Wind on us, an enormous storm, and covered the land and the sea alike with cloud, and night came rushing down out of the sky. The ships were driven sideways, and the sails torn into three and four pieces by the force of it. We got them down and rowed hard for the shore, and lay there two nights and two days, eating our hearts out with exhaustion and pain.

On the third morning we stepped the masts and hoisted the white sails and sat still, and the wind and the steersmen kept us straight; and I would have got home to my own country without a scratch. But as I was rounding Cape Malea the swell and the current and the North Wind pushed me off and drove me past Cythera.

For nine days after that I was carried over the fish-filled sea by ruinous winds. On the tenth we came ashore in the country of the Lotus-eaters, who eat a flowering food. We went ashore and drew water, and my men had a meal beside the fast ships; and when we had had food and drink I sent some of them off to find out what sort of men lived there and ate the bread of that country — two, and a third as a herald with them.

They went, and fell in with the Lotus-eaters. And the Lotus-eaters did not plan any harm to my men at all. They gave them lotus to taste.

And whoever ate that honey-sweet fruit no longer wanted to bring back a report, or to come back at all. They wanted to stay there among the Lotus-eaters, chewing the lotus, and forget the way home.

I brought them back to the ships crying, and I had to use force. I dragged them under the benches and tied them up, and told the rest of my trusty companions to get aboard the fast ships in a hurry, in case one of them ate the lotus and forgot about going home. They went aboard at once and sat down at the oars and struck the grey water with the blades.

---

From there we sailed on with our hearts full of trouble, and came to the country of the Cyclopes — a violent and lawless people.

They put nothing in with their own hands and they plough nothing. They trust the gods for it, and everything comes up for them unsown and unploughed: wheat, and barley, and vines with great heavy clusters that make wine, and the rain of Zeus swells it all. They have no assemblies for making decisions, and no laws. They live on the tops of the high mountains in hollow caves, and each one lays down the rules for his own wives and children, and none of them takes any notice of the others.[^2]

There is a wooded island lying across the mouth of their harbour — neither close in nor far out — and it is full of wild goats without number, because no human foot disturbs them there. No hunters go through it, the sort who wear themselves out in the woods going over the tops of the mountains. It is not grazed by flocks and it is not ploughed; it goes unsown and unbroken all its days, empty of men, and it feeds bleating goats.

Because the Cyclopes have no ships with painted cheeks, and there are no shipwrights among them who might build them well-benched vessels to get things done — going across to other men's towns, the way people constantly do go over the sea to one another. Men like that would have made this island a good place to live in.

It is not a bad island at all. It would bear anything in season. There are soft watered meadows along the shore of the grey sea; vines would never fail there; the ploughing is level; they could cut a deep crop off it every summer, because the soil underneath is rich. And there is a harbour where you need no moorings — no anchor stones, no stern cables. You run in and lie there as long as your sailors feel like it and until the wind gets up. And at the head of the harbour bright water runs out from under a cave, and poplars grow round it.

That is where we sailed in. Some god guided us through a black night; there was nothing to see. The fog lay thick round the ships and the moon did not show in the sky, but was shut up in cloud. Not one of us saw that island with his eyes, and we never saw the long waves rolling in to the beach either, until we had run the ships aground. We took down the sails and went out onto the shore of the sea, and slept there, and waited for the bright morning.

---

That, if you will forgive me for pointing at it, is a colonist talking. Water supply, anchorage, soil, level ploughing, two crops off it and vines that would never fail; and nobody living there, and the people across the water with no ships to do anything about it. Read his description of the Cyclopes again and it turns out to be the same passage: no hulls, no shipwrights, no assemblies, no laws, no farming, and every household answering to nobody. That is a checklist, and every item on it is something he has. This poem was made in a century when Greeks were pouring out across the Mediterranean in exactly such ships, looking at exactly such coasts, and finding people on them who did not farm as Greeks farmed — and were therefore not, in the settlers' judgement, really using the place. I shall not tell you what to make of it. I only say that when a man in a story remarks that the natives are savages who have never made anything of a fine piece of land, you are hearing a very old sentence, and it is worth hearing it as one.

---

When the dawn came up we walked round the island and admired it, and the nymphs put the mountain goats up for us so that my men could eat. We got our curved bows out of the ships, and our long boar-spears, and split into three parties and started shooting, and the god gave us a satisfying bag: twelve ships were with me, and nine goats fell to each of them, and for me alone they cut out ten.

So all day until sunset we sat and feasted on meat past counting and sweet wine. The red wine had not run out yet; there was still plenty, because we had all drawn off a great deal in jars when we took the sacred citadel of the Cicones. And we looked across at the country of the Cyclopes, which was very close, and saw smoke, and heard voices, and the noise of sheep and goats. And when the sun went down and the darkness came, we lay down to sleep on the beach.

In the morning I called my men together. "The rest of you stay here, my trusty companions. I am going over with my own ship and my own crew to find out about these people — whether they are violent and wild and unjust, or whether they take strangers in, and have god-fearing minds."

We rowed across, and it was not far, and right at the edge of the land we saw a cave, high up, with laurels round the mouth of it. A great many flocks were penned there for the night, sheep and goats, and round it there was a yard built high with quarried stone and tall pines and oaks with their high crowns. A giant of a man slept in there. He pastured his flocks alone, and a long way off, and he did not go near anybody else; he kept apart, and his mind was lawless. He was a monstrous piece of work — not like a man who eats bread, but like a wooded peak standing up by itself in a range of high mountains, away from the others.

I told the rest of my companions to stay and guard the ship, and I picked out the twelve best of them and went. And I took a goatskin of dark sweet wine with me, which Maron had given me — the son of Euanthes, the priest of Apollo (god of prophecy, and of the bow) who protects Ismarus — because we had protected him and his wife and his child, out of respect, since he lived in the wooded grove of Apollo. He gave me splendid presents: seven talents of well-worked gold, and a mixing bowl of solid silver, and then wine, twelve jars of it, all of it neat, a drink for gods. Not one of the servants in his house knew about that wine, and not one of the maids — only himself and his wife and one housekeeper. And whenever they drank it he would pour one cup of it into twenty measures of water, and a smell would come up off the bowl that was sweet past describing; and it was not a time when anybody wanted to hold back.[^3]

I filled a big skin with that and took it, and food in a bag as well. Because straight away, in my proud heart, I had a feeling that a man was going to come at us — a great brute, clothed in enormous strength, who knew nothing about justice or law.

---

We got to the cave quickly, and he was not in it; he was out pasturing his fat flocks. So we went in and looked round at everything. There were crates loaded with cheeses, and the pens were crowded with lambs and kids, each lot penned separately: the spring ones in one place, the summer ones in another, and the newborn in a third. And there were vessels swimming with whey, pails and bowls, all well made, and he did his milking into them.

And straight away my men started begging me to take some of the cheeses and go — to come back and drive the kids and the lambs out of the pens to the fast ship and sail off over the salt water.

And I would not listen to them. It would have been very much better if I had. But I wanted to see him, and I wanted to see whether he would give me guest-gifts.

He was not going to turn out delightful to my companions when he appeared.

So we lit a fire and made an offering, and helped ourselves to the cheeses and ate, and sat in the cave waiting until he came back with his flock. He came carrying an enormous weight of dry wood to burn at his supper, and threw it down inside the cave with a crash; and we bolted in terror to the deepest corner of it. He drove his fat flocks into the wide cave — all the ones he milked; the males, the rams and the he-goats, he left outside in the deep yard. Then he lifted a great doorstone and set it in place. It was huge. Two and twenty good four-wheeled wagons could not have shifted it off the ground. That was the size of the rock he put in his doorway.

Then he sat down and milked the ewes and the bleating goats, all of it in order, and put the young under each one. And half the white milk he curdled and gathered up and stored in wicker baskets; and the other half he stood in vessels, so that he could take it and drink it at his supper.

And when he had got through all his work, he lit his fire — and saw us.

"Strangers, who are you? Where do you sail from over the watery ways? Are you here on business, or do you wander about at random, like pirates, who wander about risking their own lives and bringing trouble to other people?"

Our hearts broke in us at that: we were terrified by the deep voice of him and by the size of him. But even so I answered him.

"We are Greeks. We are coming from Troy, and all sorts of winds over the great gulf of the sea have driven us off course — making for home, we came another way, by another road. That is how Zeus wanted it, I suppose. We say we are men of Agamemnon's army, whose fame is the greatest under heaven just now, so great a city did he sack and so many people did he destroy.

"And we have come to your knees, hoping you will give us a guest's welcome, or make us some other present, which is the right of strangers. Respect the gods, sir. We are your suppliants. Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers — Zeus of the Strangers, who walks in beside honourable travellers."

And he answered me straight out of a pitiless heart.

"You are a fool, stranger, or you have come a very long way, telling me to be afraid of the gods and keep clear of them. The Cyclopes do not care about Zeus who holds the storm cloud, or about any of the blessed gods, because we are a great deal stronger than they are. I would not spare you or your companions to avoid making an enemy of Zeus. Not unless my own heart told me to.

"But tell me — where did you leave your ship when you came? Somewhere far along the coast, or close by? I should like to know."

He was testing me. He did not get anywhere; I know too much. And I answered him with a crooked answer. "Poseidon (god of the sea) smashed my ship. He drove it against the rocks at the edge of your country and brought it up against a headland, and the wind carried it in off the sea. I got out from under a sheer destruction, with these men here."

He did not answer me a word. He sprang up and put his hands on my companions, and grabbed two of them together and knocked them against the ground like puppies, and their brains ran out and wetted the earth. He cut them up limb from limb and made his supper of them. He ate like a lion bred in the mountains, and left nothing: the guts, the flesh, the bones with the marrow in them.

We cried, and lifted our hands up to Zeus, watching this appalling thing done, and there was nothing we could do; helplessness held our hearts.

And when the Cyclops had filled his enormous belly with human meat and milk drunk neat on top of it, he lay down inside the cave, stretched out among his sheep. And I made a plan in my great heart to go up close to him and draw my sharp sword off my thigh and stab him in the chest, where the midriff holds the liver, feeling for the place with my hand. And another thought held me back. We would have died there too, and died on the spot, because there was no way our hands could have pushed that enormous stone off the high doorway.

So we waited, groaning, for the bright morning.

---

When Dawn came up he lit his fire and milked his fine flocks, all of it in order, and put the young under each one; and when he had got through all his work he grabbed two more of my men and made his meal. And when he had eaten he drove his fat flocks out of the cave, lifting the great doorstone easily — and then he put it back, the way a man puts the lid back on a quiver. And with a great deal of whistling he turned his flocks up towards the mountain, and left me there, working out how to do him harm.

And this is the plan that seemed best to my heart.

There was a great club of the Cyclops's lying beside a pen: green olive wood, which he had cut so that he could carry it when it had dried out. Looking at it, we thought it about the size of the mast of a black ship, a twenty-oared merchantman, broad in the beam, that crosses the great gulf of the sea. That was its length, and that was its thickness. I stood over it and cut a fathom's length off it and handed it to my companions and told them to smooth it down. They made it even, and I stood over it and sharpened the end, and then took it and hardened it in the blazing fire. And I hid it carefully under the dung, of which there was a great deal spread about the cave.

And I told the others to draw lots, to see which of them would have to take the risk with me — to lift that stake and grind it into his eye when sweet sleep came over him. And the lot fell on the four I would have picked myself, and I counted as the fifth with them.

He came back in the evening with his fine-fleeced flock, and this time he drove all of them into the wide cave and left nothing in the deep yard outside. Either he had begun to suspect something, or a god told him to. Then he lifted the great doorstone and set it in place, and sat down and milked, all of it in order, and put the young under each one. And when he had got through all his work he grabbed two more men and made his supper.

And then I stood close to him with an ivy-wood bowl of the dark wine in my hands.

"Cyclops — here. Have some wine, now you have eaten human meat, so that you know what sort of drink our ship was carrying. I was bringing it to you as an offering, in case you might pity me and send me home. But you are past all bearing. You cruel thing — how is any man in the world ever going to come to you after this? You have not behaved rightly."

He took it and drank it, and he was terribly pleased with the sweet drink, and asked for another. "Give me more, generously, and tell me your name — now, at once — so that I can give you a guest-gift, one you will be glad to have. The grain-giving earth grows wine of great clusters for the Cyclopes too, and the rain of Zeus swells it. But this is a runnel of ambrosia and nectar."

So I handed him the flaming wine again. Three times I brought it and gave it to him, and three times he drank it down, the fool. And when the wine had got round the Cyclops's wits, I spoke to him with honeyed words.

"Cyclops, you asked me my famous name. I shall tell you. And you give me the guest-gift you promised me.

"Nobody is my name. Nobody is what they call me — my mother, and my father, and all my companions."

And he answered me out of a pitiless heart. "Nobody I shall eat last, after all his companions. The others first. That is your guest-gift."[^4]

---

He went over backwards and lay with his thick neck bent to one side, and sleep that beats everything took hold of him; and the wine came up out of his throat with gobbets of human flesh in it, and he vomited in his drunken sleep.

And I drove the stake in under the deep ashes until it should get hot, and talked to all my men and put heart into them, so that nobody would lose his nerve and back out. And when the olive stake was on the point of catching fire, green as it was, and was glowing terribly, I brought it up out of the fire; and my companions stood round me, and some god breathed a great courage into us.

They took the olive stake, sharp at the end, and drove it into his eye. And I leaned on it from above and spun it.

The way a man bores a ship's timber with an auger, and the men below him keep it turning with a strap, holding the two ends, and it runs round and round and does not stop: that is how we held the fire-pointed stake in his eye and spun it, and the blood ran out round the hot thing. The flame singed his eyelids and his brow all round as the eyeball burned, and the roots of it crackled in the fire.

The way a smith plunges a great axe or an adze into cold water to temper it, and it screams aloud — because that is what gives iron its strength — so his eye hissed round the olive stake.

He screamed horribly and the rock rang with it, and we bolted away in terror. And he pulled the stake out of his eye, thick with blood, and threw it away from him, raving; and he shouted aloud to the Cyclopes who lived round about in caves on the windy heights.

They heard him and came from all directions and stood round the cave and asked him what was the matter. "What is wrong with you, Polyphemus? Why are you shouting like this through the immortal night and keeping us all awake? No mortal man is driving your flocks off against your will, surely? Nobody is killing you, surely, by force or by trickery?"

And out of the cave the mighty Polyphemus answered them: "My friends — Nobody is killing me. By trickery. Not by force."

And they answered him with words that went straight out of them and away. "Well, if nobody is doing you violence, and you are on your own, there is no escaping a sickness that comes from great Zeus. Say a prayer to your father, the lord Poseidon."

And off they went. And my own heart laughed inside me, that my name and my faultless scheme had taken them all in.

---

The Cyclops, groaning and in agony, felt about with his hands and took the stone away from the doorway, and sat down in the entrance himself with his arms spread wide, to catch anybody who tried to go out among the sheep. That is evidently how much of a fool he thought I was.

And I was planning how it might turn out best — how I might find some way out of death for my companions and for myself. I wove every kind of trick and scheme, as a man does when his life is at stake, because a great evil was very near.

And this is the plan that seemed best. There were rams there, well fed, thick in the fleece, big handsome animals with wool as dark as violets. I bound these together, silently, with the twisted withies the monster used to sleep on: three at a time, and the middle one of each three carried a man, and the two on the outside walked alongside and covered him. Three sheep to each man. And for myself there was a ram, far the best of the whole flock. I got hold of him by the back and curled myself up under his shaggy belly and lay there, and gripped that marvellous fleece with my hands, twisted into it, and held on with a steady heart.

And so, groaning, we waited for the bright morning.

When Dawn came up the males rushed out to pasture, and the females bleated round the pens unmilked, because their udders were bursting. And their master, worn out with the pain of it, felt along the backs of all the sheep as they stood up in front of him — and never noticed, the fool, that my men were tied underneath the chests of the woolly animals.

The last of the whole flock to go out was the ram, weighed down with his own fleece and with me, and I was thinking hard. And the mighty Polyphemus felt him and said:

"Ram, my old friend. Why are you going out of the cave last of the flock, like this? You have never before been left behind by the sheep. You were always far the first to graze the tender bloom of the grass, striding out; the first to reach the running rivers; and the first to want to come home to the fold in the evening. And now you are the last of them all.

"You must be grieving for your master's eye. A bad man blinded it, with his wretched companions, after breaking my wits down with wine. Nobody. And I do not say he has got out from under his death yet.

"If only you could feel with me, and had speech, and could tell me where he is dodging about away from my anger. Then his brains would be knocked out all over the floor of this cave, and my heart would get some relief from the trouble that worthless Nobody has brought on me."

And he let the ram go out through the door.

And when we had got a little way from the cave and the yard, I got out from under the ram first, and then untied my companions. And quickly, keeping turning to look behind us, we drove those long-striding animals, rich with fat, down to the ship. And our companions were very glad to see us, the men who had got away from death — and they started to cry for the others. But I would not let them cry. I stopped each man with my eyebrows, and told them to throw all that fine-fleeced stock aboard at speed and sail out on the salt water. They went aboard at once and sat down at the oars and struck the grey water with the blades.

---

And when I was as far off as a man's shout will carry, I called back to the Cyclops, jeering at him.

"Cyclops. So it was not some weakling's companions you were going to eat by main force in your hollow cave. Your own foul deeds were bound to come down on your own head, you brute — you did not shrink from eating guests in your own house. So Zeus has paid you, and the other gods with him."

That made him angrier still. He broke the top off a great hill and threw it, and it landed in front of the dark-prowed ship, and the sea heaved up under the stone, and the backwash off the deep water carried the ship in and drove it towards the land. I got hold of a long pole and shoved us off, and gave my men the nod to throw themselves on the oars; and they bent to it and rowed.

And when we were twice as far out as before, I started calling to the Cyclops again — and my men were all round me trying to stop me, one after another, with soft words. "You reckless man. Why do you want to provoke a wild creature like that? He threw something into the sea just now that drove the ship back to land, and we thought we were finished. If he hears one of us make a sound, he will smash our heads and the ship's timbers together with a jagged lump of rock, that is how far he throws."

They said that, and they did not persuade my great heart. I called out to him again with my anger up.

"Cyclops. If any mortal man ever asks you about the shameful blinding of your eye, tell him this: that Odysseus blinded it, the sacker of cities, the son of Laertes, whose home is in Ithaca."

---

That is the most expensive sentence anybody speaks in this poem.

Everything up to it has been faultless. The false name worked so well that the neighbours went home; the men came out under the bellies of the sheep; he is at sea, out of reach, with the wine and the cheeses and the flocks aboard, having beaten something forty times his size by pure invention and lost nobody in the escape. And then he turns round and hands the creature his name, his father's name and his address, with his own crew hanging off his arms begging him not to.

He is never stupid, and he is not being stupid here. He is doing something the Greeks understood far better than we do and had no word of reproach for: he is claiming the deed, because a story with nobody's name on it is not a story, and because a man who has spent his life being famous cannot bear to have been Nobody at the one moment it counted. In the older poem men die on purpose to have their names said afterwards; that is the whole economy of the thing, and every person in that Phaeacian hall grew up inside it. This poem, being the sequel, shows you the bill. He is ten years from home, and every man on those twelve ships is going to die, and it begins here, with a shout across the water that nobody asked him to make — and he tells the story himself, at dinner, to a king, and does not leave the bit out.

---

He groaned, and answered.

"So the old prophecies have come home. There was a prophet here, a good man and a tall one, Telemus the son of Eurymus, who was outstanding at prophecy and grew old among the Cyclopes as their prophet. He told me all of this would happen in time — that I would lose my sight at the hands of Odysseus.

"But I was always expecting a big handsome man to turn up here, with enormous strength in him. And now a little one, a nothing, a weakling, has put my eye out, after knocking me down with wine.

"Come here, Odysseus, and let me give you guest-gifts, and let me urge the famous Earthshaker (Poseidon) to send you on your way. I am his son, and he says he is my father. And he will cure me, if he chooses to, and nobody else will — none of the blessed gods and no mortal man."

"I only wish I could take the life and the soul out of you as surely as I know the Earthshaker will never cure that eye."

And then he prayed to the lord Poseidon, stretching his hands up towards the starry sky.

"Hear me, Poseidon, holder of the earth, with the dark hair. If I am really yours, and you say you are my father, then grant that Odysseus, the sacker of cities, the son of Laertes, whose home is in Ithaca, may never reach his home.

"And if it is laid down that he is to see his own people and come to his well-built house and his own country — then let him come late, and in a bad way, having lost all his companions, in someone else's ship. And let him find trouble in his house."

That was his prayer, and the god with the dark hair heard him.[^5]

And then the Cyclops picked up a much bigger stone and swung it and threw it with immeasurable force, and it fell just behind the dark-prowed ship, and only just missed the end of the steering oar. The sea heaved up under the stone, and the wave carried the ship forward and drove it on to the far shore.

---

We came back to the island where the rest of the ships were lying together, and our companions were sitting round them crying, waiting for us and expecting the worst. We ran her ashore and stepped out onto the beach, and got the Cyclops's flocks out of the hollow ship, and divided them up so that nobody went short of a fair share. But the ram — my well-greaved companions gave that to me alone, over and above the division, when the flocks were shared out.

And I sacrificed it on the beach to Zeus of the dark clouds, the son of Cronos, who rules over everybody, and burned the thigh pieces.

And he took no notice at all of my sacrifice. He was thinking about how every one of my well-benched ships and every one of my trusty companions might be destroyed.

So all that day until sunset we sat and feasted on meat past counting and sweet wine. And when the sun went down and the darkness came, we lay down and slept on the shore of the sea. And when the rosy-fingered Dawn came up I roused my companions and told them to get aboard and cast off the cables. They went aboard at once and sat down at the oars and struck the grey water with the blades.

And from there we sailed on, glad to be out from under our death, and grieving in our hearts for the dear companions we had lost.

## NOTES TO BOOK NINE

[^1]: **The name he finally gives.** Greek names carried their meanings on the surface, the way English surnames do — Baker, Smith, Fletcher — and everybody heard them. This one is built on a verb meaning to hate, to be furious with, to cause pain, and it runs in both directions at once: the man who is hated, and the man who deals out trouble. The Man of Wrath, roughly, or the Man Others Have It In For. It is not a compliment and was never meant as one, and there is a story about how he came by it which the poem is keeping until much later. — Which makes his self-introduction worth reading twice. He gives his name, and his father's, and then in the same breath tells a room full of strangers how famous it is. Eight books of anonymity, and the first thing he does with the name is polish it.

[^2]: **What "lawless" means here.** The Greek word is *themis* and its plural: not statutes, but the settled customs by which decisions get made and quarrels judged — what is done, what is not done, and who says so. The Cyclopes have none, and it is worth setting out the poem's indictment in full, because it amounts to a definition of civilisation by its absence: no assemblies where matters are decided in common; no customary law; no agriculture; no ships; no shipwrights; no traffic with other communities; and each male laying down the law inside his own cave and taking no interest whatever in anyone else's. — Notice what is *not* on that list. Nobody says they are stupid, and nobody says they eat people; Polyphemus turns out to eat people, but that is his own contribution, and the charge against his whole nation is that they do not farm, do not sail, and do not hold meetings. To this poem a household that answers to nobody is not free. It is the definition of a monster.

[^3]: **Maron's wine, and how the Greeks drank.** One cup to twenty of water, and still so strong that nobody could stop — an extreme ratio, quoted precisely to tell you this is no ordinary wine; the ordinary run was something nearer one to three or four. But the important thing for a modern reader is the practice itself. Greeks watered their wine as a matter of course, always, and the mixing bowl standing in the middle of every hall in this poem is there for exactly that purpose. Drinking it neat was what foreigners did, and barbarians, and men who came to a bad end. So when the Cyclops takes the bowl three times and swallows it undiluted, a Greek audience did not need to be told he was making a mistake; they had just watched him do something no civilised person at that dinner would have done.

[^4]: **"Nobody."** The most famous pun in European literature, and it does not survive the crossing into English, so here is what is actually happening. The name he gives is *Outis*, which is simply the Greek for "no one" — which is why the neighbours outside the cave go away again: they ask whether somebody is killing him, he shouts back the word for nobody, and they take it as an answer rather than as a name. That much comes across in translation. — What does not come across is the second turn of the screw. When those neighbours frame their question, the Greek idiom requires a different form of the negative, and the phrase that comes out — *mē tis*, "if nobody" — is indistinguishable in sound from *mētis*, the ordinary word for cunning, the practical intelligence that finds a way out. It is Odysseus's own defining quality, and the poem uses it of him constantly. So the Cyclopes stand outside the cave and unknowingly announce the exact truth: *cunning is killing you*. And a few lines later Odysseus tells us his heart laughed at how his name and his faultless scheme had taken them in — using, of course, the same word. It is a triple joke, set up and paid off across forty lines, and airtight in Greek. I can explain it to you. Nobody can translate it.

[^5]: **The curse, which is also the synopsis.** Count the terms of that prayer. Let him not get home at all — and if he must, then let him come *late*; let him come *in a bad way*; let him have *lost every one of his companions*; let him arrive *in a ship belonging to somebody else*; and let him *find trouble in his house*. Every one of those comes true exactly, including the ship, which is at this moment tied up in the harbour outside the hall where he is telling the story. The whole remaining shape of the poem sits in a blind man's prayer on a beach, and the man it was aimed at is reciting it aloud, from memory, to the people whose ship will fulfil the fourth clause. This is characteristic: the poem tells you what is going to happen with striking regularity, and it costs nothing, because knowing the destination has never yet spoiled the road.
