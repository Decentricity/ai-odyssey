# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK THIRTEEN
### In Which a Man Is Carried Home Asleep, and Does Not Know Where He Is

---

I have the story back, and I should say at once that everything is about to change. For twelve books we have been travelling, and if you have been keeping a map in your head it is by now a large and crowded one. From here there is no map at all. We arrive in one small place and stay in it for twelve books and never leave, and the whole of the rest of this poem happens on a rocky island you could walk across in a day, mostly indoors, and mostly among people talking to one another. The monsters are finished; there is not another one in the book. What takes their place is a farmhouse, a pigsty, a hall full of young men eating another man's dinner, and a woman upstairs who has been thinking for twenty years — and I will say plainly that I find the second half the greater achievement, because anybody with a good imagination can put a giant in a cave, and it takes something else to make you hold your breath over a husband and wife who both know more than they are saying.

---

They all sat in silence, held in the spell of it, through the shadowy hall. And Alcinous answered him:

"Odysseus — since you have come to my house with its bronze floor and its high roof, I do not think you will be driven off your course again, badly as you have suffered. And to every one of you here, who are always drinking the sparkling wine of the elders in my halls and listening to the singer, I say this. There are clothes already stored in the polished chest for the stranger, and worked gold, and all the other gifts the Phaeacian counsellors brought here. Come — let us each of us give him a great tripod and a cauldron as well; and then we will collect it back from the people afterwards, because it is a hard thing for one man to give freely without being repaid."

That pleased them all, and they went off home to bed. And when Dawn came up they hurried down to the ship carrying the bronze; and the king himself went about the ship and stowed everything under the benches with his own hands, so that none of it would get in the crew's way when they were pulling on the oars.

Then they went back to the house and made a feast, and Alcinous sacrificed an ox to Zeus; and they burned the thigh pieces and enjoyed a splendid dinner, and Demodocus sang among them, the singer whom the people honoured.

But Odysseus kept turning his head towards the blazing sun, willing it to set, because he was longing to be gone. The way a man longs for his supper when a pair of wine-dark oxen have been dragging the jointed plough through fallow ground all day for him, and the sun going down is a very welcome thing, so that he can go to his supper at last, and his knees ache as he walks — that is how welcome the setting of the sun was to Odysseus.

"Lord Alcinous, most distinguished of all your people. Pour your libations now and send me off unharmed; and my good wishes to all of you. Everything my heart wanted is done: my passage home, and gifts given in friendship. May the gods make them prosper for me. And may I find my faultless wife at home when I come, and everyone I love unhurt. And may all of you here make your wedded wives and your children glad, and may the gods grant you every kind of excellence, and may no trouble ever come near your people."

They all approved. And Odysseus stood up and put his two-handled cup into Arete's hands. "Fare well, queen, all your years, until old age comes, and death, which come to everybody. I am going now. May you have joy in this house of your children and your people, and of Alcinous the king."

And with that he stepped over the threshold. Heralds went with him down to the fast ship, and Arete sent her women too: one carrying a clean cloak and tunic, and one bringing the strong chest, and another with bread and red wine. And when they had got it all aboard, they spread a rug and a linen sheet for Odysseus on the deck at the stern of the hollow ship, so that he could sleep soundly. And he went aboard and lay down in silence.

They sat down in order at the oars and cast the cable off the pierced stone, and leaned back and threw up the sea with their blades. And a sweet sleep fell on his eyelids: unbroken, very sweet, and extremely like death.

The way four stallions harnessed abreast on a plain spring forward together under the lash, and rise high in their stride and get through the road quickly — that is how the stern of that ship lifted, and behind her the dark wave of the loud-roaring sea went seething past. She ran on, steady and safe, and not even the wheeling hawk could have kept up with her, which is the fastest thing that flies. So she ran, cutting the waves, carrying a man whose mind was like the mind of the gods, who had suffered a great many griefs in his heart, and who was now asleep, quite still, and had forgotten everything he had suffered.

---

And when the brightest star came up, the one that most often announces the light of early Dawn, the seafaring ship drew in to the island.

There is a harbour there belonging to Phorcys, the old man of the sea, in the country of Ithaca; and two headlands run out into it, sheer on the seaward side but sloping down towards the harbour, and they keep off the great waves that hard winds raise outside, so that inside, once they have come to their anchorage, well-benched ships can lie without any mooring at all. And at the head of the harbour there is a long-leaved olive tree, and close beside it a lovely cave, full of shadow, sacred to the nymphs who are called Naiads. Inside it there are mixing bowls and jars made of stone, and the bees store their honey in them; and there are long looms of stone, where the nymphs weave their sea-purple webs, and it is a wonder to see; and there is water in there, always running. And it has two doors. One faces the North Wind, and that is the way men go down into it. The other faces the South, and belongs to the gods; and men do not go in that way at all. It is the road of the immortals.[^1]

They rowed in there, knowing the place from old days; and the ship ran up onto the beach for half her length, driven on by the hands of the rowers. And they lifted Odysseus out of the hollow ship, still in the linen sheet and the bright rug, and laid him down on the sand, overpowered with sleep. And they carried out the goods the Phaeacians had given him and piled it all together beside the trunk of the olive tree, well off the road, in case some traveller came by and robbed him before he woke.

Then they went home again.

---

But Poseidon had not forgotten the threats he made against Odysseus long ago, and he went to ask Zeus what he meant to do about it.

"Father Zeus. I shall not be honoured among the immortal gods any longer, when mortal men do not honour me in the least — the Phaeacians, who are of my own blood. I always said Odysseus would get home only after a great deal of suffering; I never denied him a homecoming altogether, since you had promised him one and bowed your head on it. And now they have carried him asleep in a fast ship over the sea and set him down in Ithaca, and given him gifts past counting — bronze and gold and woven cloth, more than he could ever have brought out of Troy if he had come home unhurt with his fair share of the plunder."

"What a thing to say, wide-ruling Earthshaker. The gods do not dishonour you at all; it would be a hard business to assail the eldest and best of us with dishonour. And if any man gives way to his own strength and does not honour you, you always have your revenge afterwards. Do whatever you like, and whatever pleases your heart."

"I would have done it long ago, exactly as you say — but I always hold back from your anger and keep out of its way. What I should like now is to smash that beautiful Phaeacian ship on the misty sea as she comes back from her escort duty, so that they will stop this business and give up ferrying men about. And I should like to put a great mountain over their city."

"Here is what seems best to me. When all the people are looking out from the town, watching her come on — then turn her into a stone close inshore, a stone shaped like a fast ship, so that everyone will marvel at it. And then put your great mountain over their city."

So Poseidon went to Scheria and waited there. And when the seafaring ship came in, running fast and close, the Earthshaker came up to her and struck her flat with the palm of his hand and turned her into stone, and rooted her to the bottom by pressing down; and then he went away.

And the Phaeacians stood talking to one another, and one would look at the man beside him and say: "Who has bound our fast ship on the sea as she was running home? We could see the whole of her a moment ago."

And Alcinous spoke among them. "This is the fulfilment of the old prophecy my father used to tell me. He said Poseidon was angry with us because we give safe passage to everybody, and that one day, as a beautiful Phaeacian ship came home from an escort, the god would smash her on the misty sea and put a great mountain over our city. That is what the old man said. And now all of it is coming true.

"So let every one of us do as I say. Stop escorting mortals, from now on, whoever comes to our city. And let us sacrifice twelve chosen bulls to Poseidon, and hope he may pity us and not put a long mountain over our town."

And they were frightened, and got the bulls ready. So they were praying to the lord Poseidon — the leaders and the counsellors of the Phaeacian people — standing about the altar.

---

And there the poem leaves them, and never goes back. We are not told whether the mountain fell.

The last we see of the kindest people in this book — who took a naked stranger off a beach, fed him before asking his name, gave him a chair their own prince had to get out of, loaded him with treasure and refused to be thanked, and carried him home for nothing while he slept — is a crowd of them at an altar with twelve bulls, praying that a hill will not come down on their harbour. I have found no way of making that comfortable. Alcinous did not overreach; he kept the law of guest-friendship rather better than it required. What has happened is that a nation whose whole vocation is carrying lost men home has crossed a god whose particular business, for twenty years, has been stopping one lost man from getting home — and has done it by being good at the thing they are good at. Their virtue is the offence. And the poem stops the sentence halfway, so that we are left with the raised hands and the shadow of a hill that may or may not be coming.[^2]

---

And Odysseus woke up in his own country, and did not know it. He had been away a long time; and besides, Athena had poured a mist round the place, so that she could make him unrecognisable to everyone and tell him what he needed to know, and so that his wife and his townspeople and his friends should not know him until the suitors had paid for every one of their offences. So everything looked strange to its own master: the long paths, and the harbours where ships can lie, and the steep rocks, and the trees in leaf.

He sprang up and stood and looked at his own country, and groaned, and struck both his thighs with the flat of his hands.

"What is it now? Whose country have I come to this time? Are they violent and wild and unjust, or do they welcome strangers, and have they god-fearing minds? And where am I to take all this property? And where am I to go myself?

"I wish it had stayed with the Phaeacians. I could have gone to some other great king, who would have entertained me and sent me on my way. As it is, I do not know where to put this, and I am not going to leave it here for somebody else to walk off with.

"It seems the Phaeacian leaders were not so wise and just after all. They have taken me to the wrong country. They said they would bring me to Ithaca, and they have not done it. May Zeus of the suppliants pay them out.

"But come — let me count my goods and see whether they have gone off with anything in their hollow ship."

And he counted the beautiful tripods and the cauldrons, and the gold, and the fine woven cloth. And not one thing was missing. And then he cried for his own country, dragging himself along the shore of the loud-roaring sea, grieving heavily.

---

Home at last, after twenty years, and the first thing he does on the soil of Ithaca is take an inventory. I would not make him small for it — he has woken alone on an unknown beach with no crew, no ship and a heap of portable wealth lying in the open, which in this world is less a fortune than a reason for somebody to kill you. But notice that he finds nothing missing, and it does not shift him an inch: the Phaeacians have done exactly what they promised and stacked his treasure under a tree with more care than most men would take with their own, and he counts every cup and goes on grieving. He has been right to be suspicious so often that his mind runs there before his eyes can argue. The mist that is really keeping him from recognising the place is not entirely the goddess's.

---

Then Athena came up to him in the likeness of a young man, a shepherd of sheep, delicate-looking, the way kings' sons are, with a well-made double cloak round his shoulders and a javelin in his hand. And Odysseus was glad to see him.

"Friend — since you are the first person I have met in this place — good day to you. Do not come at me with any ill will. Save these goods of mine, and save me. I pray to you as I would to a god, and I have come to your knees. Tell me this truly. What country is this? What people? Is it one of the clear-seen islands, or some coast of the mainland sloping down to the sea?"

"You must be a fool, stranger, or you have come a very long way, if you are asking about this country. It is not so nameless as all that. A great many people know it. It is rugged, and no good for driving horses; but it is not a poor place either, for all that it is narrow. There is grain here past describing, and wine as well; and there is always rain on it and fresh dew; it is good country for goats and for cattle; there is timber of every kind, and the watering places never fail. So the name of Ithaca, stranger, has reached even to Troy, which they tell me is a long way from the land of Greece."

And the heart of the much-enduring man leapt, and he was glad because of his own country. And then he did not tell her the truth. He held back the thing he had been about to say, always turning over some very shrewd counsel in his breast.

"Yes — I heard of Ithaca even in Crete, out over the sea; and now I have come here myself, with these goods. And I left as much again behind for my children when I ran, because I killed a man: the son of Idomeneus, Orsilochus, who was quicker on his feet than anybody in the whole of Crete. He wanted to rob me of my plunder from Troy, that I had gone through so much for, because I would not do his father a favour and serve under him at Troy, but had my own command. I struck him with a bronze-tipped spear as he came in from the field; I lay in wait for him beside the road with one companion, and a black night covered the sky, and nobody saw us.

"And when I had killed him I went to a ship at once and begged the lordly Phoenicians, and gave them as much plunder as satisfied them. I asked them to take me and set me down at Pylos, or at Elis. But the force of the wind drove them away from there, very much against their will; they were not trying to cheat me. And we came here off our course in the night. We rowed into the harbour with a great deal of trouble, and not one of us gave a thought to supper, badly as we wanted it; we simply got out of the ship and lay down. And a sweet sleep came over me, tired as I was; and they took my goods out of the hollow ship and set them down beside me where I was lying on the sand, and went aboard, and sailed for Sidon. And I was left behind, sick at heart."

And Athena smiled at him, and stroked him with her hand.[^3]

---

She turned into a woman then, tall and beautiful and skilled at glorious handiwork.

"He would have to be a cunning man, and a thief, who could get past you in any kind of trickery — even if it were a god who met you. You terrible man. Crafty-minded. Never tired of deceiving. So even in your own country you were not going to give up your tricks and your lying stories, which you love from the bottom of your heart.

"Come — let us not talk about it any more. The two of us know all about craft. You are far the best of all men at planning and at speaking, and I am famous among all the gods for wisdom and for sharpness. And you did not know Athena, the daughter of Zeus, who always stands beside you and guards you in every trouble — and who made you loved by all the Phaeacians.

"And I have come here now to weave a plan with you, and to hide the goods the Phaeacians gave you, because I put it in their minds; and to tell you how much you still have to suffer in your own well-built house. Endure it. You must. And do not tell anybody, man or woman, that you have come back from your wandering. Take a great deal of pain in silence, and put up with men's violence."

"Goddess, it is hard for a mortal man to know you when he meets you, however clever he is, because you take whatever shape you please. But I do know this: you were kind to me in the old days, all the while we sons of the Greeks were fighting at Troy. And when we had sacked the steep city of Priam (Troy) and gone away in our ships, and a god scattered the Greeks — after that I never saw you. I never noticed you coming aboard my ship to keep grief away from me. I went on wandering with a torn heart until the gods let me out of it.

"So now I beg you by your father — because I do not believe I have come to Ithaca; I think I am roaming about in some other country and you are saying this to mock me — tell me whether I really have come home."

"That is always the way your mind runs. And that is exactly why I cannot abandon you when you are in trouble — because you are civil, and quick-witted, and steady. Any other man coming home from wandering would have rushed off gladly to see his children and his wife in his own halls. You do not even want to *ask* about them, or find anything out, until you have first put your wife to the test — who is sitting in your halls exactly as she always was, and the nights and the days waste away for her, miserably, while she cries.

"I was never in any doubt about it myself. I knew you would come home, after losing all your companions. But I did not want to fight with Poseidon, my father's brother, who nursed his anger against you and was furious because you blinded his own son.

"But come, and I will show you the country of Ithaca, so that you can be sure."[^4]

And she scattered the mist, and the land appeared. And Odysseus was glad, delighted with his own country, and kissed the grain-giving earth; and he lifted his hands and prayed to the nymphs. "Naiad nymphs, daughters of Zeus — I never expected to see you again. Now I greet you with these loving prayers. And we shall give you gifts as we used to, if the daughter of Zeus is kind and lets me live, and brings my dear son up to be a man."

---

That exchange is the hinge of the whole second half, and it goes past very lightly because she is laughing at him while she says it. *Any other man would have run home to his wife and children; you will not even ask after them until you have tested her.* His mother is dead of missing him and he has spoken to her ghost; his father is sleeping in the ashes; his son has grown up without ever seeing his face; and his wife has held off a hundred and eight armed men with a loom and her wits. He has landed, and his first instinct is not to find them but to find out. Athena is not scolding him — she admires it, and it is why she has stayed — but the poem is not the goddess, and it will spend ten books making us feel what it costs.

---

Then they sat down together beside the trunk of the sacred olive and planned death for the suitors.

"Son of Laertes. Think how you are going to get your hands on those shameless men, who have been lording it in your halls for three years now, courting your godlike wife and offering her wedding gifts. And she is forever grieving for you to come home, and she holds out hope to all of them, and makes each man promises, and sends them messages — and her mind is on something else entirely."

"Well. So I was very nearly going to die in my own halls the way Agamemnon died, if you had not told me all this, goddess. Come — weave me a plan, so that I can pay them back. And stand beside me, and put the same courage and daring into me that you did when we untied the shining diadem of Troy. If you would stand by me with that much fire in you, lady goddess, I would fight three hundred men."

"I shall be with you, certainly, and I shall not lose sight of you when we are about this work. And I think a good deal of the floor is going to be spattered with the blood and brains of the men who are eating up your living.

"Come now — I shall make you unrecognisable to everybody. I shall shrivel the good skin on your supple limbs, and take the fair hair off your head, and put a rag round you that anybody would shudder to see a man wearing; and I shall film over your eyes, that were so fine before — so that you look contemptible to all the suitors, and to your wife, and to the son you left behind.

"And you go first of all to the swineherd, who looks after your pigs. He is loyal to you, and fond of your son, and fond of steady Penelope. You will find him sitting with the swine, beside the Raven's Rock and the spring of Arethusa, eating acorns and drinking the black water — the things that make swine run to good fat. Stay there, and sit with him, and ask him about everything, while I go to Sparta to call your son home."

"Then why on earth did you not tell him, when you knew the whole of it? Was it so that he could wander about the barren sea in misery as well, while other men eat his living?"

"Do not let him weigh on you. I sent him myself, and I went with him, so that he might come out of it with a good name. He is not suffering at all; he is sitting comfortably in the house of Menelaus with an endless supply of everything beside him. There are young men lying in wait for him in a black ship, meaning to kill him before he can get back. I do not think it will happen. I think the earth will cover a few of those suitors first."

And with that she touched him with her wand. She shrivelled the good skin on his supple limbs and destroyed the fair hair on his head and put an old man's skin all over him, and filmed over his eyes, which had been so fine. And she put a different cloak on him, and a tunic — filthy things, torn, blackened with foul smoke — and over it all she threw the big hide of a fast deer, worn bald. And she gave him a staff, and a shabby bag full of holes with a twisted cord to sling it by.

And when they had settled everything the two of them parted; and she went off to Sparta, to fetch his son.[^5]

## NOTES TO BOOK THIRTEEN

[^1]: **The cave with two doors.** A cave at the head of the harbour, sacred to the water-nymphs, with stone mixing bowls and stone looms inside it, bees making honey in the jars, water always running, and two entrances — a northern one for men and a southern one that only gods use. It is a beautiful piece of description and later readers could not leave it alone. There survives a whole treatise on it, written some nine hundred years after the poem by the philosopher Porphyry, arguing that every detail is an allegory of the soul's descent into the body and its return: the cave is the material world, the honey is the pleasure that traps us, the two doors are the gates by which souls come into life and go out of it, and so on for many pages. I mention it partly because it is charming and partly as a warning, since this poem attracts that kind of reading the way a wall attracts ivy, and the ivy is often lovely and occasionally tells you more about the reader than the wall. At the plain level the cave is doing something simple and rather good: he is being put ashore, unconscious, at the threshold of a place where the gods have their own private door.

[^2]: **The sentence that is never finished.** The prophecy was planted in Book Eight, note 4, where Alcinous mentioned it in the middle of a boast and shrugged it off. It has now half come true: the ship is stone. Whether the mountain came down, the poem does not say. Ancient readers argued about it hard, and the argument turned on one Greek word in Zeus's instructions, which can be read either as *and cover their city* or as *but do not cover their city* — the whole fate of a nation resting on a syllable, with some manuscripts and some ancient critics going each way. So the ambiguity may not merely be the poem declining to tell us; it may be built into the text at the level of the grammar, and it has been unresolved since antiquity. I find I would rather it stayed that way. A poem that has spent three books teaching you to love a people, and then puts them at an altar under a shadow and looks away, is doing something no tidier ending would do half so well.

[^3]: **The Cretan lies.** This is the first of several — he will tell four or five more before the poem ends, to the swineherd, to the suitors, to Penelope and to his own father — and they are worth watching as a set, because they are not improvisations. They are variations on one story, told with such consistency that scholars call them the Cretan tales: he is always a Cretan of good family, there is always a killing or a wrong done to him, there is always a flight by sea, and there are almost always Phoenician traders involved. He can produce it under any pressure, in any company, at any length. Two things make Crete a good choice. It was a real and famous place with a real and famous king, far enough from Ithaca to be unverifiable but close enough to be plausible, and exactly the sort of place a wandering man of rank might genuinely have come from. And Cretans already had a certain reputation in the Greek world for telling stories; the proverb about Cretan liars is later than this poem, but the joke it draws on is not. When Odysseus wants a false name and a false country, he reaches for the one that comes with a wink attached.

[^4]: **Why Athena stayed away.** She gives her reason plainly, and it is worth having, because it explains how the gods work here and will save a good deal of puzzling later. She did not abandon him and was never in doubt that he would survive; she simply would not fight her uncle. Poseidon was angry, Poseidon had a right to be angry, and no other god was going to stand in his way while the anger lasted — not out of fear exactly, but because that is not how these gods deal with one another. They do not overrule; they wait. Zeus does the same thing in this very book: told that his brother wants to punish a whole nation, he says, in effect, *please yourself.* So divine favour in this poem is not protection. Athena loves this man and has loved him for twenty years, and it did not save one of his ships or one of his men. What it does is arrive at the right moment with the right information, once the other god's back is turned. Worth knowing, when people call the *Odyssey* a poem in which the gods look after their favourites.

[^5]: **The beggar's kit, and a debt paid.** Note what she gives him: a staff, and a bag with holes in it on a twisted string. That is the whole equipment of a professional beggar in this world, and beggary was a recognised way of living rather than a simple catastrophe — a man went from house to house, was given scraps, carried them in the bag, and had a place, of a low kind, in the order of things. Hospitality worked at the bottom of society as well as the top, and there were rules about it that men were expected to keep even towards the poorest arrival; much of what happens in the next eight books turns on who keeps them. — And there is a larger thing here. In Book Nine this man stood in the stern of a ship, out of danger, with his crew hanging off his arms begging him to be quiet, and shouted his own name across the water to a blinded giant, because he could not bear that the deed should belong to nobody. It cost him ten years. He is now going to walk into his own house in a filthy hide with a holed bag over his shoulder and not say that name to a living soul for eight books, while men throw furniture at him. Of everything the wand does to him on that beach, that is the real disguise, and it is the hardest thing this poem ever asks him to do.
