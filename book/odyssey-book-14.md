# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK FOURTEEN
### In Which a Swineherd Gives Away What He Cannot Spare, and Is Told a Great Many Lies

---

He went up from the harbour by a rough track, through wooded country and over the hills, to the place Athena had told him of, where he would find the good swineherd, who looked after his property better than any of the servants Odysseus had ever acquired.

He found him sitting in the doorway of his yard — a high yard, built in the open with a view all round, big and handsome, and standing free. The swineherd had built it himself for the pigs of his absent master, without saying anything to his mistress or to old Laertes: he had quarried the stones and put a hedge of wild pear along the top. And outside he had driven in a stockade of split oak stakes, all round, close together, taking the dark part of the wood. And inside the yard he had made twelve sties, side by side, for the sows to sleep in, and fifty breeding pigs were penned in each. The boars slept outside; there were far fewer of them, because the lordly suitors kept eating them and thinning them down, since the swineherd had to send them the pick of his fattest hog every day. There were three hundred and sixty left. And four dogs lay beside them, like wild beasts, which the swineherd had reared himself.

The dogs saw Odysseus and came at him barking, and he sat down cunningly and let the staff drop out of his hand. And there in his own farmyard he would have been badly mauled, but the swineherd came out fast on quick feet, and the leather fell out of his hands as he ran through the doorway shouting, and he drove the dogs off in all directions with a shower of stones.

"Old man, the dogs very nearly tore you to pieces, all in a moment, and you would have poured shame all over me. And the gods have given me plenty else to grieve about. Here I sit mourning for a master who was like a god, and rearing fat hogs for other men to eat, while he is very likely somewhere in a foreign country going hungry — if he is even alive and still sees the light of the sun.

"Come along in, old man. Come into the hut. When you have had bread and wine and eaten your fill, you can tell me where you are from and what you have suffered."

And the good swineherd led him in and sat him down, throwing down a great heap of brushwood and spreading over it the shaggy hide of a wild goat, which he slept on himself, and it was big and thick. And Odysseus was glad to be received like that, and said so.

"Stranger, it would not be right for me to turn away a stranger, not even one worse off than you. All strangers and beggars come from Zeus. What we give is small, and welcome; that is the way with servants, who live in fear when young men are the masters. The gods have certainly blocked the homecoming of the man who would have been fond of me and given me things of my own — a house, and a piece of land, and a wife other men wanted — the sort of thing a kind master gives a servant who has worked hard for him and whose work the gods have prospered. My work has prospered here. He would have looked after me handsomely if he had grown old in this place. But he is dead.

"I wish Helen's whole tribe had been wiped out root and branch, since she loosened the knees of so many men."[^1]

---

He got up at once and pulled his belt tight round his tunic and went out to the sties where the young pigs were penned, and picked out two and brought them in and sacrificed them both, and singed them and cut them up and spitted them. And when he had roasted it all he brought it and set it in front of Odysseus, hot, still on the spits, and sprinkled white barley meal over it; and he mixed honey-sweet wine in an ivy-wood bowl and sat down opposite and told him to eat.

"Eat now, stranger — such as servants have. Sucking pigs. The suitors eat the fatted hogs, and they have no thought in their minds of the anger of the gods, and no pity.

"The blessed gods do not love a cruel action. They honour justice, and the right things men do. Even men who go out raiding another man's country, and Zeus grants them plunder and they load their ships and come home — even on their hearts there falls a heavy fear of what the gods will do.

"But these men know something. They have heard the voice of some god about my master's grim death, and so they will not court honestly and they will not go back to their own houses, but eat up his goods at their ease, insolently, and spare nothing. Every day and every night that comes from Zeus they slaughter victims, and not one or two either; and they draw the wine off and waste it.

"His living was enormous. No lord had so much, not on the dark mainland and not on Ithaca itself. Twenty men together have not so much wealth. I will count it for you. Twelve herds of cattle on the mainland, and as many flocks of sheep, and as many droves of pigs, and as many wide-ranging herds of goats, all pastured by hired men and by his own herdsmen. And here in Ithaca there are eleven herds of goats grazing at the far end of the island, with good men watching them; and every day each man drives in a beast for the suitors, the best of the fattened goats he has. And I look after these pigs and guard them, and pick out the best boar and send it up to them."

Odysseus ate the meat and drank the wine, ravenously, and said nothing, sowing trouble for the suitors. And when he had eaten and his heart was satisfied, the swineherd filled the bowl he had been drinking from himself and handed it over, brimming; and Odysseus took it, and was glad.

"Friend — who is this man who bought you with his own property? You say he was rich and powerful, and that he died for Agamemnon's honour. Tell me his name. I may know him. Zeus and the other immortals know whether I have seen him and can bring you news. I have wandered a great deal."

"Old man, no wanderer who came here with news of him would ever persuade his wife and his dear son. Men who need a lodging tell lies as they please and are not interested in telling the truth. Anybody who turns up on Ithaca goes straight to my mistress and spins her a tale, and she takes them in kindly and asks about everything, and the tears run down her face, which is what a woman does when her husband has died somewhere else.

"You would soon make up a story yourself, old man, if somebody gave you a cloak and a tunic for it.

"But by now the dogs and the swift birds will have torn the skin off his bones, and the life will have left him. Or the fish will have eaten him in the sea, and his bones will be lying on some beach wrapped in sand. So he has died out there; and there is grief left for all his friends afterwards, and for me most of all. I shall never find so kind a master again, wherever I go — not if I went back to the house of my own father and mother, where I was born and where they brought me up.

"And I do not grieve so much for them, badly as I want to see them with my own eyes in my own country, as I am taken with longing for Odysseus, who is gone. I shrink from saying his name, stranger, even though he is not here. He was extremely fond of me and cared about me in his heart. I call him *honoured sir*, though he is a long way off."

---

I should like to introduce this man properly, because we have just met the most decent person in the poem and he is going to be in it for the rest of the way.

Eumaeus keeps pigs. He is a slave; he says so himself in the second sentence he speaks, and mentions, without any particular weight, that the master who bought him has been gone twenty years and that he has kept the herd up regardless. He has built the yard he is standing in with his own hands, quarried the stone himself, hedged it and stockaded it, and done the whole thing on his own initiative without troubling the mistress or the old man. And he has done it for a household that has not sent him an order in two decades.

Notice what he does when a filthy old vagrant with a holed bag comes out of the woods. He drives the dogs off, apologises for them, gives up his own bed of brushwood and goat hide, kills two of the pigs he is answerable for, and hands over his own drinking bowl full. He has nothing whatever, and he gives away rather more of it than he can afford, to a stranger with no claim on him at all, because all strangers come from Zeus.

Up at the house, a hundred and eight men of good family are eating the same estate at the rate of a hog a day, and have been for three years.

The poem does not point at that. It does not need to; it simply puts the two households side by side and lets you look. But it does do one thing here that it does almost nowhere else, and it is a very strange and touching mannerism: when Eumaeus speaks, the poem sometimes turns and addresses him directly — *and you said to him in reply, swineherd Eumaeus* — as though the poet had temporarily forgotten he was narrating and had begun talking to the man instead.[^2]

---

"Friend, since you deny it altogether and say he will never come back — well, I will not merely say it, I will swear it. Odysseus is coming home. And let me have my reward for the news the moment he sets foot in his own house: dress me in a cloak and a tunic, good clothes. Before that I will not take anything, badly as I need it. I hate a man who gives way to poverty and tells lies as much as I hate the gates of death.

"Let Zeus be my witness first of all, and this table of hospitality, and the hearth of Odysseus that I have come to. All of it will happen exactly as I say. Within this very month Odysseus will come here. As the old moon goes out and the new one comes in, he will come home; and he will take his revenge on every man who is dishonouring his wife and his son."

"Old man, I shall never pay you that reward, and Odysseus is not coming home. Drink your wine in peace and let us talk about something else; do not remind me of it. The heart in my breast hurts when anybody mentions my honoured lord.

"We will let the oath alone. But I do wish Odysseus would come, as I want him to, and Penelope too, and old Laertes and godlike Telemachus. And now I am grieving without stopping for the boy — for Telemachus, whom the gods reared like a young tree, and I used to think he would be no worse than his father among men, a wonder to look at. And some god, or some man, has upset the balance of his mind, and he has gone off to sacred Pylos after news of his father; and the lordly suitors are lying in wait for him on the way home, so that the line of Arceisius may be wiped out of Ithaca and leave no name.

"But let him be, whether he is caught or gets away. Tell me about your own troubles. Who are you? Where do you come from? What ship did you come on? How did the sailors bring you to Ithaca?"

---

"I will tell you all of it truly. I only wish the two of us had food and sweet wine enough to sit here in the hut and feast quietly while other people did the work — then I could talk for a whole year and not get to the end of my heart's troubles.

"By birth I am from Crete, and my father was a rich man. He had a great many other sons born and brought up in his halls, lawful sons of a wedded wife; and my mother was a bought woman, a concubine. But Castor son of Hylax honoured me exactly as he honoured his lawful sons, and he was honoured like a god among the Cretans for his good fortune and his wealth and his fine sons. And the fates of death carried him off to the house of Hades, and his sons divided up the property and cast lots for it, and gave me a very small share and a house.

"But I married a wife from a rich family, on my own merits, because I was no fool and no coward. All that is gone now; though I think you can tell what the crop was by looking at the stubble.

"Ares and Athena gave me courage. Whenever I picked the best men for an ambush, sowing trouble for the enemy, my proud heart never once saw death in front of it: I would leap out far ahead of everybody and take my man with the spear if he gave ground at all. That is what I was like in war. Work was never dear to me, nor keeping a careful house and rearing fine children; what I loved was ships with oars, and battles, and polished spears and arrows — grim things, that other people shudder at. I suppose some god put that in my heart. Different men take joy in different work.

"Before the sons of the Greeks ever set foot in Troy, I had nine times been the leader of men and fast ships against foreign parts, and a great deal fell into my hands. I took my pick of it and drew plenty more by lot, and my house grew rich quickly, and I became a man to be feared and respected among the Cretans.

"And when Zeus arranged that hateful expedition which loosened the knees of so many men, they told me and famous Idomeneus to lead the ships to Troy, and there was no way of refusing; the people's voice was too hard against it. Nine years we sons of the Greeks fought there, and in the tenth we sacked the city of Priam (Troy) and went home in the ships, and a god scattered the Greeks.

"And Zeus arranged trouble for me. I stayed one month only, enjoying my children and my wedded wife and my property; and then my heart told me to fit out ships and sail to Egypt with a crew of good men."

And he told the swineherd the whole of it: how nine ships were fitted out and the men came quickly; how they feasted six days and sailed on the seventh with a fair North Wind, easily, as if going downstream, and came to Egypt on the fifth day. How he told his crews to stay by the ships and sent scouts up to the lookout places — and how they gave way to their own insolence instead and went straight off and plundered the Egyptians' beautiful fields, and carried off the women and the little children and killed the men. How the cry reached the city, and the people came out at dawn, and the whole plain filled with foot soldiers and chariots and the flash of bronze; and how Zeus put a coward's panic into his companions and none of them dared stand, so that a great many were cut down with the sharp bronze and the rest were led away alive to work as slaves.

"And Zeus put this into my mind — and I wish I had died in Egypt then and met my fate there, because there was still more sorrow waiting to welcome me. I took the helmet off my head at once and the shield off my shoulders and threw the spear out of my hand, and went straight to the king's chariot and took hold of his knees and kissed them. And he protected me and pitied me, and put me in the chariot and took me home with me weeping. A great many of them came at me with their ash spears, wanting to kill me, because they were extremely angry. But he kept them off, and had a proper regard for the anger of Zeus who protects strangers, and who is angrier than anyone at wicked deeds."

He stayed there seven years, he said, and gathered a great deal of property among the Egyptians, because everybody gave him something. And in the eighth year a Phoenician came along, a man well practised in deceit, a greedy rogue who had already done a great deal of harm to people; and he talked him round and took him to Phoenicia, where his house and his goods were, and kept him a full year. And when the months and days had gone by and the year had come round again, he put him on a ship for Libya, having lied that he was to carry a cargo with him — and meaning in fact to sell him there for a very large price.

"I went with him, suspecting it, because I had no choice. And the ship ran before a fresh North Wind, out in mid sea beyond Crete; and Zeus arranged destruction for them. When we had left Crete and no other land showed, nothing but sky and sea, then the son of Cronos set a black cloud over the hollow ship and the sea went dark beneath it, and he thundered and threw his bolt into her. She shuddered from end to end, struck by the bolt of Zeus, and filled with the smell of sulphur, and every man fell out of her. They were carried on the waves round the black ship like sea-crows, and the god took away their homecoming.

"And Zeus himself, though my heart was in agony, put the enormous mast of the dark-prowed ship into my hands, so that I might get out from under my destruction even yet. I got my arms round that and was carried along by the ruinous winds. Nine days I was carried, and on the tenth black night a great rolling wave put me ashore in the country of the Thesprotians. And there the king of the Thesprotians, the hero Pheidon, looked after me for nothing, because his own son came on me lying there beaten down with cold and exhaustion, and raised me up by the hand and took me to his father's house, and gave me a cloak and a tunic to wear.

"And it was there I heard about Odysseus."

---

That, if I may say so, is a masterpiece, and the reason I have let it run at this length is that it is going to happen four more times before the poem ends and this is the one where you can watch the machinery.

Look at the shape of it. A Cretan of good family; a wrong done at the division of an inheritance; a life of raiding and ships; Troy; a disastrous eastern voyage; a treacherous Phoenician; a shipwreck in which everyone drowns but the teller; a rescue by a kind king. Almost all of it is untrue and every part of it is *nearly* his own life, turned a few degrees. He really did go to Troy and really did come home by way of eleven disasters and really was the only man of his crew to live. He is not inventing a stranger. He is telling his own story with the names filed off, which is why he can do it at this length without a stumble, and why it will come out the same shape again to the next four people who ask.

And there is one true thing buried in the middle of it, which is the whole point of the exercise: *Odysseus is alive, he is close by, and he is coming.* The lie is the delivery system.[^3]

---

He told him the rest: that the king had entertained Odysseus on his way home and showed him the treasure Odysseus had gathered, bronze and gold and worked iron, enough to keep a man's household to the tenth generation; that Odysseus had gone up to Dodona to ask the great oak of Zeus how he should come back to his own country after so long, openly or in secret; and that the king had sworn, pouring a libation with his own hands, that the ship was launched and the crew ready to take him home.

"But he sent me off first, because a Thesprotian ship happened to be leaving for Dulichium. He told them to take me carefully to king Acastus. And they decided among themselves to do me harm instead, so that I should come to complete misery. When the ship was well out from land they set about making me a slave. They stripped the cloak and tunic off me and put this filthy rag on me instead, and this tunic, torn, the ones you can see with your own eyes.

"And in the evening they came in to the fields of Ithaca, and tied me up in the well-benched ship with a twisted rope, and went ashore in a hurry and had their supper on the beach. And the gods themselves undid the knot for me, easily; and I wrapped the rag round my head and slid down the smooth lading-plank and put my chest to the water and swam off with both hands, and very soon I was well clear of them. Then I went up into a thicket of flowering brush and lay there crouching, and they went about groaning and looking; and when they thought there was no profit in searching any further they went back aboard.

"And the gods hid me easily, and brought me here to the farm of a man who understands things. So it seems it is still my lot to live."

"You poor man. You have stirred my heart up with all that. But you have not got it right about Odysseus, and you will not persuade me. Why does a man like you have to lie for no reason?

"I know how it stands with my master's homecoming. He was hated by all the gods, utterly, or they would have let him die at Troy, or in the arms of his friends when he had wound the war up. Then the whole Greek army would have made him a tomb, and he would have won great glory for his son as well. Now the storm winds have snatched him away without a word said.

"And I keep to myself here with the pigs, and I do not go to the city, unless sensible Penelope tells me to come, when news has arrived from somewhere. And then they all sit round the newcomer and question him closely — the ones who are grieving for their long-absent lord, and the ones who are delighted, eating up his living for nothing.

"But I have had no appetite for asking questions since an Aetolian took me in with a story. He had killed a man and wandered a long way, and came to my house, and I treated him kindly. He told me he had seen Odysseus in Crete with Idomeneus, mending his ships after a storm had broken them up, and said he would come in the summer or the autumn, bringing a great deal of treasure and his godlike crew.

"So you, old man of many sorrows — since a god has brought you here to me — do not try to please me with lies. It is not for that I shall respect you and treat you as a friend. It is out of fear of Zeus who protects strangers, and out of pity for you."

"You have a very slow heart in your breast, if not even an oath brings you round. Come then, let us make a bargain, and let the gods on Olympus be witnesses. If your master comes back to this house, you shall dress me in a cloak and a tunic and send me on my way to Dulichium, which is where I want to go. And if he does not come as I say, then set your men on me and throw me off a great cliff, so that the next beggar thinks twice about lying to you."

"Stranger — that would do wonders for my good name and my reputation among men, now and in the future: to bring you to my hut and give you hospitality and then kill you. What a light heart I should have when I said my prayers to Zeus afterwards.

"But it is time to eat. I wish the men would come, so that we could get a good supper ready in the hut."

---

They came in with the pigs and penned them, and there was an enormous squealing as the sows were shut up. And the swineherd called out to his men to bring in the best boar, so that he could kill it for a stranger from far away — "and we shall get something out of it too, who have suffered a long time over the white-tusked swine, while other men eat what we work for and pay nothing."

He split the logs with the pitiless bronze, and they brought in a very fat boar five years old and stood it by the hearth. And the swineherd did not forget the immortals, because he had good sense: as a first offering he cut hairs from the head of the white-tusked boar and threw them in the fire, and prayed to all the gods that clever Odysseus might come back to his own house. Then he struck it with a billet of oak that he had left over from splitting, and the life left it; and they cut the throat and singed it and jointed it. And the swineherd laid raw pieces from every limb on the rich fat, and threw them into the fire sprinkled with barley meal; and they cut up the rest and spitted it and roasted it and drew it off and heaped it all on platters.

And the swineherd stood up to share it out, because he had a fine sense of what was fair, and divided the whole of it into seven portions. One he set aside with a prayer for the nymphs and for Hermes; and the rest he handed round to each man. And he honoured Odysseus with the long chine of the white-tusked boar, and made his master's heart glad.[^4]

"Eumaeus — may you be as dear to father Zeus as you are to me, since you honour a man in my state with the best of what you have."

"Eat, my strange guest, and enjoy what there is. The god gives one thing and holds back another, whatever he pleases, because he can do anything."

So they ate, and when they had finished, Mesaulius cleared the food away — a man the swineherd had got for himself while his master was gone, without troubling his mistress or old Laertes; he had bought him from the Taphians with his own goods. And they put out their hands to the food, and when they had had enough of eating and drinking they turned their minds to bed.

---

And a bad night came on, black, and Zeus rained the whole of it, and the West Wind blew hard and wet.

And Odysseus said something, to try the swineherd out — to see whether he would take his own cloak off and give it to him, or tell one of his men to, since he was so careful of him.

"Listen to me now, Eumaeus, and all the rest of you. I am going to make a boast and tell you something, because the wine is telling me to, crazy wine, which sets even a sensible man singing and laughing softly, and gets him up to dance, and brings out a word that would have been better left in. Since I have started, I shall not hold it back.

"I wish I were young, and my strength what it was, on the day we laid an ambush under the walls of Troy. Odysseus was leading it, and Menelaus, and I was the third in command, because they asked for me.

"When we got to the city and the steep wall, we lay down in the thick brush among the reeds and the marsh, crouching under our arms; and night came on, a bad one, with the North Wind dropping, and frost; and snow came down over us like hoar frost, cold, and ice was forming on our shields.

"Now all the others had cloaks and tunics and slept comfortably with their shoulders covered by their shields. But I had left my cloak behind with my men when I set out, like a fool, because I did not think I would be cold; so I came along with nothing but a shield and a bright kilt.

"And in the third watch of the night, when the stars had gone over, I nudged Odysseus with my elbow — he was lying next to me — and he was awake at once.

"*Son of Laertes, I shall not be among the living much longer. This cold is finishing me. I have no cloak. Some god fooled me into coming out in a tunic, and now there is no way out of it.*

"And he had a thought in his mind on the instant, being the sort of man he was for planning as well as fighting. And he said quietly: *Be quiet, in case one of the other Greeks hears you.* And then he raised his head on his elbow and said:

"*Listen, friends. A dream has come to me in my sleep, a warning. We have come a very long way from the ships. I wish somebody would go and tell Agamemnon, in case he sends more men up from the ships.*

"And Thoas the son of Andraemon jumped up straight away and flung off his purple cloak and set off running for the ships. And I lay down gladly in his cloak, and Dawn came up on her golden throne.

"I wish I were young now, and my strength what it was. Then one of the swineherds in this farm would give me a cloak — out of friendliness, and out of respect for a good man. As it is, they look down on me, because I have poor clothes on my body."

And the swineherd said:

"Old man, that is a good story you have told, and there is not a word in it out of place or wasted. So you shall not go short of clothing or anything else that a hard-pressed suppliant deserves when he meets one — for tonight. In the morning you will be flapping about in your own rags again, because we have no store of cloaks here and no spare tunics; each man has one. But when the dear son of Odysseus comes, he will give you a cloak and a tunic himself and send you wherever your heart wants to go."

And he jumped up and made him a bed near the fire and threw sheepskins and goatskins over it; and Odysseus lay down, and Eumaeus threw over him a great thick cloak, which he kept beside him for a change of clothing when a really bad storm came on.

---

That is the most charming thing in the book and I do not want it to go past unadmired.

He wants a blanket. He is a guest in a hut where there is exactly one spare cloak in the world and its owner is going out into the rain in a few minutes; and rather than ask, which would embarrass everybody, he tells a story — announcing at the top that the wine is making him boastful, so that nobody has to take it seriously — in which a cold night is solved by a clever trick, and in which he casts himself in a small part and gives the clever trick to Odysseus.

And Eumaeus, who has already said out loud that men in need make up tales for a cloak and a tunic, listens to a tale that is transparently a request for a cloak and a tunic, praises it as a good story with not a word out of place, and hands over his own cloak with the gentlest possible caveat — *for tonight; in the morning you will be in your rags again; we are not rich here.*

Neither of them mentions what is actually happening. They are both far too well-mannered.

---

So Odysseus slept, and the young men slept beside him. But the swineherd did not choose to sleep in there, away from the pigs; he got himself ready to go out, and Odysseus was glad to see how well he looked after his master's property while his master was far away.

First he slung a sharp sword over his broad shoulders, and then put on a very thick cloak against the wind, and picked up the fleece of a big well-fed goat, and took a sharp javelin to keep off dogs and men.

And he went off to lie down where the white-tusked boars were sleeping, under a hollow rock, out of the North Wind.

## NOTES TO BOOK FOURTEEN

[^1]: **A swineherd who curses Helen.** It is worth registering how far the war reaches. Eumaeus has never been to Troy, has no rank, owns nothing, and lives at the far end of a small island looking after pigs; and his considered opinion, offered in his first speech, is that Helen's whole family should have been destroyed. Twenty years earlier a quarrel in a palace on the mainland took his master away, and everything that has gone wrong in his life since has come out of it — the lost prospects, the house he was never given, the estate being eaten in front of him. When the poem wants to show you the cost of the war it does not go to a battlefield. It goes to a farm.

[^2]: **"And you said to him, Eumaeus."** Several times in the next few books the poem stops narrating this man and starts *addressing* him, switching into the second person for a line — you said, you answered — and then switching back. It is a small thing and it has fascinated readers for a very long time. Only a handful of characters in Homer are ever spoken to like this: Patroclus in the *Iliad*, and Menelaus, and here a slave who keeps pigs. Nobody knows for certain why. The dullest explanation is metrical convenience, and it may be right. The explanation most readers prefer, and I confess I am one of them, is that the poet grew fond of him. It is the only place in this poem where the teller's own affection leaks visibly through the surface of the telling, and it happens to the poorest man in the story.

[^3]: **The Cretan tales.** This is the longest of them and the best place to watch how they are built, since he will do it four more times — to the suitors, to Penelope, and to his own father. They are not improvisations but variations on one standing story: a Cretan of good family, an inheritance grievance, a life of raiding, Troy, a voyage east that goes wrong, treacherous Phoenicians, a shipwreck nobody else survives, and a rescue by a decent stranger. What makes it work is that it is his own life at a few degrees' offset, so that he never has to invent anything from nothing and never contradicts himself. And every version carries the same small true cargo, planted where a listener will not notice it being planted: *Odysseus is alive and close by.* He is not lying instead of telling the truth. He is lying in order to deliver it.

[^4]: **Seven portions, and the chine.** Watch the swineherd carve, because the whole social order is in it. He divides the meat into seven equal shares, one for each man present, which is the mark of a fair division and the reason the poem says he had a good sense of what was right; then he sets one share aside for the nymphs and for Hermes, who looks after herdsmen and travellers, before anybody eats. And then he takes the long chine — the best cut on the animal, the piece reserved for the guest of highest standing — and gives it to the filthy old beggar sitting by his fire. It is exactly what Odysseus himself did for the blind singer at the Phaeacian dinner, and the poem notes, in one line, that it made his master's heart glad. Eumaeus does not know he is honouring his master. He is simply honouring a guest, which is what he would have done for anybody.
