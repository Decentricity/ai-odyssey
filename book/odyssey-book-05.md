# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK FIVE
### In Which We Finally Meet the Man Himself, and He Is Crying on a Beach

---

Dawn got up out of bed, from beside her husband Tithonus, to carry the light to the immortals and to men.[^1]

And the gods sat down to a meeting, and Zeus who thunders on high sat among them, the strongest of them all. And Athena started in again about everything Odysseus had suffered; because it was on her mind that he was still in the nymph's house.

"Father Zeus, and the rest of you gods who live forever.

"Let no sceptred king from this day on be kind, or gentle, or fair-minded, or have any justice in his heart at all. Let him be harsh, and let him do wicked things — since not one man remembers Odysseus among the people he ruled, and he was as gentle to them as a father.

"He is lying on an island in dreadful pain, in the house of the nymph Calypso, who keeps him there by force, and he cannot get back to his own country. He has no ships with oars and no companions to carry him over the wide back of the sea.

"And now they mean to kill his beloved son on his way home. He has gone after news of his father, to sacred Pylos and to bright Sparta."

Those first four sentences are not new. Mentor said them, word for word, in the assembly on Ithaca in Book Two, standing up in front of a crowd of men who would not meet his eye. Athena has taken an old man's speech and carried it up a mountain and said it again to the king of the gods.

I do not think that is an accident, and I do not think it is the poem running short of material. It is the poem telling you that the complaint is the same complaint at both ends of the world. Down in the dust of a village square, an old man says: *this is what comes of being a decent ruler — nothing.* And up on Olympus, in front of the being who is supposed to be in charge of how the world is arranged, his own daughter says exactly the same thing, in exactly the same words, with the same edge on it.

Zeus was not pleased.

"My child. What a thing to let past your teeth. Did you not plan the whole of this yourself? Was it not your own idea that Odysseus should come home and pay those men out? As for Telemachus — you are perfectly capable of managing him; use your skill and bring him back to his own country unhurt, and let the suitors sail home again with nothing done."

Then he turned to his son Hermes and said:

"Hermes — you are our messenger for these things. Go and tell the nymph with the beautiful hair (Calypso) that this is settled and final: the enduring man goes home. And he goes with no escort — no gods, no mortal men. He goes on a raft, lashed together, and he will suffer, and on the twentieth day he will come to Scheria, to the country of the Phaeacians, who are near cousins to the gods. And they will honour him in their hearts like a god, and put him on a ship and send him to his own land, and give him bronze and gold and clothing, more than he would ever have carried out of Troy if he had come home unhurt with his fair share of the plunder. That is how it is laid down that he sees his own people again, and his high-roofed house, and his own country."

And Hermes went, and did not argue.

He bound the beautiful sandals under his feet, the immortal golden ones that carry him over the water and over the endless land like a breath of wind; and he took up the wand he uses to close men's eyes when he wants them closed, and to open them again out of sleep. And with that in his hand he flew.

He came down past Pieria and dropped out of the sky onto the sea, and went skimming over the waves like a shearwater — the bird that hunts down the terrible troughs of the barren salt water and soaks its close wings in the spray. He went along like that over the crowding waves.

And when he reached the island, which was very far away, he stepped up out of the violet-coloured sea onto dry land and walked until he came to the great cave where the nymph with the beautiful hair was living. And he found her at home.

---

There was a big fire burning on the hearth, and the smell of split cedar and juniper going up all over the island, and she was inside singing in a lovely voice, going up and down at her loom, weaving with a golden shuttle.

And round the cave the trees had grown up in a wood: alder, and black poplar, and sweet-smelling cypress. Long-winged birds roosted in them — owls and falcons and the long-tongued sea-crows, whose business is on the water. And a garden vine had been trained round the hollow of the cave itself, heavy and flourishing, with the grapes on it.

And four springs, close together, ran clear water — four of them, turned to run in four directions. And on both sides there were soft meadows of violet and wild parsley.

Even a god, coming there, would have stopped and looked at it and been glad.

And Hermes stood there and looked at it, and admired it, and then he went inside.

Now: I have described that at the length the poem describes it, which is a good deal of length for a poem in a hurry, and I want to be sure you have taken in what it is.

It is the most beautiful place in the entire book. There is nowhere else in twenty-four books — not Menelaus's house full of amber and ivory, not the orchards we are coming to, not Ithaca itself — that gets four springs and violets and a singing voice and a fire of cedarwood and a considered opinion that a god would stop to admire it.

And it is a prison. There is a man on the far side of it, on the rocks, who has been looking at the sea for seven years and would like to die.

That is a hard trick to bring off and the poem does it without saying a word about it. It simply builds the loveliest place it can build, and then walks you round the headland.

---

They knew each other on sight, gods always do. She sat him down in a shining chair and asked him why he had come, and — because she was a proper host, and this is a poem about hospitality — she fed him first: she set out ambrosia at his side and mixed the red nectar; and only when the messenger had eaten and satisfied himself did she ask her question.

"Hermes of the golden wand. Why have you come here? You are honoured and welcome, but you do not visit. Tell me what is in your mind. I want to do it, if I can do it, and if it is a thing that can be done. But first come and let me put something in front of you."

"You are a goddess asking a god," said Hermes. "I will tell you straight. Zeus told me to come. I did not want to. Who would want to cross that much salt water — you cannot even measure it — with no city of mortals anywhere on the way, nobody sacrificing to the gods, no choice cuts of meat? But there is no getting round the will of Zeus and no getting past it.

"He says you have a man here — the unluckiest of all the men who fought round the city of Priam (Troy) for nine years, and sacked it in the tenth, and started home, and offended Athena on the way out, so that she raised a bad wind and heavy seas against them. All the rest of his crew died there. The wind and the water carried this one here.

"And now Zeus tells you to send him off. Immediately. It is not his fate to die here, far from his own people. It is laid down that he sees them again, and his high-roofed house, and his own country."

And Calypso shuddered when she heard it, and answered him with wings on the words:

"You are cruel, you gods, and jealous past all others. You cannot bear it when a goddess sleeps with a man — when she takes one openly and makes him her husband.

"When Dawn of the rosy fingers took Orion, you gods who live easy could not leave it alone until pure Artemis of the golden throne went to Ortygia and killed him with her gentle arrows.

"And when Demeter of the beautiful hair gave way to her feeling and lay with Iasion in the ploughed field — three times turned — Zeus found out about it soon enough, and threw the white lightning at him and killed him.

"And now you gods begrudge me a mortal man staying beside me. A man I saved. He was alone, riding the keel of his ship, when Zeus split his fast vessel with the white lightning and smashed it in the middle of the wine-dark sea. All the rest of his crew died there; the wind and the water carried this one here. And I loved him and looked after him, and I told him I would make him immortal and ageless, forever.

"But since there is no getting round the will of Zeus and no getting past it, let him go — if that is what Zeus wants and orders — out onto the barren sea. I am not going to send him anywhere myself. I have no ships with oars and no companions to carry him over the wide back of the sea. But I will advise him honestly, and hide nothing, so that he gets home to his own country in one piece."[^2]

I have to give her the argument, because she has one.

She is quite right about the double standard, and the two examples she reaches for are the right examples. Gods take mortal women constantly, in this mythology, by every means including deception and force, and the world goes on. Goddesses who take mortal men find that their men have accidents. She is a minor immortal complaining to the messenger of the ruling one that the rules run one way, and she is not wrong.

She is also his jailer, and she is telling the story of it in a particular way. *I saved him. I loved him. I looked after him. I offered to make him a god.* Every word of that is true. And she has left out the seven years and the word *no*.

Both of those things are the case at once. If you want the poem to make one of them cancel the other, it will not do it for you.

---

Then she went to find him, because Zeus's message had gone into her.

And she found him sitting on the shore. His eyes were never dry. The sweetness of his life was running out of him while he grieved for his home, because the nymph did not please him any more. At night, it is true, he slept beside her in the hollow cave — unwilling beside the willing one. But by day he sat on the rocks and the sand, tearing himself with tears and grief and pain, and looked out over the barren sea, and wept.

And she came and stood near him and said:

"Poor man. Do not sit here grieving any longer. Do not waste your life away. I am going to send you off, and gladly.

"Come — cut long timbers with the bronze and build yourself a broad raft, and fix decking across it, high up, so that it can carry you over the misty sea. And I will put bread aboard, and water, and red wine, plenty of it, to keep the hunger off you; and I will put clothes on you; and I will send a wind behind you, so that you get home to your own country in one piece — if that is what the gods want, the ones who hold the wide sky, who are stronger than I am at planning and at bringing things off."

And the man who had endured everything shivered, and said:

"You have something else in mind, goddess, and it is not sending me home. You are telling me to cross the great gulf of the sea on a *raft* — a thing that is hard and frightening, that fast ships do not always manage even with a good wind out of Zeus.

"I am not getting on any raft unless you can bring yourself to swear me the great oath that you are not planning some new trouble for me."

And Calypso smiled at that, and stroked his hand with hers, and said his name, and said:

"You are a rogue, and there is nothing simple about you, and it says something about you that you should think of a thing like that to say.

"Very well. Let the Earth know this, and the wide Sky above, and the water of the Styx running down — which is the greatest and most terrible oath there is for the blessed gods — that I am not planning any new trouble for you.

"I am thinking, and I will advise you, exactly as I would plan for myself if I were in your position. I have a decent mind. The heart in me is not made of iron; there is pity in it."[^3]

---

And she turned and led the way, quickly, and he came on behind in the goddess's footsteps; and they came to the hollow cave, the god and the man, and he sat down in the chair Hermes had got up from, and she put every kind of food in front of him, the things mortal men eat and drink.

And she sat down facing him, and her women put ambrosia and nectar in front of *her*; and the two of them put out their hands to the good things laid ready.

And when they had eaten and drunk, Calypso began.

"Son of Laertes. Odysseus. Resourceful man.

"Is that really what you want, then? To go home now, to your own country? Straight away?

"Well. Goodbye, in spite of everything.

"But if you knew, in your own mind, how much suffering it is laid down for you to go through before you get to your own country, you would stay here with me, and keep this house, and you would be immortal. However much you want to see your wife — and I know you long for her, every day, all the time.

"And I do not think I come off worse than she does, in the body or the figure. It would hardly be decent for a mortal woman to compete with an immortal in looks."

And Odysseus, who had a great deal of sense, answered her.

"Lady, goddess — do not be angry with me over this.

"I know it perfectly well. All of it. I know that sensible Penelope is less than you to look at, when you set the two side by side. She is mortal, and you will never die and never grow old.

"And even so, what I want — what I am hungry for, every day — is to go home and see the day of my homecoming.

"And if one of the gods smashes me out there on the wine-dark sea, I will endure it. I have a heart in me that has taken a great deal of punishment already. I have suffered plenty and worked hard, on the waves and in the war. Let this be added to it."

---

Let's stop here for a moment.

This is the middle of the poem's argument and everything else is standing round it.

A goddess has offered a man immortality and eternal youth on the most beautiful island in the world, and she has offered it in good faith, and she is not lying about any part of it — not about her looks, not about the suffering coming, not about the fact that his wife is a mortal woman who will age and sicken and die, and that he will too, and much sooner.

And he says no. He said no for seven years, and now he says it out loud, with reasons.

But look at the *reasons*, because this is where people go wrong with this scene. He does not say that Penelope is more beautiful. He does not say that human love is deeper, or that mortal life is precious *because* it is short, or any of the fine things later writers have had him say. He agrees with Calypso. He says: yes, you are better looking, and yes, you are deathless, and I know what I am giving up.

He wants to go home anyway.

That is the whole of it. Not a philosophy — an appetite. He is hungry for a particular stony island, and a particular middle-aged woman, and his own smoke going up from his own roof, and he would rather have those and die than have paradise and not.

And notice the last thing he says, because it is the sentence this man is made of, and you should learn to hear it under everything else he says for the next nineteen books. He does not say he hopes to survive. He says: *if I am smashed, I will endure it. I have taken a lot. Let this be added to it.*

That is not courage in the way a young man understands courage. It is the thing that is left when courage has been used up and the man is still standing there. It is why the poem, which has a hundred words for cleverness, keeps calling him *the man who endured*.

---

The sun went down and darkness came on, and the two of them went into the innermost part of the hollow cave and took their pleasure of love, staying beside each other.

I will not smooth that line out and I will not pretend it is simple. Four paragraphs ago the poem told you that he lay beside her unwilling, beside a willing goddess, because there was nowhere else on the island to lie down. Now, on the last night, with the oath sworn and the leaving agreed and everything he was frightened of taken off the table, the poem says they took their pleasure of it. Both sentences are in the book. I do not think the poem is confused. I think it is describing seven years of something that had a great deal of coercion in it and was not made of nothing else, which is an uncomfortable and adult thing for a poem to notice, and it notices it without comment and moves on to the carpentry.

---

Because in the morning there is carpentry, and this is the first thing in the whole book that we see Odysseus actually *do*.

Four books we have been hearing about him. He is the cleverest man alive; he thought of the horse; he crawled into Troy in rags; he held a man's mouth shut in the dark. All hearsay, all in the past tense, all told by other people at dinner. And here he is at last, in the present tense, doing something — and what he does is *build a boat*, correctly, from felling to launching, in four days, and the poem tells you exactly how.

He put on the clothes she gave him. She gave him a great axe, bronze, sharpened on both edges, sitting well in the hands, with a good olive-wood handle fitted to it; and then a smoothing adze. And she led him to the far end of the island where the tall timber was — alder and poplar and fir going up to the sky, long seasoned and dry, that would float light for him. And when she had shown him where it grew she went back to her house.

And he started cutting. It went quickly. He brought down twenty trees and trimmed them with the bronze, and planed them expertly, and trued them to a chalked line. Then she brought him augers, and he bored every timber and fitted them to each other and hammered them home with pegs and joints.

He made the floor of it as broad as a man who knows the trade lays out the bottom of a wide merchant ship. He set up the decking, fitting it to close-set ribs, and finished it off with long gunwales down the sides. He made a mast, and a yard to fit it. And he made a steering oar to hold her on course. And he fenced the whole thing about with willow withies to keep the water off, and put in a good deal of loose ballast. And Calypso brought him cloth to make the sail with, and he made that expertly too. And he lashed on the braces and the halyards and the sheets. And then he levered her down on rollers into the bright sea.

Four days, and it was finished; and on the fifth she let him go.

Now — you may skip a passage like that. Most readers do; it reads like an instruction manual with the poetry switched off. I would like you not to skip it, for two reasons.

The first is that the audience did not skip it. These were island people. A very large number of the men listening had built or repaired a boat, and every one of them could hear the difference between a poet who knew what an adze was and a poet who did not. That passage is the poem showing its papers.

The second is more important. This man is about to spend nineteen books being famous for lying. He will invent five false lives, at least, and tell them beautifully to people who deserve better. It matters enormously that the first thing we ever watch him do with his own hands is make something true — measure it, line it, peg it, and make it hold.

Keep that in mind. He is a carpenter. It comes up again, a very long way from here, in a room he built himself, and it settles everything.

---

She bathed him and dressed him in sweet-smelling clothes, and put aboard a skin of dark wine, and a bigger one of water, and a leather bag of provisions with plenty of good things in it; and she sent him a following wind, warm and gentle. And he spread his sail to it, delighted, and sat down and steered with the oar, expertly, and sleep never fell on his eyelids.

He watched the Pleiades (star constellation), and Boötes going down late, and the Bear — which men also call the Wain — that turns round in one place and keeps a lookout on Orion, and is the only one that never bathes in the Ocean.

Calypso had told him to keep that one on his left hand as he crossed. And he did.[^4]

Seventeen days he sailed, and on the eighteenth the shadowy mountains of the Phaeacians' country came up, the nearest part of it to him, and it lay there on the misty sea like a shield.

And Poseidon came back from the Ethiopians.

He was a long way off, in the mountains of the Solymi, and he saw him — saw him out there on the sea, sailing — and the anger went all through him, and he shook his head and said to his own heart:

"Well. So the gods have changed their minds about Odysseus while I was away with the Ethiopians. He is nearly at the country of the Phaeacians, where it is laid down he escapes the great net of misery that has hold of him.

"But I think I can still drive him a good long way into trouble."

I want to be sure the timing has registered, because the poem has arranged it with real cruelty. Poseidon went off to the ends of the earth in the first hundred lines of Book One, and the entire rescue was scheduled into his absence. Twenty days were needed. He has come home on the eighteenth.

He gathered the clouds together and took his trident in his hands and stirred up the sea, and he called up every storm of every wind at once, and covered the land and the water together with cloud, and night came down out of the sky. The East Wind and the South Wind fell on each other, and the hard-blowing West, and the North that is born in the bright air and rolls a great wave in front of it.

And Odysseus's knees went, and his heart, and he said to himself:

"This is bad. What finally becomes of me?

"I am afraid the goddess told me nothing but the truth when she said I would have my fill of suffering at sea before I got home. All of it is coming true. Look at the sky. Zeus is roping the clouds round the whole of it; the sea is churning; every wind there is has jumped on me at once. Now I am certain to be destroyed.

"Three times, four times over, luckier were the Greeks who died back there on the wide plain of Troy, doing a favour to the sons of Atreus (Agememnon and Menaleus). I wish I had died there. I wish it had been me, on the day the Trojans in their crowds were throwing their bronze-headed spears at me over the body of Achilles. Then I would have had my rites, and the Greeks would have carried my name. As it is, it is laid down that I am taken by a miserable death."

And as he said it a great wave came down on him from above, terribly, driving, and spun the raft round; and he was thrown clear of the timbers, and the steering oar went out of his hands. The mast broke in the middle under the awful blast of the mixed winds, and the sail and the yard went far out into the water. And the wave held him under a long time. He could not get up quickly out from under the rush of it, because the clothes Calypso had given him weighed him down.

At last he came up, and spat the bitter brine out of his mouth, and it was running down off his head in streams. And even then, wrecked as he was, he did not forget his raft. He went after it through the waves and got hold of it and pulled himself into the middle of it and sat there, dodging the end of his life; and the big waves took it and carried it up and down the current — the way the North Wind in autumn drives thistles across a plain, and they stick together as they go.

---

But Ino saw him.

She was Cadmus's daughter, with the pretty ankles; and once she had been a mortal woman who spoke like one, and now she has a share of honour in the salt water and they call her Leucothea, the White Goddess. And she pitied him, wandering out there, in that much trouble.

She came up out of the water like a gull on the wing and settled on the raft, and said:

"Poor man. Why does Poseidon hate you so unspeakably, that he plants all this trouble for you?

"He will not finish you, though, however much he wants to.

"Now do this. You do not look like a fool. Take those clothes off, and let the raft go where the winds take it, and swim for it with your arms — strike out for the land of the Phaeacians, where it is laid down that you get out of this alive.

"Here. Take this veil, this immortal thing, and spread it under your chest, and then you need not be afraid of suffering or of dying. But when you get your hands on dry land, untie it and throw it back into the wine-dark sea, well away from the shore, and turn your own head away as you do it."

And she gave him the veil, and went back down into the surging water like a gull, and the black wave closed over her.[^5]

And the man who had endured everything thought it over, and did not like it.

"No. No — I am afraid some god is weaving me another trap, telling me to leave the raft. I am not doing it yet. That land she says I can escape to is a long way off; I saw it with my own eyes.

"Here is what I will do, and it seems the best plan. As long as the timbers hold in their joints, I will stay here and put up with it, whatever it costs. And when a wave finally smashes the raft to pieces, then I will swim; there will be nothing better to think of by then."

And while he was still turning that over, Poseidon raised a great wave, terrible and hard and arching over, and drove it down on him.

The way a strong wind hits a heap of dry chaff and scatters it every way at once — that is how it scattered the long timbers. And Odysseus got astride a single plank, riding it like a man riding a horse, and stripped off the clothes Calypso had given him and got Ino's veil round his chest and tied it, and put his arms out and threw himself head first into the sea to swim.

And the lord of the earthquake (Poseidon) saw him, and shook his head, and said: "Right. Now go and drift about like that, in real misery, until you fall in with people the gods have favoured. I do not think you will find your suffering small." And he lashed his horses and drove away to Aegae, where his famous house is.

And Athena, daughter of Zeus, thought of something else.

She stopped the tracks of all the other winds and told them to fall off and go to sleep, and she raised the fast North Wind and broke the seas down in front of him, so that he could reach the Phaeacians, who love the oar, and get away from death.

---

Two nights and two days he drifted on the heavy swell, and his own heart told him a great many times that he was going to die.

But when the third morning came, the wind fell and there was a windless calm; and he lifted up on a big wave and looked out, straining, and saw land close by.

And it came to him the way it comes to children when their father's life begins again — a man who has lain a long time in a sickness, wasting, with some hard spirit wearing away at him, and then, thank god, the gods let him go; and that is how welcome the land looked to Odysseus, and the trees on it. And he swam hard, driving to get his feet on solid ground.

But when he was as close in as a man's voice will carry, he heard the sea booming on the reefs. Great waves were roaring against the dry land and breaking, and everything was covered in spray; there were no harbours and no roadsteads for a ship, nothing but jutting headlands and rocks and cliffs.

And his knees went, and his heart, and he said to himself:

"So Zeus lets me see land I never hoped to see, and I have cut my way through all that water — and now there is no way out of the grey sea at all.

"Sharp rocks outside, and the surf howling round them, and the cliff going up sheer, and deep water close in with no place to stand on my two feet and get clear of it. If I try to climb out, a big wave will pick me up and throw me on the stone, and it will have been a wasted effort.

"And if I swim on further, looking for a beach that slopes, or a river mouth, I am afraid the storm will pick me up again, groaning as I go, and carry me back out into the fish-filled sea. Or some god will send a monster up at me out of the water. Amphitrite keeps a great many of those, and I know how much the famous Earthshaker hates me."

And while he was turning it over, a big wave carried him in at the rough shore. He would have been stripped of his skin and had his bones smashed, if Athena had not put it into his mind to grab at a rock with both hands as he came in; and he held on, groaning, until the great wave went by.

He got past that one. And then the backwash hit him as it came out, and struck him hard, and threw him far out to sea again.

And the way the pebbles come away stuck all over the suckers of a squid when it is dragged out of its hole — that is how the skin came off his strong hands on the rock. And the great wave closed over him.

Then and there the wretched man would have died before his time, if Athena had not given him a clear head. He came up out of the surge that was vomiting in against the land, and swam along outside it, watching the shore, hoping for a beach that sloped, or a river mouth.

And swimming, he came to the mouth of a river with a good current; and that seemed the best place to him, clear of rocks, and sheltered from the wind.

And he felt the water running out, and he prayed in his heart:

"Hear me, lord, whoever you are. I have come to you, as a man comes to what he has been praying for, running from the sea and from Poseidon's threats. Even the immortal gods respect a man who arrives as a wanderer — and that is what I am now, coming to your current and to your knees after a great deal of suffering. Pity me, lord. I claim to be your suppliant."

And the river checked his current, and held the wave back, and made calm water in front of him, and brought him safe into his own mouth.

And he came out with both his knees bent under him and his strong hands hanging, because the salt water had beaten the heart out of him. His whole body was swollen and the sea was running out of his mouth and his nostrils in streams. He lay there without breath and without a word, hardly able to move, and a terrible tiredness had come over him.

And when he got his breath back and the spirit came together in him again, he untied the goddess's veil and dropped it into the river running out to sea. A big wave carried it back down the current, and Ino caught it in her hands.

And he crawled up out of the water and lay down in the reeds and kissed the earth that gives men grain.

---

And then, because he was still in the world and the world does not stop, he had a new problem, and he put it to himself:

"What now? What finally becomes of me?

"If I keep watch by the river through this miserable night, I am afraid the hard frost and the wet dew together will finish me off — weak as I am, with the breath barely in me — and the wind off a river blows cold before dawn.

"And if I climb the slope into the dark wood and lie down in the thick brush, and the cold and the tiredness let go of me, and sweet sleep comes — I am afraid I become prey and a meal for wild animals."

And thinking it over, that seemed the better of the two; and he went up into the wood, and found a place near the water, in a clearing.

He crawled in under a pair of bushes growing out of the same spot: one of them a wild olive, one a grafted olive. No wet wind ever blew through them; the sun never struck through them with its rays; the rain never got through, they were grown together so close. And Odysseus crawled in under them and at once heaped himself a wide bed with his own hands, because there was a great drift of fallen leaves lying there — enough to cover two men or three, in the winter season, however hard the weather.

And when he saw it he was glad, and he lay down in the middle and poured the leaves over himself.

The way a man on a lonely farm, out at the edge of everything, with no neighbours, buries a burning log in the black embers and keeps the seed of the fire alive so that he does not have to go somewhere else to fetch it — that is how Odysseus lay covered over in the leaves.

And Athena poured sleep over his eyes and closed the lids of them, so that the exhaustion he had suffered would let go of him soonest.

## NOTES TO BOOK FIVE

[^1]: **Tithonus.** Dawn's husband, and a joke the poem does not stop to make, though it puts him in the first line of a book about a man refusing to be made immortal. Dawn fell in love with a mortal prince and asked Zeus to make him deathless, and Zeus granted exactly what she asked for — and she had forgotten to ask for youth. So Tithonus did not die. He got older, and older, and older, past strength, past sight, past speech, until in the end there was nothing left of him but a voice, and she shut him in a room, and he is in there still. The Greeks had a number of stories about people acquiring immortality and every single one of them is a horror story. It is worth carrying that into the cave with you when Calypso makes her offer.

[^2]: **Calypso, and what a nymph is.** Her name is built on the Greek verb *kalyptō*, to cover, to conceal, to hide away — the same root as the veil Ino gives him later in this book. She is, by name, the Concealer, and what she conceals is a man: for seven years nobody in the world, god or mortal, knew where Odysseus was. — As for what she is: a nymph is a minor female divinity attached to a place, a spring, a tree, a stretch of coast. Deathless, but not an Olympian; powerful in her own patch and nowhere else. Note what she cannot do. She cannot defy Zeus. She cannot build a ship. She cannot leave. She has spent seven years on an island in the middle of the sea with one man and no way of going anywhere either, and there is a reading of this book — not the only one, but a real one — in which two prisoners are talking to each other on that beach and only one of them knows it.

[^3]: **The oath by the Styx.** The Styx is a river of the underworld, and swearing by its water is the one oath even the gods cannot wriggle out of. Hesiod (playwright like Homer), who is precise about the penalties, says a god who swears falsely by it lies breathless and speechless for a year, cut off from nectar and ambrosia, and is then barred from the councils and the feasts of the other gods for nine more. — What matters here is that Odysseus demands it. A goddess who has just been ordered by the king of the gods to release him tells him he is free to go, and his response is to require a legally binding oath, and hers is to laugh and call him a rogue and give him one. That is the relationship in miniature. It is also, exactly, why he is going to survive the next nineteen books: he does not believe good news.

[^4]: **Steering by the Bear.** Greek sailing was mostly coastal and mostly in daylight and mostly in summer, hopping from headland to headland; there was no compass, no chart, and no instrument of any kind. What there was, was the sky. The constellation the poem calls the Bear or the Wain — our Plough, or Big Dipper — is circumpolar in these latitudes, which is what the poem means by saying it is the only one that never bathes in the Ocean: it wheels round the pole all night and never sets. So it is always in the north. And if you keep the north on your left hand, you are sailing east. That is the whole of Calypso's sailing direction, and it is a real one; a man could cross with it. Notice also that Odysseus does not sleep for seventeen days and nights. He is single-handing a small boat across open water with no crew to take a watch, and the poem knows precisely what that costs.

[^5]: **Ino, who is called Leucothea.** She had been a mortal woman: a princess of Thebes, sister to the mother of Dionysus, and she took the infant god in and nursed him — for which Hera drove her mad, and she went off a cliff into the sea with her own son in her arms, and the sea took her in and made her a goddess. She now spends her immortality pulling drowning men out of the water. Bear that in mind when a veil comes up out of the sea and a voice says *you need not be afraid of dying*; the poem has chosen, out of everyone available, the one divinity in the Mediterranean who knows from the inside what it is to go under. — And notice what Odysseus does with the help: he takes it, thanks nobody, and then refuses to follow the instructions until the raft is smashed out from under him. He is right, too. She told him to abandon a floating vessel two days from land. Being rescued by a goddess is not the same as being told the truth by one, and he has learned to tell the difference.
