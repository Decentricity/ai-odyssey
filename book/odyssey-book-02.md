# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK TWO
### In Which There Is a Meeting, and a Sign in the Sky, and a Ship Sails at Night

---

When Dawn came up, with her rosy fingers, Odysseus's dear son got out of bed.

I should say something about that dawn before we go any further, because you are going to meet her a great many times and I do not want you to think I have run out of ideas.

In the original, Dawn is a goddess with rose-red fingers, and she has them every single morning without exception, in this poem and in the other one, for the excellent reason that "rosy-fingered Dawn" is a block of six syllables that fits the metre exactly and can be dropped into the front of a line whenever the sun comes up. The sea is wine-dark. Ships are hollow. Athena is grey-eyed. Achilles is swift-footed even when he is sitting down sulking in a tent, which is most of the time. These are not lazy writing. They are the load-bearing timbers of a poem that had to be composed *while it was being performed*, out loud, in front of an audience, without a script; and a bard who had four hundred such phrases in his head, sorted by length, could build a line the way a mason builds a wall, reaching for the stone that fits the gap.[^1]

So: rosy-fingered. Every time. I shall use it when it seems to want using, and you may take it as a small ceremonial noise the poem makes at sunrise, like a clock chiming.

---

Telemachus got up and dressed, and slung a sharp sword over his shoulder and tied good sandals on his shining feet, and came out of his room looking, the poem says, very like a god; and he told the heralds to sound the call and summon the Greeks of Ithaca to assembly.

They sounded it, and the men came in from all over the town — slowly at first, then faster, because word got round, and word had something remarkable in it. There had not been an assembly on Ithaca since Odysseus sailed for Troy.

Twenty years. Think about what that means. It is not that the island had been badly governed; it is that it had not been governed at all. No court had sat, no dispute had been judged in public, no decision had been made in the hearing of the men it fell on. Whatever machinery Ithaca once had for the ordinary business of a community had simply been let go, and grass had grown up through it, and everyone had got used to that — and the hundred and eight young men in the great house had grown from boys into men in a country where no one had asked anybody anything in front of witnesses for two decades. That is not a coincidence. That is the soil the whole disaster grew in.[^2]

Telemachus went down to the meeting-place with a bronze spear in his hand and two quick dogs trotting at his heel. And Athena poured a supernatural grace over him as he came, so that the whole assembly stared as he walked in; and the elders made way for him, and he sat down in his father's seat.

The first to speak was an old, old man named Aegyptius, bent double with age and knowing a very great deal. He had had four sons. One farmed his fields at home. Two were sitting in that assembly among the suitors of Penelope, eating the estate of Odysseus with the rest. And the fourth had sailed with Odysseus to Troy in the hollow ships, and his name was Antiphus, and his father did not know what had become of him.

I am obliged to tell you what had become of him, because the poem's first audience knew it before Aegyptius opened his mouth, and the scene is built out of that knowledge. Antiphus was eaten. He was eaten alive in a cave by the Cyclops Polyphemus, in the dark, along with five of his shipmates — the very act that set Poseidon against Odysseus and is the reason none of these people have had a king for ten years past the end of the war. His father does not know. His father will never know. He is about to stand up and speak kindly to the son of the man who lost his boy, and there is nothing anyone can do about it, and the poem does not even pause. It simply lets you sit there holding it.

The old man leaned on his staff and said: "Listen to me, men of Ithaca. We have not held a session, we have not sat in assembly, since the day Odysseus went away in the hollow ships. Who has called us now? Which of the young men, or which of the older ones? Has he heard news of the army coming home, and does he mean to tell us plainly what he heard first? Or is it some other public matter he wants to raise? He seems to me a good man, and a lucky one. May Zeus bring to pass whatever good thing he has in mind."

Telemachus was very glad to hear that, and did not sit still long. He stood up in the middle of them all, and a herald put the speaker's staff into his hand, and he turned first to the old man.

"Sir, he is not far off — you are about to see him, and yes, I am the one who called you, and yes, I am in more pain than any man here. No, I have not heard news of the army coming home, and I have no other public business to raise. This is my own trouble. Two calamities have fallen on my house.

"The first is that I have lost my father — who was once *your* king, and a gentle one, like a father to every one of you. The second is worse, and it is the ruin of my whole house and everything I have. Men are courting my mother against her will — and they are the sons of the very men sitting in this assembly. They will not go to her father Icarius's house and ask him for her properly, and let him settle the bride-gifts and give his daughter to whichever man he likes best. Instead they come to my house, day after day, every day, killing my cattle and my sheep and my fat goats, feasting and drinking my bright wine, and there is no end to it. There is no man in the house like Odysseus to drive this ruin off. I cannot do it. I am not that man, not yet — and I know exactly how little I can do, and so do they.

"I tell you I would defend myself if I had the strength. This has gone beyond bearing. My house is being destroyed in a way that is not decent. Be angry, you men. Be ashamed in front of the neighbours who live round about. Be afraid of the gods, in case they turn on you in disgust at what is being done. I beg you, by Zeus and by Themis, who breaks up assemblies and calls them into session — *stop*, my friends, and leave me alone with my bitter grief. Unless — unless my father, good Odysseus, ever did some deliberate harm to the Greeks, and you are all paying me back for it out of spite. I would rather it were you eating my stores and my cattle. If you ate them I might at least be paid for them one day: I could go up and down the town pressing my claim until I got my property back. As it is you fill me with a hurt I have no way to answer."

And in a rage he threw the staff on the ground, with tears breaking out of him — and pity went through the whole assembly.

Everyone sat quite silent. No man had the heart to answer Telemachus harshly.

Except Antinous.

---

"Telemachus. That is a big speech, and an unbridled one, and you should be ashamed of yourself, trying to fix the blame on us. Your trouble is not the Greek suitors. It is your own mother, who is a past master at this sort of thing.

"Three years it has gone on — no, this is the fourth — and the whole time she has been leading us on. She holds out hope to every man of us, she sends each one private messages and encouragement, and what she is thinking is nothing like what she says.

"And here is the trick she pulled. She set up a great loom in the hall, and started weaving a wide fine web, and she said to us: *Young men, my suitors — Odysseus is dead. Be patient; do not press me to marry, however eager you are, until I finish this cloth, or all my thread will be wasted. It is a shroud for the lord Laertes (Odysseus's father), for the day when the pitiless end of death takes him. I will not have the women of this country talking against me — that a man who won so much lay dead with no proper cloth to cover him.*

"That is what she said, and we agreed, because how could we not? So she wove at the great web in the daytime — and at night, by torchlight, she unravelled it. Three years she got away with it. She fooled the Greeks. And in the fourth year, when the seasons had come round again, one of her women told us — one who knew all about it — and we caught her in the act of pulling out the beautiful cloth. So she had to finish it, whether she liked it or not.

"Now here is our answer to you, and you may hear it, and so may all the Greeks. Send your mother away. Tell her to marry whichever man her father chooses and whichever man pleases her. But if she goes on tormenting the sons of the Greeks much longer, on the strength of the gifts Athena has given her — the beautiful handwork, and the excellent mind, and the tricks, which nobody in the old stories ever had the like of — then know this: we will go on eating your living and your goods for exactly as long as she keeps to this plan of hers. She is winning a great name for herself. You are losing a great deal of property. We are not going back to our own lands, or anywhere else, until she marries one of us."

Now.

I ask you to look at what has just been handed to us, because it is the finest single piece of characterisation in the first quarter of this poem, and it comes out sideways, in a hostile speech, from a man who thinks he is complaining.

For three years — three years — a woman with no legal power, no army, no husband and no adult son held off a hundred and eight armed men *with a piece of sewing*. And notice the shape of the trick, because the shape is everything. She did not refuse them; a refusal could be overruled. She agreed. She simply attached a condition to the agreement so pious that no man in Greece could object to it without looking like a monster in front of his neighbours: I am weaving a burial cloth for my husband's elderly father, so that when he dies he will not lie uncovered and shame us all. Every man there had to nod. Every man there had to wait. And she used the one activity that was so entirely and unremarkably *women's work* that not one of them thought to check on it — for three years — and every night she picked out by torchlight what she had put in by daylight, so that the work went on forever and never grew.

Do you remember what I told you Athena was the goddess of? War, and weaving, and cleverness. Penelope, at her loom, in the dark, is all three at once.

And I want you to hold on to something else. This poem is about a man famous above all other men for being cunning, who lies his way across the entire known world; and we have not properly met him yet; and the first great feat of deception in the whole book belongs to his wife. They have been apart for twenty years, and they are still doing the same thing. That is not a detail. That is the marriage. Keep it in mind for the very end of the story, when the two of them are finally in a room together, and one of them sets a trap and the other one walks straight into it.

One thing more, and it is not a pleasant one. Antinous says a woman betrayed her — one of her own household, one who knew all about it. That is a small line, and it goes past very fast, and it will be paid for horribly. Remember that a maid told.[^3]

---

Telemachus answered him.

"Antinous, I cannot possibly throw the mother who bore me and brought me up out of my house against her will — not while my father is somewhere else in the world, alive or dead. And if I sent her back to Icarius of my own accord, I would have to pay her father back a great deal, and it would go hard with me.[^4] I would suffer at his hands, and the gods would give me worse, because my mother would call down the terrible Furies on me as she left the house — and I would be despised by every man alive. That word will never be spoken by me.

"If your own hearts are troubled by this, then get out of my house. Feast somewhere else. Eat your own goods, and take it in turns, going from one man's house to another. But if you think it better and more profitable to ruin one man's living and never pay for it — go on. And I will call on the gods who live forever, and see whether Zeus will let there be a settling. And you will die in this house, and there will be no payment taken for any of you."

That is the second time he has said that last sentence, and it is not a young man's bluster. It is very nearly a legal formula: it means *you will be killed lawfully, as men killed in the wrong, and no blood-price and no vengeance will be owed for you.* He is not threatening them. He is describing, quite accurately, what is going to happen in this hall in about twenty books' time, and he does not know it yet.

And Zeus, who arranges these things, sent two eagles.

They came down off a mountain peak and flew a long while on the wind, wing to wing, sailing side by side; and when they were exactly over the middle of the assembly, over all those upturned faces, they wheeled and beat their wings hard and glared down at every head, and there was death in their eyes. And then they clawed at each other's faces and necks, tearing, and swung away to the right, out over the houses of the town, and were gone.

The men stared after them, appalled, and turned it over in their minds, wondering what was coming.

And an old man named Halitherses stood up — much the best of his generation at reading birds and saying what they meant — and he said:

"Listen to me, men of Ithaca, and let me speak chiefly to the suitors, because this is rolling towards them. Odysseus is not going to be away from his people much longer. I think he is somewhere very close by at this moment, and that he is sowing the seed of death and doom for every man here — and it will not stop with them, either. A great many of the rest of us who live in bright Ithaca will suffer for it too.

"So let us think, well ahead of time, how we may put a stop to this — or rather, let *them* stop it themselves, which would be much the better way, and they had better be quick about it. I am not a beginner at prophecy; I know what I am talking about. Everything I said about that man is coming true, exactly as I said it would on the day the Greeks sailed for Troy and clever Odysseus went with them. I said he would suffer terribly, and lose all his companions, and come home in the twentieth year, unknown to everybody. And here we are."

And Eurymachus said, smoothly:

"Go home, old man. Go home and prophesy to your own children, before something unpleasant happens to them.

"I am a great deal better at reading this than you are. There are any number of birds flying about under the sun, and by no means all of them mean anything. Odysseus is dead, far away, and I only wish you had died with him — then we would not have to sit through all this prophesying of yours, and you would not be stirring up a boy who is already angry, in the hope that he will make you a present out of gratitude.

"Now, here is what I say, and it will happen. If you — an old man who knows better — talk this youngster round into losing his temper, it will be the worse for *him*, first of all, and he will still not be able to do a thing about any of it. And you, sir, we shall fine, and you will pay it, and it will hurt.

"And here is my advice to Telemachus, in front of everyone. Tell your mother to go back to her father's house. They will arrange the wedding and settle the very large bride-gifts that ought to go with a daughter people are fond of. I do not believe the Greeks will leave off this hard courtship until then. We are not afraid of anybody, and certainly not of Telemachus, however many speeches he makes. And we take no notice of prophecy — the sort of thing you dribble out, old man, that comes to nothing and only makes people hate you more."

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to say that the difference between Halitherses and Eurymachus is that one believes in omens and the other does not, and that the poem is on the side of the believer. That is nearly right but not quite. Everyone in that assembly believed in omens; the question was always *what a given omen meant*, and men argued about that the way men argue about evidence. What condemns Eurymachus is not scepticism. It is that he does not actually offer a reading. He announces that most birds mean nothing, threatens the old man's children, promises to fine him, and repeats what he wanted anyway. He is not weighing the sign. He is refusing to be interrupted. There is a particular kind of man who calls that clear thinking, and the poem has his number.

---

Telemachus had stopped arguing.

"Eurymachus — and the rest of you proud gentlemen — I am done pleading, and I will say no more about it. The gods know, and all the Greeks know. Just give me a fast ship and twenty men to work her, and I will go up and down the sea-lanes. I am going to Sparta and to sandy Pylos to ask about my father's homecoming, and how long he has been gone. Some man may tell me. I may hear a rumour out of Zeus, which is how news mostly gets about.

"If I hear my father is alive and on his way home, then for all my weariness I will hold out one more year. And if I hear that he is dead and gone, then I will come back to my own country and heap up a mound for him, and burn the offerings on it, all that is proper and in full measure — and I will give my mother to a husband."

And he sat down. And Mentor stood up.

Mentor was an old friend of Odysseus's — the friend, in fact, to whom Odysseus had handed over the whole household on the day he sailed, with instructions to keep everything in order and obey the old man Laertes. (You will recall from Book One's notes that this is the name Athena is going to borrow, and the name our word *mentor* comes from. That is still true. This is the actual man.)

And he did not attack the suitors. Please notice this, because it is the moral centre of the entire book and it is very easy to skim past.

"Listen to me, men of Ithaca.

"Let no sceptred king from this day on be kind, or gentle, or fair-minded, or have any justice in his heart at all. Let him be harsh, and let him do wicked things — since not one man remembers Odysseus among the people he ruled, and he was as gentle to them as a father.

"I do not particularly grudge the proud suitors doing violent things out of the wickedness of their own minds. They are staking their own heads on it when they eat that man's house down and tell each other he will never come back.

"It is the *rest of you* I am angry with — the way you all sit there, saying nothing. You are many. They are few. You have never once opened your mouths to hold them back."

And a man called Leocritus answered him:

"Mentor, you troublemaker, you lunatic — what are you talking about? Setting them on to stop us? It is a hard thing to fight men over a dinner, even when you outnumber them. Why, if Odysseus of Ithaca himself came home this minute and found us feasting in his hall, and set his heart on driving us out of it — his wife would get no joy of his coming, however much she has wanted it. He would meet a very ugly end, taking on so many single-handed. You are talking nonsense.

"Come on — break it up, all of you, and go back to work. Mentor and Halitherses can see the boy off on his journey; they were friends of his father's, after all. Though I think he will sit at home a good long while yet, getting his news in Ithaca, and never make that trip at all."

And with that he dissolved the assembly, and they went off to their houses, and the suitors went to the house of Odysseus.

---

Telemachus went away by himself, down to the edge of the sea, and washed his hands in the grey salt water, and prayed.

"Hear me — you who came to my house yesterday, whoever you are, and told me to take a ship and go over the misty sea to ask about my father's homecoming. The Greeks are blocking every part of it, and the suitors most of all, in their arrogance."

And Athena came and stood beside him in the shape and the voice of Mentor, and said:

"Telemachus. You will not be a coward after this, or a fool, if you have any drop of your father's spirit in you — and he was a man who finished what he said he would do. This journey of yours will not come to nothing.

"But if you were *not* his son, and Penelope's, then I would have no hope of you finishing anything. Few sons turn out to be their fathers' equals. Most are worse. A very few are better. But you are not going to be a coward or a fool, and Odysseus's wits have not entirely failed to reach you, so there is every reason to think you will bring this off.

"Never mind what the suitors are plotting. There is no sense in them, and no justice either, and they have no idea that death is standing next to them and that they are all going to die on one day. As for your journey, it will not be delayed long. I was your father's friend, and I will find you a fast ship and come with you myself. Go home now, and mix with the suitors, and get provisions ready — wine in jars, and barley meal, the strength of men, in stout skins. I will go through the town and raise a crew of volunteers. There are plenty of ships in sea-girt Ithaca, new and old, and I will find the best of them for you, and we will get her fitted out and launched on the open sea."

And Telemachus went home.

---

He found the suitors in the courtyard, skinning goats and singeing pigs. And Antinous came straight over to him, laughing, and took him by the hand, and said his name, and said:

"Telemachus, you great orator, you unstoppable force — no more hard words and no more hard feelings. Eat and drink with us the way you used to. The Greeks will see to all of this for you: a ship and a picked crew, so that you can get to sacred Pylos quickly and hear about your noble father."

And Telemachus took his hand out of Antinous's hand.

"Antinous, there is no way in the world I can have a quiet dinner with you people, or take my ease, or enjoy myself. Was it not enough that you wore away all my best possessions while I was still a child? Now I am grown, and I have listened to what other men say, and there is a spirit rising in me — and I am going to try to send the black spirits of death down on you. I will go to Pylos, or I will go about it here in this country. I am going, and the journey I am talking about will not be for nothing — as a passenger, since I have no ship and no rowers of my own. I suppose you found that arrangement more convenient."

And he pulled his hand lightly out of Antinous's hand, easily, in front of everybody.

The suitors, meanwhile, were setting out the meal, and jeering at him, and needling him, and one of the insolent young men would say something like this:

"I do believe Telemachus is planning to murder us. He'll bring back allies from sandy Pylos, or from Sparta — he's frightfully keen on it. Or perhaps he'll get as far as Ephyra, that rich country, and bring back poison to drop in the wine-bowl and finish the lot of us."

And another would say:

"Who knows? He may go off in his hollow ship and die a long way from his friends, wandering about like his father did. That would make more work for us — we'd have to divide up all his property, and give the house to his mother, and to whichever of us marries her."

That is what they said, to a young man's face, in his own house, about his father, and about his own death. And then they went in to dinner.

---

But Telemachus went down to his father's storeroom.

It was high-roofed and wide, and it held gold and bronze in heaps, and clothing folded in chests, and quantities of fragrant olive oil. And there were great jars standing along the wall in rows against the wall, full of old sweet wine, unmixed, waiting — the god's own drink — kept there in case Odysseus ever came home after all his suffering. The doors were double, close-fitted, double-locked. And the housekeeper was in there day and night, watching over everything with a very shrewd mind indeed: Eurycleia.[^5]

He called her into the storeroom and said:

"Nurse, draw off some wine for me into jars — sweet wine, the best there is after the vintage you keep for my poor lord, in case he comes home from somewhere, having got out from under death. Fill twelve, and fit them all with lids. And pour me out barley meal into well-sewn bags — twenty measures of milled barley. And keep this to yourself. Have it all put together, because I shall come for it in the evening, once my mother has gone upstairs and is thinking about her bed. I am going to Sparta and to sandy Pylos, to see if I can hear anything about my dear father coming home."

And the old nurse cried out and burst into tears.

"Dear child — how did *this* get into your head? Where are you going, an only son and a beloved one, over all that country? He is dead, that man, far from home, in some place we do not know. And the moment you turn your back these people will start plotting, and you will die by a trick, and they will divide up everything you own. Stay here and sit on your own property. There is no need for you to suffer and wander about on the barren sea."

"Courage, nurse. There is a god behind this plan. But swear to me that you will say nothing to my dear mother — not until eleven days have gone by, or twelve, or until she misses me herself and hears that I have gone. I do not want her spoiling her lovely skin with crying."

So the old woman swore the great oath of the gods, and swore it properly; and then she drew off the wine into the jars, and poured the barley meal into the well-sewn bags; and Telemachus went back into the hall and mixed with the suitors.

And Athena thought of something else.

She went through the town in the likeness of Telemachus, and stood beside each man she wanted, and told him what to do, and said: be at the fast ship this evening. And she asked Noemon, son of Phronius, for a swift ship, and he was glad to give it.

The sun went down and the streets grew dark. Then she ran the fast ship down into the sea and put in her all the gear that well-benched ships carry, and moored her at the far end of the harbour; and the good crew gathered round her, and the goddess put heart into every one of them.

And Athena thought of one thing more. She went to the house of Odysseus, and there she poured sweet sleep over the suitors — sent them wandering as they drank, and knocked the cups out of their hands. They did not sit long. They got up and went off through the town to find their beds, because sleep was falling on their eyelids and they could not keep them open.

Then grey-eyed Athena called Telemachus out of the great hall — in Mentor's shape, with Mentor's voice.

"Telemachus. Your crew are sitting at the oars already, waiting for you to start. Come on. Let us not hold up the journey any longer."

And she led the way, quickly, and he came on behind her, in her footsteps.

They came down to the sea and to the ship, and found the long-haired crew on the shore, and Telemachus said: "Come on, friends — let us fetch the provisions. Everything is stacked and ready in the hall. My mother knows nothing about it, and neither do the servants. Only one woman was told."

And he led the way, and they came behind, and they carried it all down and stowed it in the ship, the way Odysseus's dear son directed.

Then Telemachus went aboard, and Athena went first and sat down in the stern, and Telemachus sat beside her. The crew cast off the stern cables and climbed in and sat down at the oars.

And grey-eyed Athena sent them a following wind — the fresh West Wind, singing over the wine-dark sea.

Telemachus called out to his men and told them to lay hold of the tackle, and they heard him and did it. They raised the pine mast and stepped it in the hollow box and made it fast with forestays, and hauled up the white sail with twisted oxhide ropes; and the wind bellied out the middle of the sail, and the dark wave sang loud along the stem as the ship went, and she ran along on the swell, making her way.

Then they made the running-gear fast in the black swift ship, and set up mixing bowls brimming with wine, and poured out an offering to the gods who live forever and always — most of all, and above all, to the grey-eyed daughter of Zeus.

And all night long, and into the dawn, the ship ran on and cut her way.

## NOTES TO BOOK TWO

[^1]: **Formulas.** In the 1930s a young American scholar named Milman Parry went to what was then Yugoslavia and recorded illiterate village singers performing enormously long heroic poems from memory, and discovered that they did it exactly this way — by assembling stock phrases of fixed metrical shape, improvising the joins. It settled an argument that had run for centuries. The repeated epithets in Homer are not decorative and they are not clumsy: they are the technology of oral composition, and to a listening audience they were not tiresome at all but reassuring, the way a returning chorus is reassuring. They are also, in translation, largely untranslatable in their original function, since English prose has no metre to fill. I keep the best of them for their flavour and let the rest go.

[^2]: **The assembly.** Not a parliament; nobody voted, and nothing was carried by a majority. It was a gathering of the free adult men of a community, called by whoever had the standing to call it, in which a man who held the speaker's staff had the right to be heard without interruption, and in which grievances were aired in public and pressure was applied by shame. Its power was entirely a matter of opinion — which sounds weak until you remember that these were small societies where a man's standing among his neighbours was the only capital he had. That is precisely why Mentor's speech later in the book lands where it does. The assembly's one real weapon was collective disapproval, and Ithaca had spent three years declining to use it.

[^3]: **The shroud.** A body was washed, dressed, and wrapped in cloth before burning or burial; to leave a great man uncovered was a public disgrace that fell on his family. Weaving that cloth for one's father-in-law was an act of conspicuous piety, and it also fell squarely within the work a noblewoman was expected to do — indeed the work she was *praised* for doing. This is why the trick is a masterpiece rather than merely a delay: Penelope hid three years of resistance inside the most approved behaviour available to her, and the suitors policed everything about her except the one activity they thought was harmless. — As for the woman who told: the poem will come back to this. When the reckoning arrives, it does not fall only on the suitors. Twelve of the household women are killed for having gone over to them, and the manner of it is the coldest passage in the book. I mention it now, a long way off, because I do not want it to arrive as a surprise, and because I would rather you carried the question with you through everything in between: whether these women were traitors, or captives with no good options, or both. The poem is quite sure. I am not.

[^4]: **Why sending Penelope home would ruin him.** Marriage in this world involved property moving in both directions — gifts from the suitor's family to the bride's, and a settlement from the bride's father with her. If Telemachus sent his mother back to Icarius on his own initiative, rather than her leaving of her own accord, he would owe her father substantial restitution — and he has no cattle left to pay it with, because the suitors have eaten them. They have engineered a situation where the one thing they demand is the one thing he cannot afford. He is being asked to solve his problem by bankrupting himself.

[^5]: **The storeroom.** Notice what wealth *is* here: no coins, because coinage had not been invented yet. Wealth was bronze and gold by weight, fine cloth, oil, wine, and above all livestock — which is why "eating the estate" is not a figure of speech but a precise description of what is happening to this family. It also explains why great households ran on gift-exchange rather than purchase, and why hospitality-gifts (see Book One, note 7) were serious economics and not just good manners. And it is why the locked room matters: a house's whole worth stood in jars against a wall, behind a double door, with one trusted old woman holding the key.

*Book Three: In Which an Old Man Talks a Great Deal, and Some of It Is Useful.*
