# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK THREE
### In Which an Old Man Talks a Great Deal, and Some of It Is Useful

---

The sun came up out of the beautiful water and into the brazen sky, to shine on the gods who never die and on men who do, on the grain-giving fields of earth; and the ship came in to Pylos, the well-built citadel of Neleus.

And there were four and a half thousand men on the beach, killing bulls.

That is not a figure of speech and I have not exaggerated it. They were sitting on the shore in nine great companies, five hundred men to a company, and each company had brought nine bulls, and they were sacrificing them to Poseidon of the dark hair — eighty-one black bulls, and the fires going, and the smell of it out over the water; and Telemachus of Ithaca came sailing in out of the dawn straight into the middle of the largest religious ceremony he had ever seen in his life.

You should understand what was actually happening on that beach, because *sacrifice* is one of those words that has gone strange on us. It sounds like waste — like good meat burned up and thrown away to buy favour from heaven. It was very nearly the opposite.

Here is the arrangement. When an animal was killed, the thigh bones were cut out and wrapped in a double fold of fat, with a few raw scraps laid on top, and burned on the altar with wine poured over them; and the smoke went up, and that was the gods' portion. Everything else — all the meat, every edible ounce of it — was roasted on the spot and eaten by the people standing there. That is what those five hundred men to a company were doing. They were having dinner.

In this world you did not, as a rule, simply slaughter an animal and eat it. Killing was a religious act, and a communal one, and the meal *was* the ceremony. The god got the smoke and the bones. The men got the beef.

And the Greeks knew perfectly well that this was a strange sort of bargain, and they had a story to account for it, in which the trickster Prometheus divided up the first ox and hid the good meat inside the ugly stomach-bag, and dressed the bare bones up in glistening fat, and invited Zeus to choose; and Zeus chose the fat, and got the bones, and has been getting them ever since. Whether he was fooled or was pretending to be fooled is a question the story leaves carefully open — and the answer, I think, is that when you are telling a story about why you get to eat all the steak, it is best to be a little vague about whose idea it was.[^1]

---

Athena, in the shape and the voice of Mentor, told him to get on with it.

"There is no need to be shy now, not for a moment. You crossed all that water for exactly one reason — to find out about your father — so go straight up to Nestor the horseman and ask him. He will not lie to you. He is far too sensible for that."

And Telemachus said, and I love him for it:

"Mentor — how am I to go up to him? How do I even *greet* him? I have no practice at making elegant speeches, and it is embarrassing for a young man to question an older one."

That is one of the truest lines in the poem. He has spent two books discovering his courage. He has faced down a hall of armed men and thrown the speaker's staff on the ground in front of the whole island. And now he has to walk up to a distinguished stranger and introduce himself, and he would rather do almost anything else. Courage and social nerve are different muscles, and nobody has both at twenty.

"Telemachus," said the goddess, "some of it you will think of yourself, and a god will put the rest into your head. I do not believe you were born and brought up without the gods' goodwill."

And she went off ahead of him, quickly, and he came on behind in the god's own footsteps.

---

They came up to the crowd of Pylian men sitting in their companies, and there was Nestor with his sons around him, and his people busy roasting meat and running spits through it. And when they saw strangers coming, the whole party got up together and waved them in and sat them down.

The first to reach them was Nestor's son Peisistratus, who took them both by the hand and settled them on soft fleeces on the sand between his father and his brother Thrasymedes; and he gave them helpings of the inner meat and poured wine into a golden cup and put it into Athena's hands first — because she looked the elder of the two, and that is how it was done — and said:

"Say your prayer to lord Poseidon, stranger, since you have come in on his feast. And when you have poured your offering and prayed, hand the cup to your friend so that he can pour as well. He is a young man, about my own age, and I imagine he prays like the rest of us."

I hope somebody is enjoying this as much as I am. A well-brought-up young man has just handed a golden cup to the goddess Athena and invited her to pray to Poseidon — who is the one god in the world who wants Odysseus dead, whose feast this is, and who is at this precise moment at the far ends of the earth so that Athena can get on with the rescue behind his back.

And she takes the cup. And she prays. She prays graciously and at length for glory to Nestor and his sons, and for a good return for the men of Pylos; and last of all, quietly, at the end, that Telemachus might get what he came for and go home.

She was, as I have said, the goddess of doing the sensible thing well.

---

They ate; and when they had eaten as much as they wanted, old Nestor started the talking, and asked at last who his guests were. Not before. Never before. You feed a man and then you ask his name.[^2]

"Now is the proper moment to enquire, now that our guests have had their dinner. Strangers — who are you? Where do you sail from over the sea-roads? Are you here on business? Or are you cruising at random, like pirates, who wander about risking their own lives and making trouble for everybody else's?"

Telemachus took his courage in both hands — Athena had put it there — and answered.

"Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Greeks. You ask where we come from, and I will tell you. We are out of Ithaca, and my errand is my own, not the state's. I am looking for word of my father, if there is any to be had anywhere: Odysseus, that great heart, who they say fought beside you once and sacked the city of Troy.

"Every other man who went to that war, we know about. We know where each of them died; we have their names. But this one Zeus has made unaccountable, and nobody can tell me where he went down — whether on land, beaten under by enemies, or somewhere out at sea in the waves.

"So I have come to your knees, in the hope that you will tell me how he died: whether you saw it yourself, or heard it from some other man wandering. His mother bore him for suffering, if anyone ever was. And do not soften it out of kindness or pity. Tell me plainly what you saw. I beg you — if my father ever promised you anything and made it good, in that country of the Trojans where we Greeks suffered so much, remember it now, and tell me the exact truth."

And Nestor took a breath.

---

Now: I must prepare you.

Nestor talks. He is the oldest of all the men who went to Troy; he has ruled through three generations of men; he has outlived nearly everyone he ever fought beside; and he cannot answer a direct question without going round by way of the entire war. In the other poem, the *Iliad*, he is famous for it, and the younger heroes can be seen visibly bracing themselves when he stands up. He is the sort of old man who says *that reminds me* and means it, and who will get to your point eventually, having said four useful things and eleven charming ones on the way.

And I want to defend him, because it is easy to find him funny and much better to find him necessary.

There was no writing worth speaking of in this world. No records, no dispatches, no lists of the dead. Everything anybody knew about a ten-year war fought overseas by men out of a hundred scattered kingdoms was stored in the memories of the men who came back, and nowhere else at all. When a man like Nestor died, the things that only he knew went out like a lamp.

That is why this journey is worth four books of a poem. Telemachus is not going to Pylos to interview a witness. He is going to the library. The library is ninety years old, has a shaky sense of what is relevant, and will not stop talking; and it is the only one there is.

So. Nestor told him about the war. About how they suffered there, the unmanageable ones, ranging the misty sea after plunder wherever Achilles led them, and fighting round the great city of Priam (last king of troy). And how the best men died there. *Ajax lies there, and Achilles, and Patroclus, who gave counsel like the gods themselves — and my own son, Antilochus, faultless and strong, a great runner and a fighter. And we suffered a great deal besides. No mortal man could tell the whole of it. If you stayed five years, six years, asking me what the Greeks went through there, you would give up and go home before I had finished.*

"For nine years we worked at them with every trick we could invent, and the god barely let us finish it. And in all that time no man tried to match himself against Odysseus in cunning, because your father won every time — if you really are his son. I am astonished, looking at you. Even the way you talk is his; you would not think a young man could sound so like him. In all those years he and I never once spoke on opposite sides, in assembly or in council. We were of one mind, and we gave the Greeks the best advice we had.

"But when we had sacked the steep city of Troy, and gone down to the ships, and a god scattered the Greeks — then Zeus planned a wretched homecoming for them, because not all of them had been just or sensible; and a great many came to grief through the deadly anger of the grey-eyed daughter (Athena) of the mighty Father.[^3]

"She set the two sons of Atreus quarrelling (Agamemnon and Menelaus). They called the whole army to assembly — carelessly, out of order, at sunset, and the Greeks came in heavy with wine — and each of them made his case. Menelaus (king of Sparta) said everyone should sail home at once. Agamemnon (king of Mycenae) would not have it: he wanted to hold the army there and burn sacrifices until Athena's terrible anger softened. The fool. He did not know that she would not be turned; the mind of the everlasting gods does not change so quickly. So the two of them stood there trading hard words, and the army sprang up with a noise like the sea, and the whole thing split in two.

"We slept that night thinking bitter things about one another. In the morning half of us hauled our ships down and put our goods aboard and sailed — and Odysseus with us, all his men, and then he turned back and sided with Agamemnon after all. As for me, I ran, with everything I had, because I could see a god was cooking up trouble.

"And we came home, some of us. Diomedes got his men back. Menelaus caught us up at Lesbos. And I came home without ever learning which of the others made it and which did not; but I can tell you what I have heard since, sitting in my own house, as is proper.

"They say the Myrmidons came home safe under Achilles's son. Philoctetes got home. Idomeneus brought back to Crete every man of his who came out of the fighting alive; the sea took none of them.

"And Agamemnon — well. Even living as far off as you do, you will have heard how he came home, and how Aegisthus killed him, and paid for it miserably. It is a fine thing when a murdered man leaves a son (Orestes) behind him. That one took his revenge on the murderer, on the crafty Aegisthus, who had killed his famous father.

"And you, my friend — and you are tall, and you look well — be brave, so that men born long after you speak your name kindly."


---

"Nestor, son of Neleus, great glory of the Greeks," said Telemachus, "yes — he took his revenge, and the Greeks will carry his name a long way, and singers will make a song of it for men to come. I only wish the gods would put strength like that round *me*, so that I could pay the suitors back for the outrages they commit, and the arrogant things they invent against me. The gods have spun out no such happiness for my father or for me. There is nothing to do now but endure it."

"Well, my friend, since you bring it up," said Nestor, "they do say a great crowd of men are courting your mother in your own house against your will, and doing you harm. Tell me — are you letting it happen? Have the people of your country turned against you, following some voice from a god? Who knows: he may yet come home and pay them out for all that violence, alone, or with the whole force of the Greeks behind him.

"If grey-eyed Athena would only choose to look after you the way she looked after glorious Odysseus in the country of the Trojans, where we Greeks suffered — I never in my life saw the gods show their love so openly as Pallas Athena stood by that man — if she would care for you like that, and take it to heart, some of those gentlemen might stop thinking about marriage altogether."

"Sir," said Telemachus, "I do not think that will ever happen. It is too much. It frightens me even to talk about it. I could not hope for such a thing, not even if the gods wanted it."

And grey-eyed Athena said: "Telemachus. What a thing to let past your teeth. A god can bring a man home safe from a long way off, easily, whenever he chooses."

---

But Telemachus wanted the other story, and asked for it: how Agamemnon died, and where Menelaus had been all that while, and how Aegisthus ever dared it, killing a man so much better than himself.

And Nestor told him.

This is the third time in three books that we have been given this story (of Orestes and Aegisthus), and each time it has come a little closer and grown a little more detail — first as an example Zeus tossed out at dinner, then as a threat and an encouragement from a disguised goddess, and now at last in full, as history, from a man who lived through the same years. That is not the poem repeating itself. That is the poem's method. It circles a thing, and each time round you are standing nearer, and by the end you can see the whole of it.

And there is a new detail in this telling, and it is aimed straight at the heart of our story, so please do not read past it.

"We were away at Troy," said Nestor, "doing hard work. And he (Aegisthus) was at his ease in the meadow-corner of Argos, working on Agamemnon's wife with soft words.

"And at first she would have nothing to do with it. She refused the shameful thing. She had a good mind in her.

"And there was a man with her — a singer, whom Agamemnon had left behind when he sailed, with strict instructions to keep watch over his wife. But when the gods' will closed round her, Aegisthus took that singer off to a lonely island and marooned him there, to be a prize and a meal for the birds. And after that she was willing, and he was willing, and he took her to his own house.

"And he burned a great many thigh bones on the gods' altars and hung up a great many offerings, gold and woven cloth, because he had brought off a thing he had never in his life expected to bring off."

Sit with that a moment.

The first defence of a good marriage in this poem is a *bard*. Not a guard, not a wall, not a garrison — a singer, left behind on purpose, whose whole commission was to stay in the house and keep the queen in mind of who she was. And the seduction only became possible once he had been taken out and left on a rock to be eaten by birds.

Now recall a hall on Ithaca, three years running, with a singer in it who cannot leave and does not want to be there, performing for men who did not invite him and will not let him go. His name is Phemius. I told you to remember him in Book One, and I am telling you again. The house of Odysseus still has its bard. The house of Agamemnon did not. The poem thinks this matters, and it thinks the difference between those two women may rest partly on it — which is either a very beautiful idea about what stories do for people, or a professional bard's advertisement for his own trade, and I am not at all sure it is not both.

"Meanwhile," Nestor went on, "Menelaus and I sailed out of Troy together, as friends. But when we came to holy Sunium, the headland of Athens, the god struck down Menelaus's helmsman with his gentle arrows — Phrontis, the best man in the world at holding a ship on course in a gale — with the steering oar still in his hands. So Menelaus had to put in and bury his companion and do it properly, though he was in a great hurry.

"And when he put out again, storms took him and carried him the whole way south to Egypt. And there he stayed, gathering a great deal of gold, wandering with his ships among people whose language he could not speak.

"And all that time Aegisthus was king in Mycenae, planning ugly things, and the people were ground down under him. Seven years he ruled there. In the eighth, Orestes (Agamemnon's son, Menelaus's nephew) came back from Athens and killed him; and he gave the Greeks a funeral feast over his hateful mother and over that coward Aegisthus. And on that very day Menelaus came home, with as much treasure as his ships would carry.

"So do not you wander far from home, my friend, and do not leave your property behind you with violent men in your house, or they will divide up everything you own and eat it, and your journey will have been for nothing. But go to Menelaus (in Sparta) — I tell you to go. He came home last of anybody, from a place no one comes back from, over a sea so wide that not even birds cross it in a year. Go by ship if you like, or go by land, and take a chariot and my sons, and they will bring you to bright Lacedaemon (another name for Sparta), to fair-haired Menelaus. Ask him for the truth yourself. He will not lie to you. He is far too sensible for that."[^4]

The sun went down and darkness came on, and Athena said that they should cut the tongues of the sacrifice and mix the wine and pour out the offerings, to Poseidon and to the other gods, and then think about bed; for the light had gone, and it is not decent to sit long at a god's feast.

---

Then Telemachus said that he would go and sleep down on the ship, and old Nestor very nearly fell out of his chair.

"Zeus and the other immortals forbid that you should go from *my* house to a fast ship to sleep — as though I were a beggar, a man with no clothes in his house, no blankets, no covers, nothing soft to lie on for himself or his guests. I have plenty of good blankets. The dear son of that man Odysseus is not going to lie down on a ship's deck while I am alive, or while there are children left in this house to look after any guest who comes to it."

"You are quite right, sir," said Athena (still pretending to be Mentor), "and Telemachus should do as you say; it is very much better. Let him go up with you now and sleep in your house. As for me, I shall go down to the black ship and hearten our crew and give them their orders — I am the eldest of them; the rest are younger men, all of an age with Telemachus, who came along out of friendship. I shall sleep beside the black hull tonight; and in the morning I mean to go on to the Cauconians, where a debt is owed me, and not a small one, nor a new one."

And having said it, she went away in the form of a sea-eagle.

Everyone who saw it was astonished; and the old man took Telemachus by the hand and said his name and said:

"My friend, I do not think you will turn out a coward or a weakling, if the gods walk beside you at your age. That was no other of the ones who live on Olympus. That was the daughter of Zeus — the Glorious, the Third Daughter — who used to honour your father above all the Greeks.

"Be gracious to us, lady, and give a good name to me, and to my sons, and to my honoured wife; and I will sacrifice you a yearling heifer, broad in the brow, unbroken, that no man has ever led under the yoke. I will offer her to you with gold poured round her horns."

So he prayed, and she heard him.

And they went up to the fine house and slept: Telemachus on a corded bedstead in the echoing colonnade, and Peisistratus beside him — the one son still unmarried and living at home.

---

When Dawn came up, rosy-fingered, the old horseman (Nestor) was out of bed first, and went and sat down on the polished white stone benches outside his high doors, where his own father used to sit; and his sons came out of their rooms one after another, six of them, and gathered round him. And Nestor said:

"Quickly now, my children. I want to satisfy Athena, who came to me openly, before my own eyes, at the god's rich feast. One of you go out to the field for a heifer and have the herdsman drive her in. One go down to the black ship and bring up Telemachus's crew, and leave two behind. One send for Laerces the goldsmith, to pour the gold round the heifer's horns. The rest of you stay here, and tell the women in the house to make everything ready."

And they all set to. The heifer came in from the field; the crew came up from the ship; the smith came with his tools in his hands — his anvil and his hammer and the well-made tongs he worked the gold with. And Athena came too, to accept her offering.

And old Nestor gave out the gold, and the smith worked it and poured it round the heifer's horns, so that the goddess would be pleased when she saw it. And Nestor's sons led the animal in by the horns, and water was brought in a flowered basin, and barley grains in a basket; and Thrasymedes stood close with a sharp axe in his hand to strike her down, and Perseus held the bowl for the blood.

And old Nestor began the rite with the water and the scattered barley, and prayed hard to Athena as he began, and cut hairs from the animal's head and threw them into the fire.

And when they had prayed and thrown the barley, Thrasymedes, standing close, struck. The axe cut through the tendons of the neck and unstrung the heifer's strength; and the women cried out — the daughters, and the sons' wives, and Nestor's honoured wife Eurydice, eldest of the daughters of Clymenus. And the men lifted the animal's head from the trampled ground and held it up, and Peisistratus cut the throat.

And when the dark blood had run out and the life had gone from the bones, they broke the body down quickly, and cut out the thigh pieces in the proper way, and covered them with a double fold of fat, and laid raw meat on top. And the old man burned them on split kindling and poured bright wine over them, and the young men stood beside him with five-pronged forks in their hands. And when the thigh pieces were burned up and they had tasted the inner meat, they cut the rest into small pieces and ran the spits through and roasted it, holding the sharp spits in their hands.

And meanwhile Telemachus was being bathed by Polycaste, Nestor's youngest daughter, who washed him and rubbed him with olive oil and put a fine cloak and a tunic on him; and he came out of the bath looking like one of the immortals, and went and sat down beside Nestor, the shepherd of the people.[^5]

---

Then they roasted the outer meat and drew it off the spits and sat down and ate, and good men stood by to see that the gold cups stayed full.

And when they had eaten, Nestor said: "Boys — bring the horses out and get them under the yoke, so that Telemachus can be on his way."

They did it quickly; and a housekeeper packed in bread and wine and the sort of food kings eat. And Telemachus got up into the fine chariot, and Peisistratus got up beside him and took the reins in his hands and laid the whip on; and the horses went out over the plain, and Pylos and its steep citadel went away behind them.

All that day the yoke rocked on their necks.

And the sun went down, and all the ways grew dark; and they came to Pherae, to the house of Diocles, and slept there, and he treated them as a host should. And when Dawn came up again, rosy-fingered, they yoked the horses and got up into the chariot and drove out through the echoing gateway; and the whip cracked, and the horses ran on willingly, and the wheat-bearing plain went by underneath them.

And so they finished the journey — that is how well those fast horses ran.

And the sun went down, and all the ways grew dark.

## NOTES TO BOOK THREE

[^1]: **Sacrifice, and the Prometheus bargain.** The story is told properly by Hesiod, a poet roughly contemporary with Homer, in a poem about how the world got organised. Prometheus divides the first ox at a place called Mecone and offers Zeus the choice; Zeus takes the fat-wrapped bones, and is angry, and takes fire away from men in return; and Prometheus steals it back, and is chained to a rock — the whole apparatus. What the tale exists to explain is a piece of ordinary practice: why, at every sacrifice, the god's share is precisely the part you could not have eaten anyway. It is the kind of story a culture tells to make a comfortable arrangement look like ancient law — and the Greeks were not fools about it either; the whole thing is shot through with a sense that Zeus knew exactly what he was choosing and let it stand. The practical point is the one to carry with you: eating meat was a public and religious act. When you read that a household "sacrificed" and then feasted, do not picture a bonfire of good food going to waste. Picture a barbecue with a grace before it that took an hour.

[^2]: **Feed first, ask afterwards.** See Book One, note 7. Nestor observes the law exactly — and even his blunt question about piracy is not the insult it appears to be. Raiding was a recognised occupation with no great shame attached to it, and asking a stranger about it was roughly like asking what line of work he is in. Telemachus takes no offence, and neither should you.

[^3]: **Why Athena turned on her own side.** She had been the Greeks' most committed backer for the whole war — and then, on the night Troy fell, a Greek called Ajax (a different and lesser Ajax, son of Oileus) dragged the Trojan princess Cassandra away from Athena's own altar, where she had taken sanctuary, and raped her; and the army did not punish him for it. That is what Nestor means when he says the goddess's anger could not be bought off with sacrifices. It is worth pausing on, because the poem's logic is quite clear and quite bleak: the Greeks won, and then behaved so foully in victory that the gods scattered them on the way home and drowned a great many. Everything that happens to Odysseus happens downstream of that night. The *Odyssey* is not a poem about a triumph. It is a poem about the bill arriving.

[^4]: **Orestes and his mother.** Notice how little Nestor says. Aegisthus is killed at length and with satisfaction; Clytemnestra (Agamemnon's former wife and Orestes' mother) gets a few words in passing, at a funeral feast, and nobody describes the act or asks what it cost. This is one of the places where our poem stands at a real distance from the version most people half-remember — because three hundred years later the Athenian tragedians took this same story and made the matricide the entire point of it. Aeschylus wrote three plays running on it, in which Orestes is hounded mad by the Furies for killing his mother and has to be tried and acquitted by a court at Athens before the world can go on. None of that is here. In this poem Orestes is simply a good son who did the right thing, held up to a young man as a model, and nobody flinches. It is a useful reminder that "the Greeks" were not one people with one opinion, and that the distance between Homer and Aeschylus is greater than the distance between Shakespeare and us.

[^5]: **The bath.** A young unmarried daughter of the house personally bathing a male guest and rubbing him with oil is not a scandal, not a seduction, and not even especially remarkable. It is high-status hospitality performed by a high-status member of the family, and handing the job to a servant would have been something of a slight. (The oil is because they had no soap.) There is also a later Greek tradition that Telemachus eventually married Polycaste, of which our poem knows nothing whatever — but somebody clearly read this scene and thought, well, *obviously*.
