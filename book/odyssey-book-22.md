# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK TWENTY-TWO
### In Which the Doors Are Shut

---

Then Odysseus of the many devices stripped off his rags and sprang onto the great threshold with the bow in his hands and the quiver full of arrows, and poured the swift shafts out at his feet, and spoke to the suitors.

"So that decisive contest is finished. Now I shall find out whether I can hit another mark, one that no man has hit yet — and may Apollo grant me the glory of it."

And he aimed a bitter arrow at Antinous.

Antinous was on the point of lifting a beautiful cup, a golden one with two handles, and he had it in his hands and was turning it about so as to drink. Death was nowhere in his thoughts. Who among a company of men at dinner would imagine that one man, alone against so many, however strong he was, would bring an evil death and a black fate down on him?

And Odysseus took aim and hit him in the throat, and the point went clean through the soft neck. He sank down sideways, and the cup fell out of his hand as he was hit, and a thick jet of blood came at once from his nostrils. He kicked the table away from him sharply with his foot, and the food went all over the floor, and the bread and the roast meat lay in the mess.

And the suitors began shouting through the hall when they saw the man go down, and they sprang up out of their chairs and rushed about, staring at the well-built walls on every side. There was not a shield anywhere, and not a heavy spear to lay a hand on.

And they shouted at Odysseus in fury.

"Stranger, you shoot at men to your own cost. You will never enter another contest. Your utter destruction is certain now. You have killed the man who was far the best of the young men in Ithaca. The vultures will eat you here."

Every one of them thought he had not meant to kill the man. They were fools. They did not know that the ropes of destruction had been made fast on the whole company.

And Odysseus looked at them from under his brows.

"You dogs. You thought I would never come home from the country of the Trojans, so you wore my house away, and forced the women of my household to lie with you, and courted my wife by stealth while I was still alive — and you had no fear of the gods who hold the wide sky, and no thought that any vengeance of men would come on you afterwards.

"Now the ropes of destruction are made fast on the whole company of you."

And a green fear took hold of all of them, and every man looked about him for somewhere to escape a sheer destruction.

---

Eurymachus was the only one who could answer him at all.

"If you are really Odysseus of Ithaca, come home, then everything you say about what the Greeks have done is just — a great many reckless things in your halls and out on your land.

"But the man who is to blame for all of it is lying dead already. Antinous. He was the one who set the whole business going, and not so much because he wanted the marriage or needed it, but with something else in mind, which the son of Cronos did not grant him: that he should be king himself over the people of well-built Ithaca, once he had lain in wait for your son and killed him.

"Now he is dead, and it served him right. Spare your own people. We will go round the country afterwards and make good everything that has been eaten and drunk in your halls, and each man will bring you the worth of twenty oxen out of his own property, and pay you back in bronze and gold until your heart is warmed. Until then, nobody could blame you for being angry."

And Odysseus looked at him from under his brows.

"Eurymachus. Not if you gave me the whole of your fathers' property, everything you have now and everything you could add to it from anywhere else — not even then would I hold my hands from killing, until the suitors had paid the whole price of their offence.

"The choice in front of you now is to fight or to run, if any man of you can get away from death and the fates. But I do not think a single one of you will escape a sheer destruction."

---

I should like to stop for one moment over that exchange, because it is easy to hear it as a furious man refusing to listen, and it is not quite that.

What Eurymachus is offering is not a bribe. It is the ordinary and honourable settlement of this world, and it has a name and a procedure and a long history behind it. When a man had been injured or killed, the injuring party or his family could offer compensation — cattle, bronze, gold, worked to a figure — and if it was accepted, that was the end of the matter, with no dishonour to anybody. It was how a society without police or courts of appeal stopped one killing turning into forty years of killings. In the older poem there is a shield with a whole city engraved on it, and in the middle of that city two men are arguing in front of a crowd over precisely this: whether a blood-price has been properly paid. Everyone listening knew the mechanism, and knew it as a good thing.

So Eurymachus is doing the correct thing, and doing it with some skill. He blames the dead man, who cannot contradict him; he concedes the whole charge; he offers full restitution and then a great deal on top of it, from every man present. Any ordinary hearing of the case would end there.

And Odysseus refuses it, which he is entitled to do — the offer always could be refused, and the refusal was itself a recognised move, and it meant blood. What he says is that no quantity of property will do, because what is owed is not property. It is the only moment in the poem where he is offered a way to have everything back except the satisfaction, and turns it down.

---

Their knees went loose under them and their hearts melted; and Eurymachus spoke to them a second time.

"Friends, this man is not going to hold his hands. Now that he has the polished bow and the quiver he will shoot from the smooth threshold until he has killed every one of us. So let us think about fighting. Draw your swords and hold the tables up against the arrows; let us all go at him together in a body, and see if we can push him off the threshold and out of the doorway, and get into the town and raise the alarm. Then this man would very soon have shot for the last time."

And he drew his sharp two-edged sword and sprang at him with a terrible shout. And in the same instant Odysseus let an arrow go and struck him in the chest beside the nipple, and drove the swift shaft into his liver. He let the sword drop out of his hand and pitched forward across the table, doubled up, and spilled the food and the two-handled cup on the floor; and he beat the ground with his forehead in his agony, and kicked at the chair with both feet until it rocked; and a mist came down over his eyes.

And Amphinomus went for Odysseus with his sword drawn, to try to force him back from the door. But Telemachus was too quick for him and hit him from behind with his bronze-tipped spear, between the shoulders, and drove it through his chest. He went down with a crash and struck the ground with the whole of his forehead. And Telemachus sprang away, leaving the long spear in the body, because he was afraid one of the Greeks might rush at him with a sword as he stooped to pull it out.

He ran to his father. "Father, I shall fetch you a shield now, and two spears, and a bronze helmet to fit your temples; and I shall arm myself as I go and give arms to the swineherd and to the cowherd. It is much better to be armed."

"Run and fetch them, while I still have arrows to keep them off — or they will push me away from the doorway when I am one man alone."

So Telemachus went to the store-room where the famous weapons were, and took four shields and eight spears and four bronze helmets with horsehair crests, and came back quickly to his father. And he armed himself first, and the two servants put on their fine armour, and they stood beside Odysseus, who was clever and full of schemes.

And as long as he had arrows he kept shooting, and picked the suitors off one by one in his own house, and they fell thick and fast. But when the arrows gave out he leaned the bow against the doorpost of the well-built hall, and slung a shield of four hides over his shoulders, and put a well-made helmet on his powerful head, with a horsehair crest that nodded terribly above it, and took up two heavy spears tipped with bronze.

---

Now there was a side door in the well-built wall, and at the level of the topmost part of the threshold of the great hall there was a way through into a passage, closed with well-fitting doors. Odysseus had told the swineherd to watch it and stand close by, because there was only the one approach.

And Agelaus called out to them all: "Friends, will nobody climb up to the side door and tell the people and get the alarm raised? Then this man would very soon have shot for the last time."

And Melanthius the goatherd answered him. "It cannot be done, Agelaus. The fine gates of the courtyard are terribly close, and the mouth of the passage is awkward; one man could hold it against the lot of us, if he had any courage. But wait — I shall fetch you arms out of the store-room. Because that is where Odysseus and his son have put them, I am sure of it, and nowhere else."

And he went up by the steps of the hall to the store-rooms of Odysseus, and took out twelve shields and as many spears and as many bronze helmets with horsehair crests, and brought them down and gave them to the suitors, very quickly.

And Odysseus felt his knees and his heart go under him when he saw them putting armour on and shaking their long spears in their hands. The task looked very large. And he said to Telemachus: "One of the women in the house is stirring up an ugly fight against us. Or it is Melanthius."

"Father, I am the one who made this mistake, and nobody else is to blame. I left the close-fitting door of the store-room ajar, and one of them was a better watcher than I was. Go, good Eumaeus, and shut the door of the store-room, and find out whether it is one of the women doing this, or Melanthius the son of Dolius, which is what I think."

And while they were saying it, Melanthius went up to the store-room again for more armour; and the good swineherd saw him and said at once to Odysseus, who was standing near: "Son of Laertes, that pestilent fellow, the one we suspected, is going up to the store-room again. Tell me plainly — shall I kill him, if I turn out to be the better man, or bring him here to you, so that he can pay for all the crimes he has been planning in your house?"

"Telemachus and I will hold the lordly suitors inside the hall, however furiously they come on. You two twist his feet and his hands up behind his back and throw him into the store-room and tie boards behind him; and put a twisted rope round him and haul him up the tall pillar until he is close under the roof-beams, so that he stays alive a good long while and suffers hard pain."

They went up, and he did not see them, because he was hunting for weapons in a corner of the room; and they took their stand one on either side of the doorposts and waited. And when Melanthius came over the threshold, with a fine helmet in one hand and in the other a broad old shield, spotted with mould, which the hero Laertes had carried when he was young and which had been lying by with the stitching of the straps gone — the two of them rushed at him and got hold of him and dragged him in by the hair and threw him on the floor, in a very bad state of terror; and they tied his hands and feet together behind him with a painful knot, exactly as the son of Laertes had told them, and put a twisted rope round him and hauled him up the tall pillar until he was close under the roof-beams.

And you mocked him then, Eumaeus, swineherd, and said: "Now you will keep watch all night, Melanthius, lying on a soft bed, which is what you deserve; and you will not miss the early Dawn coming up on her golden throne out of the streams of Ocean, at the hour when you usually drive the goats in for the suitors to make their dinner in the hall."

So they left him there, stretched in that cruel bond, and put their armour on and closed the bright door, and went back to Odysseus, who was clever and full of schemes.

And there they stood, breathing fury: four men on the threshold, and inside the hall a great many, and good men.

---

And Athena came and stood near them, in the likeness of Mentor, and looking like him and sounding like him. And Odysseus was glad when he saw her, and said: "Mentor, keep destruction off us. Remember your old friend, who did you a good many kindnesses; and you are the same age as I am."

He said that, though he suspected it was Athena, who rouses armies.

And the suitors shouted threats at her from the other side of the hall, and Agelaus was the first of them.

"Mentor, do not let Odysseus talk you into fighting the suitors and defending him. Because I think this is how it will go for us: when we have killed these two, the father and the son, then you will be killed along with them, for the things you are proposing to do in this hall; you will pay for it with your own head. And when we have taken your strength away from you with the bronze, we shall mix everything you own, indoors and out, with the property of Odysseus; and we shall not let your sons live in your house, or your daughters, or your good wife go about the town of Ithaca."

That made Athena angrier still in her heart, and she turned on Odysseus with hard words.

"There is no steadiness left in your strength, Odysseus, and no courage, of the kind you had when you fought the Trojans for nine years without a break over Helen of the white arms, and killed a great many men in that terrible fighting, and took the wide streets of Priam's city by your own planning.

"How is it that now, when you have come home to your own house and your own possessions, you complain about having to be strong against a crowd of suitors?

"Come here, my friend. Stand beside me and watch my work, and see what sort of man Mentor son of Alcimus is at repaying kindness in the middle of enemies."

And even so she did not yet give him the strength to settle the battle either way, because she was still making trial of the strength and the courage of Odysseus and of his glorious son. She darted up to the roof-beam of the smoky hall and sat there in the likeness of a swallow, and watched.[^1]

---

And Agelaus and Eurynomus and Amphimedon and Demoptolemus and Peisander and Polybus urged the suitors on; these were by far the best men of those still alive, and they were fighting for their lives. The bow and the thick-flying arrows had already brought the rest down.

"Friends," said Agelaus, "this man is going to hold his hands at last. Mentor has gone off after all his empty boasting, and they are on their own at the outer door. So do not throw your long spears all together. Six of you cast first, and let us hope Zeus grants that Odysseus is hit and we win the glory. There will be nothing to worry about from the others once he has gone down."

So the six threw as he told them. And Athena made all of it come to nothing. One of them hit the doorpost of the great hall, and another the close-fitting door, and another's heavy ash spear struck the wall.

And when they had got past that volley, Odysseus said: "Friends, now I say it is our turn to throw into the crowd of them, who are trying to kill us on top of everything else they have done."

And all four of them threw, aiming carefully. Odysseus killed Demoptolemus, Telemachus killed Euryades, the swineherd killed Elatus, and the cowherd killed Peisander; and all of them bit the wide earth together. And the suitors drew back to the innermost part of the hall, and the four rushed in and pulled the spears out of the dead men.

And the suitors threw again, hard, and Athena made most of it come to nothing again — one hit the doorpost, another the door, another's ash spear struck the wall. But Amphimedon caught Telemachus on the wrist, a graze that took the skin off; and Ctesippus grazed the shoulder of Eumaeus with a long spear that went over the top of his shield and fell to the ground beyond.

And the four threw again into the crowd. Odysseus killed Eurydamas, Telemachus killed Amphimedon, the swineherd killed Polybus. And the cowherd hit Ctesippus in the chest, and boasted over him.

"Son of Polytherses, you great lover of jokes — do not ever give way to foolishness again and talk so big; leave the last word to the gods, since they are a great deal stronger than you are. That is a guest-gift in return for the ox's foot you gave to godlike Odysseus when he was begging round the house."

Then Odysseus wounded Agelaus with his long spear at close quarters, and Telemachus got Leiocritus in the flank and drove the bronze straight through, so that he pitched forward and struck the ground with the whole of his forehead.

And then Athena held up the deadly aegis high from the roof — the terrible thing she carries, that turns men's courage to water when they look at it — and their hearts were terrified, and they went fleeing through the hall like a herd of cattle that the darting gadfly falls on and drives before it in the spring, when the long days come.

And as vultures with hooked claws and curved beaks come down off the mountains and dart at the smaller birds, which fly low over the plain shunning the clouds, and the vultures fall on them and kill them, and there is no defence and no escape, and men are glad at the chase — so those four went at the suitors and struck them down right and left through the hall, and a hideous groaning went up as heads were broken, and the whole floor ran with blood.

---

And Leiodes rushed forward and got hold of the knees of Odysseus and begged him.

"I beg you by your knees, Odysseus. Respect me and pity me. I tell you I never yet did any wrong to one of the women in your halls, by word or by deed; and I tried to stop the other suitors when any of them was doing such things. And they would not listen to me and keep their hands off wickedness; so through their own reckless folly they have met a shameful end.

"And I was their soothsayer, and I have done nothing wrong, and I shall lie down among them. So there is no gratitude afterwards for good things done."

And Odysseus looked at him from under his brows.

"If you claim to have been the soothsayer of these men, then you must often have prayed in these halls that the day of my sweet homecoming should be kept far away from me, and that my dear wife should follow you and bear you children. So you shall not escape a bitter death."

And with his heavy hand he picked up a sword that was lying on the ground, which Agelaus had dropped when he was killed, and drove it full into his neck; and his head was in the dust while he was still speaking.[^2]

---

And Phemius the singer was still trying to get away from the black fate — the man who sang for the suitors because they made him. He was standing near the side door with the clear-toned lyre in his hands, turning it over in his mind whether to slip out of the hall and sit down at the great altar of Zeus of the courtyard, where Laertes and Odysseus had burned so many thigh-pieces of oxen, or whether to run forward and take hold of the knees of Odysseus and beg.

And as he thought about it, taking hold of the knees seemed better. So he laid the hollow lyre down on the ground, between the mixing bowl and the silver-studded chair, and ran forward and got hold of the knees of Odysseus and begged him.

"I beg you by your knees, Odysseus. Respect me and pity me. It will be a grief to you yourself afterwards if you kill a singer — me, who sing for gods and for men.

"I am self-taught, and the god has planted every kind of song in my mind; and I am fit to sing beside you as beside a god. So do not be in a hurry to cut my throat.

"And Telemachus will tell you this himself, your own dear son: that it was not of my own will and not for any profit that I came to your house to sing for the suitors at their dinners. They were too many and too strong for me, and they brought me here by force."

Telemachus heard him, and said quickly to his father, who was close by: "Hold — do not hurt this man with the bronze; he is innocent. And let us spare the herald Medon as well, who always looked after me in this house when I was a child — unless Philoetius has killed him already, or the swineherd, or he came in your way while you were going through the hall."

And Medon heard him say it. He was lying curled up under a chair with a newly flayed ox-hide over him, trying to keep off the black fate; and he got out from under the chair at once and stripped off the hide and ran to Telemachus and took hold of his knees.

"Friend, here I am. Hold back and tell your father, before he does me some harm with the sharp bronze in his strength, being so angry with the suitors, who wore away his property in this house and were fools enough to show you no honour at all."

And Odysseus smiled at him and said: "Take heart. He has got you out of it and saved you, so that you may know in your own heart, and tell it to somebody else, how much better doing good is than doing harm.

"But go out of the hall, the pair of you, and sit down in the courtyard away from the killing — you and the singer of many songs — while I get on with what has to be done in the house."

So the two of them went out of the hall and sat down at the altar of great Zeus, looking about them in every direction, expecting to be killed at any moment.

---

And Odysseus looked round his own house to see whether any man was still alive and hiding, keeping clear of the black fate. And he found the whole company of them fallen in the blood and the dust — a great many of them.

As fish that fishermen have dragged up out of the grey sea in the meshes of a net onto the curve of a beach, and they lie heaped on the sand longing for the salt water, and the bright sun takes the life out of them: so the suitors lay heaped on one another.

And Odysseus said to Telemachus: "Go and call the nurse Eurycleia for me. There is something I want to say to her."

So Eurycleia came, and found Odysseus among the bodies of the dead, spattered with blood and filth, like a lion that has been feeding on an ox of the farmyard, with his whole chest and his cheeks on both sides bloodied, so that he is a terrible thing to look at.

And when she saw the dead and the great pool of blood she was on the point of raising the cry of triumph, because she had seen a great work done. And Odysseus stopped her, and held her back, keen as she was.

"Be glad in your own heart, old woman, and hold it in, and do not cry out. It is not a holy thing to boast over dead men.

"It was the fate the gods sent and their own reckless behaviour that destroyed these men. They honoured nobody at all on the earth, bad or good, whoever came among them. So they brought a shameful death on themselves by their own folly."

---

That is a strange thing to say standing in that room, and I do not want it to slip past, because it is one of the sharpest lines the poem draws and the modern reader is unlikely to have been expecting it.

The old woman's impulse is the natural one, and it was the ordinary one in that world too: a shrill cry of triumph over a fallen enemy, the noise a woman raised at a moment of victory or at a sacrifice. Nobody in the hall would have thought it out of place. And her master, who is standing in the blood with his hands and feet red to the ankles and the wrists, tells her to stop, and gives a reason — that it is unholy to exult over dead men.

He does not say that they did not deserve it, and he does not soften a word about what they were. What he says is that gloating is not permitted, and that the dead have passed out of the reach of it and into the hands of the gods. It is a piece of religious feeling rather than of mercy, and it comes from the same source as everything else he has been careful about all evening. He has just killed a hall full of people and he has one more thing to do tonight, and he is not going to add impiety to it.

I set it down without comment for the moment, and ask you only to hold on to it for the length of about two pages.

---

"But tell me about the women in these halls," he said, "which of them dishonour me and which are guiltless."

"I shall tell you the truth, child. There are fifty women servants in the house, whom we have taught to do their work — to card wool and to bear the life of slaves. Twelve of them altogether have gone the way of shamelessness, and take no notice of me, or of Penelope either. And Telemachus has only just grown up, and his mother would not let him give orders to the women."

"Do not wake her yet. Tell those women to come here — the ones who have been behaving disgracefully."

And the old woman went off through the hall to tell them.

Then he called Telemachus and the cowherd and the swineherd to him.

"Begin carrying the bodies out now, and tell the women to help you. And after that clean down the beautiful chairs and the tables with water and porous sponges. And when you have put the whole house in order, take the women out of the hall, between the round-house and the good fence of the courtyard, and strike them with your long swords until you have taken the life out of every one of them, and they have forgotten the love they had with the suitors when they lay with them in secret."

And the women came out all together, wailing terribly and letting the big tears fall. First they carried the bodies out and propped them against one another under the colonnade of the fenced courtyard, while Odysseus himself gave the orders and hurried them on; and they had to do it. Then they cleaned the beautiful chairs and the tables with water and sponges. And Telemachus and the two herdsmen scraped the floor of the well-built hall with hoes, and the women carried the scrapings out and threw them out of doors.

And when the whole hall had been put in order, they took the women out of the building, into the space between the round-house and the good fence of the courtyard, and shut them into a narrow place from which there was no getting away.

And Telemachus said: "I shall not take the lives of these women by any clean death — women who have poured insults on my head and on my mother's, and slept with the suitors."

And he tied the cable of a dark-prowed ship to a great pillar and threw it round the round-house, stretching it high up, so that none of them could touch the ground with her feet.

And as when long-winged thrushes or doves come into a net that has been set in a thicket, making for their roost, and the bed that receives them is a hateful one — so the women held their heads in a row, and there were nooses round all their necks, so that they should die most pitiably. And their feet kicked a little while, but not for long.

And they brought Melanthius out through the doorway and the courtyard, and cut off his nose and his ears with the pitiless bronze, and tore away his privates to be eaten raw by the dogs, and cut off his hands and feet in the fury of their hearts.

Then they washed their own hands and feet and went back into the house to Odysseus, and the work was finished.[^3]

---

I promised, a very long way back, that I would come to this and would not pretend it was something else, so here we are.

Twelve women are hanged in a row in a yard, by the son of the house, for having slept with the men who had occupied it; and a man is mutilated to death beside them; and the poem, which has spent five lines telling us the birds' feet kicked a little while, moves straight on to the housekeeping. There is no comment. Telemachus's stated reason is that a clean death — the sword, which he had been ordered to use — would be too good for women who had insulted him and his mother, and the poem lets that stand as sufficient.

I said in Book Two that the poem is quite sure about these women and that I am not, and everything since has made me less sure rather than more. Here is what the text itself gives us. They were slaves. Not one of them chose to be in that house. The men they went with were the effective masters of it for three years, and one of the twelve, Melantho, was the lover of the second most powerful man in the room, who is now dead on the floor inside. Nobody in the poem asks what a young woman in that position could have done instead, or what the cost of refusal would have been, or which of the twelve went willingly and which did not — and the poem, which has just had its hero forbid an old woman from so much as cheering because it is unholy to exult over the dead, does not extend to them a fraction of the scruple it extended to Antinous's corpse.

That is not a modern complaint smuggled in. It is the poem's own standard, applied a page later to the wrong people, and I think it is fair to notice the difference.

What I will not do is pretend the passage is an accident, or that Homer disapproved and left us clues. There are none. This is the world the poem is describing and largely the world it endorses, and the same book that gives us the swineherd's kindness and the warning against gloating gives us this yard, and expects us to read the whole of it with the same appetite. Two things can be true at once about a very old book, and both of them are.

---

Then Odysseus said to Eurycleia: "Bring me sulphur, old woman, that cleans away pollution, and bring me fire, so that I can fumigate the hall. And tell Penelope to come here with her women; and order all the women in the house to come."

"All that you say is right, my child. But let me bring you a cloak and a tunic to put on. Do not stand about in your own halls with rags over your broad shoulders; that would be a thing to blame."

"Fire first. Let there be a fire made in this hall."

So she brought fire and sulphur, and Odysseus fumigated the hall thoroughly, and the rest of the house, and the courtyard.[^4]

And the old woman went off through the fine house of Odysseus to take the message to the women and tell them to come. And they came out of their quarters holding torches, and gathered round Odysseus and welcomed him, and took hold of him and kissed his head and his shoulders and his hands.

And a sweet longing came over him to weep and to cry aloud, because in his heart he knew every one of them.

## NOTES TO BOOK TWENTY-TWO

[^1]: **The aegis, and the goddess on the roof-beam.** The aegis is Athena's — sometimes described as a goatskin, sometimes as a shield, fringed and tasselled and terrible, and its effect on mortals is not to wound them but to unmake their courage; when it is held up, armies run. So the moment it appears above the roof the fighting stops being a fight and becomes a slaughter, and the poem's similes change accordingly, from men throwing spears to cattle driven by a gadfly and small birds broken up by vultures. — What I like best is where she sits to do it. She has been present in the hall for some time in the shape of Mentor, taking part in the argument and being threatened by Agelaus, and when she finally acts she darts up to a beam and turns into a swallow. A goddess who has just declined to settle the battle for him, on the grounds that she wanted to see what he was made of first, watches the end of it from the rafters in the shape of a small bird.

[^2]: **The two men who beg, and why one dies.** Leiodes and Phemius make almost the same appeal, in almost the same words, in the space of a page: they take hold of his knees, which is the binding form (see Book Six, note 3), and each says he is innocent and tried to hold back. One is killed in the middle of speaking and one is spared, and it is worth being clear about the difference, because it is not a matter of who is nicer. Phemius can point to a witness in the room — Telemachus, who confirms he was brought there by force — and his trade is one the gods protect. Leiodes has no witness, and his trade was to read the omens for the suitors, which means, as Odysseus points out immediately, that he had spent three years praying professionally for exactly the outcome the household most dreaded. Whether that is fair is another question; the poem told us in Book Twenty-One that he alone found their conduct hateful. But it is not arbitrary. Odysseus makes a judgement about complicity in about four seconds, on the evidence available, and it is the same judgement he is about to make about the women, and it goes the other way.

[^3]: **The manner of it.** Two details are worth having. The first is that hanging was regarded as a peculiarly shameful death, the death of women who killed themselves in disgrace, and not a soldier's end at all; Telemachus chooses it deliberately, and says so, having been given an order that specified swords. The second is the rope: he uses a ship's cable, the same kind of cable Philoetius used to lash the courtyard gates shut an hour earlier, and the poem notes it without comment. — As for Melanthius, what is done to him is precisely what Antinous threatened to have done to a beggar in Book Eighteen, and what the Lapiths did to the drunken centaur in the story Antinous told in Book Twenty-One. The poem has mentioned this specific mutilation three times, always as something one hears about happening elsewhere, and then it happens.

[^4]: **Sulphur, and what it is for.** This is not cleaning. Burning sulphur was used against *miasma* — the ritual pollution that killing produces, which attaches to a place and to a person regardless of whether the killing was just, and which has to be dealt with before ordinary life can resume there. It is closer to disinfecting a house against something invisible than to sweeping it. Note that Odysseus calls for the fire and the sulphur before he will accept a change of clothes: the nurse wants to make him presentable, and he wants the room made clean in the other sense first. Note also what it implies. The killing was lawful, and everybody in the poem agrees it was lawful, and it has still left the house in a condition that has to be burned out of the air before his wife can be brought downstairs into it.
