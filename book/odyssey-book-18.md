# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK EIGHTEEN
### In Which Two Beggars Fight for a Sausage, and a Wife Comes Downstairs

---

Then a beggar came in, a public one, who used to go begging round the town of Ithaca, and who was famous for a greedy belly and eating and drinking without stopping. He had no strength and no muscle, though he was very big to look at.

His name was Arnaeus; his mother had given him that at birth. But all the young men called him Irus, because he ran errands for anybody who asked.[^1]

He came in and tried to drive Odysseus out of his own house.

"Get away from the door, old man, before you get dragged out by the foot. Do you not see them all winking at me and telling me to haul you out? I would be ashamed to. Get up, or the two of us will be settling it with our hands."

And Odysseus looked at him from under his brows.

"You strange man. I am doing you no harm and saying nothing about you, and I do not grudge it if somebody gives you something, even a good deal. This threshold will take both of us, and you have no call to be jealous over other men's property. You look to me like a vagrant, the same as I am; it is the gods who hand out prosperity.

"Do not push me too far with your hands, or you will make me angry; and old as I am I shall spoil your chest and your lips with blood. And I should have a quieter day of it tomorrow, because I do not think you would come back a second time to the hall of Odysseus."

"Listen to the tramp rattling away, like an old woman at the oven. I shall work him over with both hands and knock every tooth out of his jaws onto the floor, the way you do with a pig caught in the corn. Put your belt on, and let all these gentlemen see us fight. How are you going to take on a younger man?"

Antinous heard them squabbling on the polished threshold, and laughed with real pleasure.

"Friends, there has never been anything like this. What sport the god has brought into this house. The stranger and Irus are challenging each other to a fight. Let us set them on."

They all jumped up laughing and gathered round the two beggars in their rags.

"Listen to me, suitors," said Antinous. "There are goats' paunches lying at the fire that we set aside for supper, stuffed with fat and blood. Whichever of the two wins and proves the better man can stand up and take whichever of them he likes. And he shall always eat with us afterwards, and we will let no other beggar come in and beg among us."

They all liked that. And Odysseus, with cunning in his mind, said:

"Friends, there is no possible way for an old man worn down with trouble to fight a younger one. But my belly tells me to — my belly, that does all the harm — so that I get myself beaten. But swear me all a great oath first: that no man of you, out of favour to Irus, will hit me with his heavy hand and put me down for him unfairly."

They swore it. And Telemachus said: "Stranger, if your proud heart tells you to defend yourself against this man, then have no fear of any other Greek here, because whoever strikes you will have more men to fight. I am the host; and the princes Antinous and Eurymachus agree, and they are both sensible men."

---

Then Odysseus pulled his rags up and belted them round his loins, and out came his thighs, big and handsome, and his broad shoulders showed, and his chest and his powerful arms; and Athena stood beside him and made the limbs of the shepherd of the people greater still.

The suitors were extremely surprised, and one would look at the man beside him and say: "Irus will very soon be un-Irused, and it will be trouble he asked for. What a thigh the old man shows from under those rags."

And Irus's spirit went badly to pieces. The servants had to belt him up and bring him out by force, terrified, with the flesh trembling on his bones.

And Antinous rounded on him. "You braggart. Better if you had never been born, if you are shaking like that at an old man worn out by the misery he carries about with him. I will tell you plainly, and it will be brought about: if he beats you, I shall put you on a black ship and send you to the mainland to king Echetus, who mutilates everybody — and he will cut off your nose and your ears with the pitiless bronze and tear away your privates and give them raw to the dogs to eat."[^2]

That made him tremble a great deal more; and they led him into the middle, and both men put their hands up.

Then Odysseus considered whether to hit him so as to knock the life out of him where he stood, or to fell him gently and stretch him out. And gently seemed better, in case the Greeks looked too closely at him.

So when they had their hands up, Irus hit him on the right shoulder, and Odysseus hit him on the neck under the ear and crushed the bones inside, and the red blood came up through his mouth at once, and he went down in the dust bellowing, with his teeth chattering and his heels drumming on the ground.

And the lordly suitors threw up their hands and died laughing.

And Odysseus took him by the foot and dragged him out through the doorway into the courtyard and propped him against the wall and put his staff in his hand.

"Sit there now and keep the pigs and the dogs off. And do not go setting yourself up as lord over strangers and beggars, you miserable creature, or you may get something worse."

Then he slung the shabby bag over his shoulders, with all its holes, and went back and sat down on the threshold.

And they went in laughing and made him welcome. "May Zeus and the other gods give you whatever you want most, stranger, since you have stopped that insatiable creature begging round the country. We shall very soon be shipping him over to king Echetus."

And Odysseus was glad at the sound of it.

---

I should like you to hold two things about that scene at once, because the poem plainly does.

It is funny. A hall full of men roaring, a bragging beggar who cannot fight being belted into the ring by servants while his knees knock, and a supposed old man who turns out, when he lifts his rags, to have the thighs of a wrestler. The suitors' joke about being *un-Irused* is a genuine joke and it is meant to be laughed at, and the poem is enjoying itself.

And underneath it, look at what is actually happening. A man with nothing is being made to fight another man with nothing, by rich men, for the amusement of the rich men, with a bag of tripe as the prize; and when he loses, the sponsor of the entertainment promises to have him shipped to a foreign torturer who will cut pieces off him. Nobody objects. The oath Odysseus asks for — that none of them will join in and hold him down — was not a foolish precaution.

Irus is a bully and a fool and he picked the fight. He is also the only person in this book who tries to defend that doorway from an intruder, and he is thrashed for it by the intruder, in front of the men who are actually stealing the house.

---

Then Amphinomus took two loaves out of the basket and brought them to him, and pledged him in a golden cup.

"Good health, father stranger. May you have prosperity in time to come; you are held down by a great many troubles now."

And Odysseus answered him.

"Amphinomus, you strike me as a man of good sense; you are your father's son, and I have heard his good name — Nisus of Dulichium, a good man and a rich one. They say you are his, and you seem a reasonable man. So listen to me, and take in what I say.

"Of everything that breathes and moves upon the earth, the earth raises nothing feebler than a man.

"While the gods give him prosperity and his knees are quick, he thinks he will never suffer anything in time to come. And when the blessed gods measure him out sorrow, he carries that too, unwillingly, with an enduring heart. Because the mind of men on the earth is like the day the father of gods and men brings on them.

"I too was once on my way to being a prosperous man, and I did a great many reckless things, giving way to my own strength and power, and trusting in my father and my brothers. So let no man ever be lawless. Let him keep whatever the gods give him in silence.

"And these are the sort of reckless things I see the suitors devising — wasting the property and dishonouring the wife of a man who I do not think will be away from his friends and his own country much longer. He is very near.

"May some god take you home out of his path. May you not meet him when he comes back to his own dear country. Because I do not think the suitors and he will be parted without blood, once he is under his own roof."

And he poured a libation and drank the honey-sweet wine and put the cup back into the prince's hands.

And Amphinomus went off through the hall with his head down and his heart heavy, because his spirit foresaw trouble. And even so he did not escape his fate; Athena had bound him too, to be beaten down by the hands and the spear of Telemachus.

And he sat down again in the chair he had got up from.

---

That is the only warning Odysseus gives anybody in this poem, and it is a real one — not a threat dressed as advice, but a genuine attempt to get one man out of the building before the roof comes in. He does it because Amphinomus brought him bread and spoke to him kindly, which nobody else in that hall has done.

And it does not work. Not because the man is stupid — he understands it perfectly; the poem says his heart foresaw trouble — but because he goes and sits down again in the same chair. Which is, I am afraid, what most people do.

---

Then Athena put it into the mind of Penelope to show herself to the suitors, so as to set their hearts fluttering and make herself more honoured than before in the eyes of her husband and her son.

She gave a little laugh, for no reason, and said: "Eurynome, my heart wants something it never wanted before: I want to show myself to the suitors, hateful as they are to me. And I should like to say a word to my son that would do him good — not to spend all his time with those overbearing men, who speak kindly to him and are planning harm."

"All of that is well said, child. Go and speak to your son. But wash first, and put oil on your cheeks. Do not go down with your face all stained with crying; it does no good to grieve endlessly. And your son is now the age you always prayed to see him."

"Eurynome, do not urge me to wash and oil myself out of kindness. The gods who hold Olympus destroyed my beauty on the day my husband went away in the hollow ships. But tell Autonoe and Hippodameia to come and stand beside me in the hall. I will not go among men alone. I am ashamed to."

And while the old woman was gone to call them, Athena thought of something else, and poured a sweet sleep over the daughter of Icarius (Penelope); and she sank back and slept where she sat, and all her joints let go.

And the shining goddess gave her immortal gifts, so that the Greeks should marvel at her. She washed her beautiful face with the ambrosial beauty that Aphrodite (goddess of love) uses when she goes into the lovely dance of the Graces; and she made her taller and fuller to look at, and whiter than new-sawn ivory.

Then she went away; and the women came in, chattering, and the sound of them woke Penelope, and she rubbed her cheeks with her hands.

"That was a soft sleep that wrapped me round, miserable as I am. I wish pure Artemis (goddess of the hunt) would give me so soft a death, now, this minute, so that I need not go on wasting my life away grieving and longing for the excellence of my husband, who was the best of all the Greeks."

And she went down from her bright upper room, not alone; two women went with her. And when she came among the suitors she stood beside the doorpost of the great hall, holding her shining veil in front of her face, and a faithful woman stood on either side of her.

And their knees went weak where they stood, and their hearts were bewitched with desire, and every one of them prayed to lie in her bed.

---

But she spoke to her son.

"Telemachus, your mind is no longer steady. When you were a child you had better judgement. Now you are grown and have come to the measure of a man, and any stranger looking at your height and your looks would say you were the son of a wealthy house; and your mind and your judgement are no longer what they were.

"What a thing to have happened in these halls — that you let this stranger be knocked about so shamefully. How would it be if a stranger, sitting in our house, came to harm from being handled like that? It would be your shame and your disgrace among men."

"Mother, I do not blame you for being angry. But I do notice things and understand them, both good and bad, and I am not the child I was.

"All the same, I cannot manage everything sensibly. These men sit on either side of me knocking me off balance, planning harm, and I have nobody to help me. And the fight between the stranger and Irus did not turn out the way the suitors wanted; the stranger was the better man.

"I wish to heaven the suitors were sitting in our halls at this moment with their heads hanging, beaten — some of them in the courtyard and some inside — the way Irus is sitting out there by the courtyard gate now, with his head lolling like a drunk, unable to stand up straight or get home, because his legs have gone."

---

Then Eurymachus said: "Daughter of Icarius, sensible Penelope, if all the Greeks in Argos could see you, there would be more suitors than these feasting in your halls from tomorrow morning; because you are beyond all other women in beauty and in stature and in the balance of the mind inside you."

"Eurymachus, the immortals destroyed my excellence and my beauty and my figure on the day the Greeks embarked for Ilium (Troy), and my husband Odysseus went with them. If he were to come home and take care of my life, my name would be greater and better than it is. As it is I am in trouble; a god has let loose so much of it on me.

"When he was going he took me by the wrist of my right hand and said: *Wife, I do not think all the Greeks will come home from Troy unhurt. So I do not know whether a god will spare me or whether I shall be caught there. Take care of everything here. Look after my father and my mother in the house as you do now, or more, while I am away. And when you see our son with a beard on his face, then marry whom you like and leave your house.*

"That is what he said, and all of it is coming about. The night will come when a hateful marriage finds me, whose happiness Zeus has taken away.

"But here is what grieves me to the heart. This was never the way of suitors before. Men who want to court a good woman, the daughter of a rich house, and to compete with each other for her, bring in their own cattle and their own fat sheep and make a feast for the bride's friends, and give her splendid gifts. They do not eat up somebody else's living for nothing."

And Odysseus was glad, because she was drawing gifts out of them and charming their hearts with soft words, while her mind was set on something else entirely.

And Antinous said: "Daughter of Icarius, whatever gifts any of the Greeks wants to bring here, take them; it is not right to refuse a gift. But we shall not go back to our own lands, or anywhere else, until you marry the best man among us."

So they all sent their heralds to fetch presents. Antinous's man brought a great embroidered robe, very beautiful, with twelve golden brooches on it fitted with curved clasps. Eurymachus's brought a chain of gold strung with amber beads, that shone like the sun. Two men brought Eurydamas a pair of earrings, three drops to each of them, with a great grace shining out of them. Another brought Peisander a necklace. And every one of the Greeks gave something fine.

And the queen went up to her upper room, and her women carried the beautiful presents up behind her.

---

Now: watch what has just happened, because it is one of the cleverest things anybody does in this poem and it is done in about nine sentences.

She comes down looking better than she has looked in twenty years, at a moment she did not choose and by an intervention she does not know about. She reproaches her son in front of everybody for allowing a guest to be abused, which puts her firmly on the side of the household and the law. She then quotes her absent husband's own parting instructions, which have the effect of announcing that she is now, technically, free to marry — and she gets the sob in the right place.

And then she observes, sweetly, that the gentlemen have not been doing this properly. Real suitors bring cattle. Real suitors bring gifts.

Within a minute there are heralds running all over Ithaca and the property is flowing the right way up the road for the first time in three years.

Her husband is sitting ten feet away in rags watching it, and the poem says he was *glad* — glad because she was getting presents out of them while her mind was on something else. He does not admire her because she is loyal, or because she is sad, or because she is beautiful, though she is all three. He admires her because she is working the room.

That is the marriage this whole poem has been walking towards, and here is the first glimpse of the two of them in the same space, doing the same thing, on the same side, and only one of them knows it.

---

Then they turned to dancing and singing and enjoyed themselves and waited for the evening to come on; and the dark evening came on them while they were enjoying themselves.

And they set up three braziers in the hall to give light, and put dry firewood in them, long seasoned and newly split, and mixed torches in; and the maids of enduring Odysseus took turns to keep the flame going.

And Odysseus said to them:

"Maids of Odysseus, of the master who has been away so long — go into the house where the honoured queen is. Sit with her and turn the spindle and comfort her, or card the wool in your hands. I will provide the light for all these gentlemen. Even if they want to sit here till the golden Dawn, they will not tire me out. I am very used to enduring."

And the women laughed and looked at one another. And Melantho abused him — a girl with pretty cheeks, the daughter of Dolius, whom Penelope had brought up herself and treated like her own child and given toys to whenever she wanted them. And even so she had no grief in her heart for Penelope; she was Eurymachus's lover.

"You miserable stranger, you must be out of your wits, if you will not go off and sleep at a smithy or a lodging-house instead of standing here talking loudly among all these lords, and not the least bit frightened. Either the wine has your wits, or your mind is always like this and you talk nonsense. Are you so beside yourself because you beat that tramp Irus? Take care a better man than Irus does not stand up against you and knock your head about with his strong hands and throw you out of the house covered in blood."

And Odysseus looked at her from under his brows. "You bitch. I shall go straight over there and tell Telemachus what you have said, and he will cut you to pieces where you stand."

And that frightened the women off. They fled through the hall with their knees shaking, because they thought he meant it.[^3]

---

And he took his stand beside the burning braziers and kept the light going, looking at all of them; and his mind was turning over other things, which were not going to be left unfinished.

But Athena would not let the arrogant suitors leave off their biting insults, so that the pain should sink deeper still into the heart of Odysseus.

And Eurymachus started it, raising a laugh at him.

"Listen to me, suitors of the glorious queen. It cannot be without a god's hand that this man has come to the house of Odysseus. At any rate the torchlight seems to be coming from the man himself — off the top of his head, where there is not one hair, no, nor even a few."

And then to Odysseus: "Stranger, would you be willing to work for hire, if I took you on at a farm on the boundary — your wages guaranteed — gathering stones for walls and planting tall trees? I would keep you in bread all the year round and give you clothes for your body and sandals for your feet. But since you have learned nothing but bad habits, you will not want to work; you would rather beg round the country and feed that bottomless belly of yours."

And Odysseus said:

"Eurymachus, I only wish there could be a contest between the two of us in the spring, when the days grow long — in a hayfield, and I with a curved scythe in my hands and you with another, and plenty of grass, and the two of us at work with nothing to eat until it was fully dark.

"Or oxen to drive, the best there are, big tawny beasts, both of them well fed on grass, the same age and the same strength, and their power not easy to wear out; and a field of four acres, with the soil giving way under the plough. Then you would see whether I could cut an unbroken furrow all the way.

"Or if Zeus were to raise a war somewhere this very day, and I had a shield and two spears and a bronze helmet fitted to my temples — then you would see me in among the front-line men, and you would not be standing there making jokes about my belly.

"But you are extremely insolent and your mind is hard, and you think you are a great man and a strong one because you keep company with a few worthless people. If Odysseus were to come back to his own country, those doors, wide as they are, would very soon be too narrow for you as you ran out through them."

Eurymachus went black with rage. "You wretch, I shall very soon do you an injury for talking so freely among all these men, and not the least bit frightened. Either the wine has your wits, or your mind is always like this and you talk nonsense. Are you so beside yourself because you beat that tramp Irus?"

And he snatched up a footstool. And Odysseus sat down quickly at the knees of Amphinomus of Dulichium, out of the way of Eurymachus; and the stool hit the wine-steward on the right hand, and the jug went down with a clang and the man went over backwards in the dust, groaning.

The suitors made an uproar in the shadowy hall, and one would look at the man beside him and say: "I wish this stranger had died somewhere else before he came here; then he would not have made all this trouble. Now we are brawling about beggars, and there will be no pleasure in a good dinner, because the worse things are winning."

And Telemachus said: "Sirs, you are mad. You are no longer hiding what the food and drink are doing to you; some god is driving you. Now that you have dined well, go home and go to bed whenever your heart tells you. I am not driving anybody out."

They bit their lips and stared at Telemachus, astonished that he had spoken so boldly.

And Amphinomus said: "Friends, when a fair word has been spoken, nobody should take offence and answer it roughly. Do not manhandle the stranger, or any of the servants in the house of godlike Odysseus. Come — let the steward pour a drop into the cups so that we can make our libations and go home to bed. And leave the stranger in the hall of Odysseus for Telemachus to look after; it is to his house that he has come."

That pleased them all. And when they had poured and drunk as much as they wanted, they went off to their own houses to sleep.

## NOTES TO BOOK EIGHTEEN

[^1]: **Irus, who is not called Irus.** His name is Arnaeus. *Irus* is a nickname the young men of the town gave him, and it is a joke: it is made from Iris, the goddess who carries messages for the gods, because this man will carry a message for anybody who pays him in food. It is the sort of name that sticks to a person and slowly replaces him, and the poem tells you his real one exactly once, in the line before it stops using it. There is a second turn of the same joke a page later, when the suitors say he is about to be *un-Irused* — which in Greek is a single coined word, made by putting a negative in front of his nickname, and is the kind of thing a hall full of drunk young men would find extremely funny.

[^2]: **King Echetus.** He is mentioned three times in this poem and nowhere else in Greek literature, always in the same breath as mutilation, always as a threat, and always to somebody helpless. Nobody knows whether he was a figure from some lost story or simply a bogey — the equivalent of naming a place people are sent and do not come back from. What matters is that Antinous produces him casually, as a piece of ordinary banter, to frighten a beggar into fighting; and that nothing in the scene suggests anybody present thought the threat outlandish. It is worth remembering when the reckoning comes, and readers begin to feel that it goes too far.

[^3]: **Melantho, and what the poem tells you about her.** Four facts, in one sentence, delivered without comment: Penelope raised her from a child, treated her as her own daughter, gave her whatever toys she asked for — and she feels nothing for Penelope, and sleeps with Eurymachus. She is the sister of Melanthius the goatherd, who kicked Odysseus at the spring in Book Seventeen; the two of them are the household's answer to Eumaeus and the cowherd, and the poem is arranging its servants into two columns. — She is also one of the twelve women who will be hanged in Book Twenty-Two, and I will not pretend her behaviour here is anything but cruel. But I said in Book Two that I would keep the question open, and I shall: the poem gives her a motive that is entirely her own, and gives her a lover who is the second most powerful man in that hall, and never once asks what a young slave woman's alternatives were when he came looking for her.
