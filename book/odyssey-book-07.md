# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK SEVEN
### In Which There Is a Garden That Is Always in Fruit, and a Queen Who Recognises the Laundry

---

So he prayed there in the grove, while the strong mules carried the girl on into the town.

And when she came to her father's famous house she pulled up at the gateway, and her brothers came out and stood round her looking like immortals, and unyoked the mules and carried the clothes indoors; and she went off to her own room. And her old nurse lit the fire for her — Eurymedousa, who had been brought over from Apeira long ago in the curved ships, and had been picked out of the plunder and given to Alcinous, because he was king over everybody and the people listened to him as though he were a god. She had brought Nausicaa up in that house, and now she lit the fire and set about getting her supper.[^1]

And Odysseus got up to go into the town.

Athena, who was looking after him, poured a thick mist round him as he went, in case one of the great-hearted Phaeacians should meet him on the road and pass some remark and ask him who he was. And just as he was about to step into the town she came to meet him herself, in the shape of a young girl carrying a water pitcher; and she stood there in front of him, and Odysseus said:

"Child, could you take me to the house of a man called Alcinous, who is king over the people here? I am a stranger. I have been through a great deal. I have come a long way, out of a distant country, and I do not know a single soul who lives in this town."

"Certainly, sir," said Athena. "I will show you the house; my own father lives close by it. But go quietly, and let me lead the way, and do not look at anybody and do not ask anybody anything. These people are not very tolerant of strangers, and they do not exactly make a foreigner welcome. What they trust is their fast ships, and crossing the great gulf of the sea, because Poseidon (god of the sea, and the one who has been keeping Odysseus from home these ten years) gave them that."

I hope you have noticed that this is not at all what the girl on the beach told him a few hours ago, and I think the difference is worth a moment of our time before we go in through the gate. Nausicaa said that all strangers and beggars come from Zeus, that a small gift is a welcome one, and that her people are dear to the gods; and she was speaking honestly and out of a generous nature, and every word of it was true. Athena, who has been watching this nation for rather longer than seventeen years, says: keep your head down, they do not much care for outsiders. And that is true as well. The Phaeacians are about to treat this man magnificently — feed him, bathe him, entertain him, load him with gold and carry him home for nothing — and they are also, every one of them, going to make him prove himself first, and one of them is going to insult him to his face in public before the day is out. Hospitality in this world is a law; but laws are kept by people, and people are a mixed business, and a poem that told you otherwise would not be worth reading.

---

She led the way, quickly, and he came along behind her in the goddess's own footsteps; and the Phaeacians, the famous sailors, never noticed him going through the middle of their town, because Athena would not allow it. And as they walked she told him about the woman whose knees he was going to take hold of.

"That house you asked about, sir — the king sits in it, and his wife is called Arete, and she comes of the very same stock the king himself comes from.

"Poseidon began that line, with Periboea, who was the loveliest woman of her time and the youngest daughter of Eurymedon, who was king over the Giants once, and destroyed them, and himself along with them. Poseidon lay with her and she bore a son, Nausithous, who ruled the Phaeacians; and Nausithous had two sons, Rhexenor and Alcinous.

"Apollo (god of prophecy, and of the bow, whose arrows bring sudden death) shot Rhexenor down when he was newly married and had no son in the house, and he left one daughter behind him: Arete. And Alcinous made her his wife, and honours her as no other woman on this earth is honoured — no other woman anywhere who keeps a house under a husband.

"That is the honour she has, and has always had, from her own children, and from Alcinous himself, and from the people, who look at her as though she were a god and speak to her that way when she goes through the town. There is nothing lacking in her own good sense either, and she settles quarrels, even quarrels between men, for the people she thinks well of.

"If she takes to you in her own mind, then there is hope for you: that you will see the people you love, and get back to your own high-roofed house and your own country."

And with that Athena went away over the barren sea, and left lovely Scheria behind her, and came to Marathon and to Athens of the wide streets, and went into the strong house of Erechtheus.

---

Now, at the end of the last book I pointed at Nausicaa's instruction — walk straight past my father and put your arms round my mother's knees — and told you that the explanation was coming and that I would let it arrive in its own good time rather than spoil it. Here it is; and I have to tell you, in all honesty, that it does not explain anything at all.

What we are given is a family tree. A god fathered the line; the elder brother died young leaving one daughter; her uncle married her; and she is honoured as no woman on earth is honoured. And then the same fact three more times over, which is generally a sign that somebody is filling a silence: her children honour her, her husband honours her, the people honour her like a god when she walks down the street.

Notice what is missing from all of that. There is no office. She is not a priestess, and not a judge, and not a co-ruler under any constitutional arrangement that anybody troubles to name; and the one line in the whole speech that describes her actually doing something — that she settles disputes, *even between men* — is thrown out in half a breath as though it followed naturally from what has gone before, which it does not. It follows from nothing. It simply sits there, remarkable and unexplained, in the middle of a genealogy.

So we have been handed an answer with a hole in the middle of it, and the hole is the shape of a woman with real power that nobody in the building can quite account for. And I have come to think that this is the truth of the matter rather than a failure of the poem, because there have been such women in every society that has ever existed, including a great many societies with no legal room for them whatsoever, and the way you can always tell is exactly this: everybody knows to go to her, nobody can point to the rule that says so, and if you ask a local why, you are given a genealogy and a list of the people who respect her.

There is one other thing in that speech which slides past very fast, and I shall mention it once and then leave it alone. She is his brother's daughter, and he married her, and the poem reports this in the same breath as everything else without the smallest flicker of embarrassment — because to its first audience this was not scandal but administration. A royal house that has suddenly lost its heir has a succession problem and a property problem at the same moment, and marrying the orphaned niece to her nearest kinsman solves both of them at a stroke.[^2]

---

Then Odysseus came to the famous house, and stopped where he was, with his heart turning over a great many things, before he stepped onto the bronze threshold.

Because there was a brightness like the sun and the moon over that high-roofed house. The walls ran bronze on both sides, all the way in from the threshold, with a cornice of dark blue enamel round the top of them. Golden doors shut the strong house in; silver doorposts stood on the bronze threshold, with a silver lintel above and a golden handle. And on either side of the doors there were dogs made of gold and silver, that Hephaestus (the lame smith of the gods, who makes everything the immortals cannot make for themselves) had built with his own skill and knowledge, to keep watch over the house of great-hearted Alcinous — deathless dogs, that do not grow old, ever.

Inside, chairs stood along the wall on both sides, the whole way in from the threshold, and light finely-woven covers were thrown over them, the work of the women of the house; and the Phaeacian leaders sat there to eat and drink, because there was always something going on. And there were young men made of gold standing on their well-built pedestals, holding burning torches in their hands, to light the hall at night for the people at dinner.[^3]

And there were fifty serving women in that house. Some of them grind the yellow grain at the millstone; others weave at the loom, or sit twirling the spindles, and their hands move like the leaves of a tall poplar; and the close-woven linen is so smooth that the oil runs off it. For the Phaeacian men are the best sailors in the world at driving a fast ship over the sea, and their women are the best in the world at the loom, because Athena gave them the skill for it, and good sense besides.

And outside the courtyard gate there was an orchard of four acres, with a hedge running round both sides of it and tall trees flourishing inside: pears and pomegranates and apples with their bright fruit, and sweet figs, and olives in their prime. And the fruit of those trees never dies and never fails, winter or summer, the whole year round; there is always a west wind blowing there, and it is always bringing some fruit on and ripening others, so that pear grows old upon pear, and apple upon apple, grape upon grape and fig upon fig. And there was a vineyard rooted in the same ground, heavy with grapes, and part of it a level drying-floor where the bunches lie in the sun; and other clusters they are gathering, and others they are treading; while in the front rows the unripe grapes are still dropping their blossom, and others just beginning to darken. And at the far end of the vineyard there were neat beds of vegetables of every kind, green all the year. And two springs ran there: one of them led all through the garden, and the other going the other way, out under the courtyard threshold and up towards the high house, and the townspeople came and drew their water from that one.

That was the gods' shining gift in the house of Alcinous.

---

And Odysseus stood outside in the road and looked at it, and I should like to stand there with him a moment, because he has been in a rich house before and this is not the same thing at all.

You will remember Menelaus's palace in Book Four, where this same young man's own son stood in the doorway and said out loud that it must be what the house of Zeus looks like inside, and was gently corrected by his host. But that house was full of *treasure*: gold and amber and ivory and silver, every piece of it carried home in a ship, and every piece of it a receipt for eight years of wandering about the eastern sea while his brother was being murdered at his own front door. Menelaus recited the inventory and then told the boy, in the same breath, that he would hand over two thirds of it tomorrow to have the dead men back.

This is not treasure. What Odysseus is looking at over that hedge is a working farm with the seasons taken out of it.

And I would ask you to notice what the poem chooses to dwell on, at length and with evident pleasure, in the wealthiest house it will ever show us. Not the gold. The gold gets a line and a half. It is the fruit that gets the paragraph: four acres of pears and figs and olives, and a vineyard standing at every stage of the year all at once, some of it being picked and some of it being trodden and some of it still in flower; and the vegetable beds; and the two springs, one of which is led out under the wall so that the neighbours can fill their jars at the king's water. The marvel of the place is not that its owner is rich. It is that nothing there ever fails, and nobody there ever has to wait.

That is a fairy-tale, of course, and the poem knows quite well that it is a fairy-tale; no one has ever farmed a country where the wind is always westerly and the apples are always coming. But it is a very particular fairy-tale, and it is precisely the one that a farming people would dream, because it is made entirely out of the absence of the things that frighten them. No hungry gap in February. No hail the week before harvest. No year when the olives simply do not come.

And there is a man standing at the gate looking at it who has been at sea for ten years; whose own orchard is on a stony island where the goats do better than the grain; and whose father, at this moment, is sleeping in the ashes by the fire with the servants, out in the real dirt and the actual weather, grieving.

He looks at it for a long time. Then he goes in.

---

Inside, the Phaeacian leaders were pouring the last of their wine to Hermes (the messenger of the gods, and the one who walks with travellers), which is what they always did at the end of an evening before they began thinking about bed. And Odysseus went through the house wrapped in the thick mist Athena had poured round him, until he came to Arete and to the king, and threw his arms round Arete's knees.

And at that moment the miraculous mist fell away from him, and every person in that hall found themselves looking at a man who had not been there a second before. They went silent. They stared. They wondered.

And he made his appeal.

"Arete, daughter of godlike Rhexenor. I come to your husband, and to your knees, and to these guests, after a very great deal of suffering. May the gods grant them all good lives, and may every man here hand on to his children the wealth in his house and whatever honour the people have given him. But arrange an escort for me, so that I can get to my own country quickly. I have been away from the people I love a long time, and I have suffered."

And he sat down in the ashes of the hearth, beside the fire.

And nobody said a single word.

---

That is the most awkward pause in the poem so far, and it deserves to be looked at rather than hurried past, because a great deal is going on inside it.

A man has appeared out of nothing in the middle of a closed room and taken hold of the strongest position their law allows to a helpless person: his arms are round the queen's knees, which puts him under the protection of Zeus himself and makes shaking him off an act of impiety rather than mere rudeness. Then he lets go and sits down in the fireplace — in the ash, at the hearth, which is at once the sacred centre of the house and the coldest and lowest place in it to put a guest. It is a gesture of complete self-abasement, and, this being Odysseus, it is also completely unanswerable. Every person in that room now has a religious problem sitting in their fire, and not one of them can think what to say about it.

What breaks the silence is an old man. Echeneus was the oldest Phaeacian present and the best speaker among them, and he knew a great many old things; and he said, in effect, what everybody in the room should have said several seconds earlier — that this would not do, that it did the king no credit to have a stranger sitting on the ground in the ashes, that everybody was holding back and waiting for him, and that the man should be got up out of the fireplace at once and put in a silver-studded chair, and the wine mixed, and a proper supper brought out of the stores.

And two things happen next which I would not like you to miss, because they are small and they tell you a great deal about how this poem thinks good rule works. The first is that the correction comes from the oldest man in the room, in front of the whole council, and the king takes it instantly and without the smallest flicker of resentment — which is the point. Good kingship in this poem is not a matter of never getting it wrong; it is a matter of what happens when a man of ninety tells you that you have. And the second is that when Alcinous needs a seat of honour for the stranger, he turns his own favourite son out of one: he tells Laodamas, who was sitting next to him and whom he loved best, to get up and go and sit somewhere else. That is how honour worked in a world with no money in it. There were only so many good chairs, and giving one to somebody always meant taking it away from somebody else, and everybody in the hall saw who moved.

---

Then a maid brought water in a beautiful golden jug and poured it out over a silver basin for him to wash his hands, and drew up a polished table beside him; and the housekeeper brought bread and a great many good things out of her stores; and the man who had endured everything ate and drank.

And Alcinous told the herald to mix the bowl and serve everyone in the hall, so that they could pour out to Zeus, who walks in beside anybody who deserves respect. And when they had poured, and drunk as much as they wanted, the king dismissed the council for the night. In the morning, he said, they would call in more of the elders and entertain the stranger properly and make good sacrifices, and then see about his escort, so that this man could get to his own country without trouble and without pain, however far away it was; and after that he would suffer whatever his fate was, and whatever the heavy Spinners spun into his thread on the day his mother bore him.[^4]

"But if he is one of the immortals come down out of the sky," he added, "then the gods are up to something new. Because up to now they have always shown themselves to us plainly whenever we make them a proper sacrifice, and they sit down and eat beside us; and if one of us meets them out on the road on his own, they do not hide themselves, because we are close to them — the way the Cyclopes are, and the wild tribes of the Giants."

And Odysseus said:

"Alcinous, get that out of your head. I am nothing like the immortals who hold the wide sky, not in my body and not in what I am. I am like men who die.

"Whoever you know who carries the heaviest load of sorrow among human beings — I will set my griefs against theirs and match them. And I could tell you a great deal more, and everything I have suffered through the will of the gods.

"But let me eat, in spite of my grief. Because there is nothing more shameless than a man's cursed belly, which insists on being remembered, however badly he is hurt and however much sorrow he has in his mind. I have sorrow in my mind. And it keeps telling me to eat and drink, and it makes me forget everything I have been through, and it will not stop until it is filled.

"But hurry, all of you, at first light. Put me down on my own country, wretched as I am. I have suffered enough. Let the life leave me the moment I have seen my own property, and my servants, and my great high-roofed house."

---

That is one of the strangest and best speeches in the poem, and it is not heroic in the least, and that is exactly the point of it.

A man is being asked, in the politest possible terms, whether he might not be a god in disguise. And his answer is that he is not, and his proof is that he is hungry.

Set it for a moment beside the other poem, because the two of them are arguing here and it is worth hearing the argument. In the *Iliad*, when Achilles's dearest friend is killed and he is torn to pieces by grief, they bring him food and he will not touch it; and his refusal to eat is presented as the true and towering response of a great soul, so that in the end a goddess has to come down and feed him in secret because he will not do it for himself. Grief in that poem is a thing you starve on. It lifts you clear of the ordinary business of the body and sets you above other men.

This poem does not believe a word of it.

What this poem says, through the mouth of a man sitting in borrowed clothes at a stranger's table, is that the belly is shameless; that it nags; that it will not permit you to concentrate properly on your own tragedy. However much you have lost, however far from home you are, however many of your friends have been eaten or drowned in front of you, there comes a point in the afternoon when the appalling fact is that you would like your dinner — and you will eat it, and while you are eating it you will actually stop thinking about the thing for a few minutes, which is somehow the most humiliating part of the whole business.

And he is not saying this lightly or as a joke. He says it with real bitterness — *the cursed belly*, the thing that makes you forget. He would very much rather be the sort of man who cannot eat. He is not that sort of man. Almost nobody is.

And the poem's judgement on him, offered quietly and without any fuss at all, is that this is not a failure. It is how a person gets through twenty years. The man who can eat when he is heartbroken is the man who is still alive when the ship goes down; and the reason he gets home in the end is not that his grief was smaller than anybody else's. It is that he kept eating.

---

Then they poured the last of the wine and went off home to bed, and Odysseus was left in the hall with Arete and Alcinous sitting beside him, while the maids cleared the dishes away.

And Arete spoke first, because she had recognised the clothes. She had looked at the cloak and the tunic on his back and known them at once, having made them herself with her own women.

"Stranger. There is something I should like to ask you first, myself. Who are you? Where are you from? Who gave you those clothes? Did you not say you came here wandering, over the sea?"

And Odysseus told her — and told her the truth, which is worth pausing on, because he is going to spend a great deal of the rest of this poem doing nothing of the kind. He told her about the island of Ogygia and the goddess Calypso; about the ship split open by lightning and the crew drowned, and nine days clinging to the keel; about the seven years, and the offer of deathlessness that never once persuaded him; about the raft, and the seventeen days of open water, and Poseidon smashing him within sight of land; about the river, and the leaves.

And then he came to the part with the girl in it, and there he was careful.

"And I saw your daughter's maids playing on the shore, and her among them, looking like a goddess; and I asked her for help. And she showed better sense than you would ever expect from somebody of that age — you do not generally find young people being sensible; they are usually thoughtless. She gave me bread, and red wine, and had me washed in the river, and gave me these clothes. That is the truth, painful as it is to tell."

And Alcinous said: "Stranger, there is one thing my child did not handle properly. She should have brought you here herself, with her women, since it was to her that you first appealed."

And Odysseus lied to him.

"Sir, do not blame your blameless daughter for that. She did tell me to follow her with the women. I was the one who refused. I was afraid, and I did not want you to be angry at the sight of it. We are a suspicious lot, we people on this earth."

---

I have been waiting several books to say this, so you will forgive me for stopping in the middle of a dinner party to say it properly.

That is the first lie we ever watch Odysseus tell. Not the first he has ever told, of course — this is the man who walked into Troy dressed as a beggar and walked out again over a pile of bodies, and we have heard two separate people describe it — but it is the first one told in the present tense of this poem, in front of us, in a room, in real time. And from here to the very last pages he will be lying almost continuously: false names, false countries, four or five entirely invented lives, told to swineherds and beggars and princes and, in the end, to his own wife.

And this first one is a lie told by a shipwrecked stranger with nothing in the world, to the king whose goodwill is the only thing standing between him and dying on that island, for the sole purpose of lifting a mild fatherly criticism off the shoulders of a seventeen-year-old girl who helped him.

He gains nothing whatever by it. It costs him a small risk, at the worst possible moment to be taking small risks. And notice precisely where the falsehood lies, because it is beautifully placed: he does not lie about whether she behaved well. He lies about *whose decision it was*. He takes her careful calculation about the gossips in the town — which she made deliberately, and explained to him at length on the road — and quietly moves it onto his own account, where a father's disapproval cannot reach it.

Keep that firmly in mind two hundred pages from now, when he is telling an enormous falsehood to somebody who loves him and you find yourself wondering what to think. It is the same faculty at work. Lying is not a moral quality in this man at all; it is a tool, and it does whatever the hand holding it wants done. Which is why the interesting question about him is never *is he lying* — he is, almost always — but *what is he protecting*.

---

And Alcinous answered him, and it must be said that Alcinous is a thoroughly decent man.

"Stranger, my heart is not the kind that gets angry for no reason. Everything is better done properly. By Father Zeus, and Athena, and Apollo — a man like you, thinking the way I think, I could wish you would have my daughter, and be called my son-in-law, and stay here. I would give you a house and everything in it, if you stayed of your own free will. But nobody in this country will hold you against it; Zeus forbid.

"Your escort I shall fix for tomorrow, so that you may know it is settled. You will lie down and sleep, overcome, while they row you over the calm water until you reach your own country and your own house, or wherever else you like — even if it is further off than Euboea, which the people here say is the furthest place there is. Some of our men saw it once, when they carried Rhadamanthus over to visit Tityos; and they went all that way and came home again the same day without turning a hair.

"You will find out for yourself how good my ships are, and how good my young men are at throwing up the sea with the flat of an oar."[^5]

And Odysseus was delighted, and prayed aloud: "Father Zeus, let Alcinous do everything he has said. Let his fame never go out over the grain-giving earth. And let me get to my own country."

Now I want you to notice what he did not say, because the silence is the whole of it.

A king has just offered him a daughter, a house, an estate and a place among a people the gods sit down to dinner with; and has said, in the same breath and with real grace, that he will not be held to any of it and may leave whenever he likes. It is the third such offer he has been made in this poem — a goddess offered him deathlessness, a girl on a beach said out loud in front of her maids that she wished he could be her husband, and now this — and it is by some way the best-made of the three, coming from a good man who means it kindly and who has taken care to leave the door open behind it.

And Odysseus neither accepts nor refuses. He prays for a safe journey, out loud, in front of the man who has just made the offer, and lets that stand as his answer.

There is nothing else to be said, and he knows it. He said the true thing yesterday morning, on a beach, to this man's daughter, when he had nothing on but a branch: that there is nothing in this world better or stronger than a man and a woman keeping house together with one mind between them. He has one. It is a very long way from here, and he has not seen it for twenty years, and he is not certain it is still there. Everything he turns down, he turns down for that.

---

Then Arete told her women to put a bed under the covered walk, and to lay beautiful purple blankets on it and spread rugs over them and put fleecy cloaks on top for a covering. And they went out of the hall with torches in their hands, and when they had made the bed up they came and stood beside Odysseus and said: "Come and lie down, stranger; your bed is made."

And he was very glad to sleep.

So the man who had endured everything slept there, in the corded bed, under the echoing colonnade. And Alcinous lay down in an inner room of the high house, and his wife lay beside him, and she had made up the bed.

## NOTES TO BOOK SEVEN

[^1]: **Eurymedousa.** She was taken from a place called Apeira in the curved ships and given to Alcinous as his share of the plunder, because he was king; and then she raised his daughter. She gets two lines and no further mention, and she is the fourth household we have been shown — after Ithaca, Sparta and Pylos — in which the woman who brought up the children arrived as somebody's property. The pattern is worth naming plainly this once and then leaving alone: whenever this poem wants to show you a house running warmly and well, there is a woman at the centre of the domestic arrangements who was carried there in a ship against her will. See Book One, note 10, and Book Four, note 1.

[^2]: **Arete.** On the marriage: an uncle marrying his brother's orphaned daughter would have raised no eyebrows in a royal house that had just lost its heir, since it kept the succession, the property and the claim inside one family at a single stroke. Greek practice in later centuries permitted marriage between an uncle and a niece for exactly this reason — an heiress with no brothers was very often married to her nearest kinsman on the father's side, precisely so that the estate should not walk out of the family. The poem states it flatly because to its audience it was administration and not scandal. — On the name: it stands close to a Greek verb meaning to pray for or to entreat, and so carries a faint shadow of *the one who is prayed to*, which suits a woman whose knees a suppliant is instructed to grasp; and it is also close to *aretē*, excellence. The Greeks enjoyed this sort of resonance and were not at all systematic about it. I would not build very much on it, but it is there.

[^3]: **The gold dogs and the gold torch-bearers.** These are not ornaments. The dogs are deathless and ageless and they are on guard, and the young men holding the torches are standing on pedestals doing a job of work; they are made things that function — machines in the shape of servants and animals, built by the smith of the gods — and they are among the earliest of their kind in European literature. Hephaestus does this elsewhere too: in the *Iliad* he owns twenty tripods on golden wheels that roll into the gods' assembly by themselves and roll home again afterwards, and he is attended by handmaids made out of gold who can think and speak and have strength in them. What strikes me most is how completely untroubled everybody is. Nobody in this poem finds the golden dogs uncanny, or asks whether they are alive, or wonders what they do when the smith is not watching. They are simply the finest work of the finest craftsman there is, and the mark of the finest house there is, and the guests walk past them in to dinner.

[^4]: **The Spinners.** The Fates: three of them in the developed mythology, one who spins the thread of a life, one who measures it and one who cuts it. But notice how lightly the poem holds all this. Alcinous says the stranger will suffer whatever was spun for him at his birth, and then in the very same speech makes elaborate practical arrangements to see that he suffers as little as possible — and nobody present feels the least tension between the two. That is characteristic. Fate in this poem is not a machine that removes choice; it is closer to the shape of a life as seen from the outside, or from the end. People still deliberate, take advice, change their minds and are held responsible for what they do; and Zeus himself, in the first hundred lines of Book One, complains bitterly that men blame the gods for troubles they have manufactured entirely on their own.

[^5]: **Euboea, and the size of the world.** Alcinous names Euboea as the furthest place his people have ever heard of, out at the very rim of things, so distant that his sailors still talk about the voyage. Euboea is a long island lying off the east coast of mainland Greece, close enough to the shore in places to shout across. It is, in other words, extremely near — near to Athens, near to Boeotia, near to the poet and near to his audience. The joke is aimed gently at the Phaeacians: for all their magical ships, their sense of the world is a fairy-tale sense, and the ends of the earth to them are somebody else's back garden. It is also, in a poem so preoccupied with distance, a quiet piece of consolation. The furthest place in the world is a day's sail, if you have the right ship.

*Book Eight: In Which a Blind Man Sings, a Young Man Is Rude About Sport, and a Stranger Cries Into His Cloak.*
