# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK TWENTY
### In Which Nobody Sleeps, and the Last Dinner Is Served

---

Odysseus lay down to sleep in the forecourt. He spread an undressed oxhide on the ground and heaped over it a great many fleeces from the sheep the Greeks had been killing, and Eurynome threw a cloak over him when he had lain down. And there he lay awake, turning over trouble for the suitors in his mind.

And the women came out of the hall, the ones who had been going with the suitors, laughing among themselves and making merry together. And the heart in his breast was stirred, and he debated hard, in his mind and in his heart, whether to rush out and deal death to every one of them, or to let them lie with the arrogant suitors this one last time. And the heart inside him growled.

As a bitch stands over her tender puppies and growls at a man she does not know, and is ready to fight him, so the heart growled inside him at their wickedness. And he struck himself on the breast and spoke to his own heart.

"Endure this, heart. You put up with something fouler than this on the day the Cyclops, who had no restraint in him, ate my strong companions. And you held out, until cunning got you out of that cave when you had thought you were dead."

So he spoke, rebuking the heart in his own breast; and his heart held firm and stayed obedient. But the man himself went on tossing from side to side.

As a man turns a paunch stuffed with fat and blood this way and that over a blazing fire, wanting it roasted quickly, so he rolled from side to side, working out how he was ever going to lay hands on the shameless suitors, one man against so many.[^1]

And Athena came down to him from the sky, in the likeness of a woman, and stood over his head.

"Why are you awake, unluckiest of all men? This is your house. Your wife is in it, and your son — and a son any man would be glad of."

"Goddess, everything you say is right. But my heart keeps working at this: how am I to lay hands on the shameless suitors, alone as I am, when they are always here in a body? And there is a bigger thing than that which I keep turning over. If I do kill them, by the will of Zeus and yourself, where am I to escape to afterwards? I ask you to think about that."

"You obstinate man. Most people are content to trust a weaker friend than I am — a mortal, who does not know as much. I am a god, and I have guarded you the whole way through, in every one of your troubles. I shall put it plainly: if fifty companies of mortal men stood round the two of us, wanting to kill us in a fight, you would drive their cattle and their fat sheep away afterwards. So let sleep take you. It is a weariness to lie awake and keep watch all night. You are coming up out of your troubles now."

And she poured sleep over his eyelids and went back to Olympus.

---

But as sleep took hold of him, loosening the cares of his heart, his true wife woke, and sat up in her soft bed and cried; and when she had had her fill of crying she prayed first of all to Artemis (goddess of the hunt, whose arrows bring a quiet death to women).

"Artemis, honoured goddess, daughter of Zeus — I wish you would put an arrow in my breast this minute and take my life away. Or let a storm wind catch me up and carry me off down the misty ways and throw me out where the circling Ocean pours in, the way the storm winds carried off the daughters of Pandareus.

"The gods had killed their parents and left them orphans in the house; and Aphrodite (goddess of love) fed them on cheese and sweet honey and good wine, and Hera (queen of the gods) gave them beauty and sense beyond all other women, and holy Artemis gave them height, and Athena taught them how to do glorious work with their hands. And while Aphrodite was going up to high Olympus to ask Zeus to arrange happy marriages for them, the storm winds snatched the girls away and handed them over to the hateful Furies to look after.

"I wish those who live on Olympus would blot me out that way, or that Artemis of the lovely hair would strike me, so that I might go down under the hateful earth still looking at Odysseus, and never gladden the heart of a lesser man.

"An evil is bearable when a person cries all day, with a heavy heart, but sleep takes hold of them at night; because sleep makes you forget everything, good and bad, once it has closed your eyelids. But some god sends me bad dreams as well.

"This very night there was somebody lying beside me, like him, looking as he did when he went away with the army; and my heart was glad, because I thought it was not a dream but the truth at last."

She said that, and the golden Dawn came up.

And Odysseus, lying there, heard the sound of her crying; and in the drowsy state between sleeping and waking it seemed to him that she already knew him, and was standing beside his head. So he gathered up the cloak and the fleeces he had been lying on and put them on a chair in the hall, and carried the oxhide out of doors, and lifted his hands and prayed to Zeus.

"Father Zeus, if you gods brought me over the dry land and the water to my own country of your own will, after you had afflicted me so badly — then let one of the people waking in this house speak a word of omen for me, and let some other sign be shown to me outside."

And Zeus the counsellor heard him, and thundered at once out of shining Olympus, from high up above the clouds; and Odysseus was glad.

And a woman grinding grain spoke the word, close by, in the house, where the king's mills stood. Twelve women in all worked at them, making barley meal and wheat flour, which is the marrow of men. The others were asleep, having ground their wheat, but this one alone had not finished yet, because she was the weakest of them. She stopped her mill and spoke the words that were a sign for her master.

"Father Zeus, who rule over gods and men — you have thundered loudly out of the starry sky, and there is not a cloud anywhere. You must be showing somebody a sign.

"Bring about what I am going to say, wretched as I am. Let this be the very last day the suitors take their sweet dinner in the halls of Odysseus. They have loosened my knees with the miserable work of grinding their meal. Let them eat their last supper now."

And Odysseus was glad at the word of omen and at the thunder of Zeus, because he believed he was going to make the guilty pay.[^2]

---

The other women were gathering in the fine house by now and kindling the unwearying fire on the hearth; and Telemachus got out of bed and dressed and slung his sharp sword over his shoulder and tied his good sandals under his shining feet, and took up his heavy spear with the sharp bronze point.

He stopped in the doorway and said to Eurycleia: "Dear nurse, did you honour the stranger in the house with a bed and food, or is he lying about uncared for? Because that is my mother's way, sensible as she is. She will honour a worse man blindly, and send a better one away without honour."

"You should not blame her now, child, when she is not to blame. He sat and drank his wine as long as he wanted, and said he was not hungry any more, because she asked him. And when he began to think about bed and sleep, she told the women to make one up for him. But he was like a man who is completely miserable and out of luck, and he refused to sleep in a bed under blankets. He lay in the forecourt on an undressed oxhide and some sheepskins, and we threw a cloak over him."

So Telemachus went out through the hall with his spear in his hand, and two swift dogs went with him, and he made his way to the assembly place. And Eurycleia set the women to work: sweeping the house and sprinkling the floors, putting the purple covers on the chairs, wiping down the tables with sponges, cleaning the mixing bowls and the two-handled cups. Twenty of them went off to the spring for water, and the rest worked in the house, deft about their business.

And the suitors' men came in and began splitting wood skilfully; and the women came back from the spring. And Eumaeus came after them, driving three fat hogs, the best of the whole herd. He left them to feed in the fine yard, and he spoke to Odysseus kindly.

"Stranger, are the Greeks looking at you any better now, or are they still treating you badly in the halls, as before?"

"Eumaeus, I only wish the gods would pay them back for the outrage they commit in another man's house, insolently, without a shred of shame in them."

And while they were talking, Melanthius the goatherd came up, driving in the best goats of all his flocks for the suitors' dinner, and two herdsmen came with him; and he tied the goats under the echoing colonnade and spoke to Odysseus again with his usual sneer.

"Stranger, are you still going to be a nuisance here in this house, begging off the gentlemen? Are you never going to clear out? I do not think the two of us are going to part without tasting each other's fists, because you beg past all decency. There are other dinners among the Greeks."

Odysseus did not answer him a word. He shook his head in silence, turning over trouble in the depths of him.

And a third man came in, Philoetius, a leader among men, bringing a barren heifer and some fat goats for the suitors; the ferrymen had brought them over, who take anybody across who comes their way. He tied the animals up carefully under the echoing colonnade, and came and stood beside the swineherd.

"Who is this stranger, swineherd, who has just come to our house? What family does he claim? Where are his people and his father's fields? Poor man. He has the build of a king about him; but the gods bring misery on men who have wandered a long way, once they have spun trouble for them, kings included."

And he took Odysseus by the hand and said:

"Good day, father stranger. May you have some happiness in the time to come; you are held down by a great many troubles at present.

"Father Zeus, no god is more deadly than you. You have no pity for men, though you made them yourself; you let them get acquainted with misery and hard pain.

"I broke out in a sweat when I saw you, and my eyes are full of tears, because I thought of Odysseus. I expect he is dressed in rags like these too, wandering about among people somewhere, if he is still alive and looking at the sunlight.

"And if he is dead already and in the house of Hades — then I grieve for faultless Odysseus, who set me over his cattle when I was only a boy in the country of the Cephallenians. They have increased past counting since then. No man's herd of broad-browed cattle could have grown better.

"And now other men order me to drive them in for themselves to eat. They take no notice of the son in the house and no notice of the anger of the gods, because they are in a hurry to divide up the property of a master who has been away too long.

"And here is the thing that keeps turning over in my heart. It is a very bad business, while the son is alive, to take the cattle and go off to another country, to strangers. And it is worse still to stay here in misery, minding cattle that belong to other men. I would have run long ago and gone to one of the other great kings, because this is past bearing. But I keep thinking about that unhappy man, and hoping he may come from somewhere and scatter the suitors through the halls."

"Cowherd," said Odysseus, "since you seem to be neither a bad man nor a fool, and I can see for myself that you have sense in your mind, I shall tell you something and swear a great oath on it. Let Zeus be my witness first, and this table of hospitality, and the hearth of Odysseus that I have come to: Odysseus will come home while you are still here, and you shall see with your own eyes, if you want to, the killing of the suitors who lord it in this house."

"Stranger, I wish the son of Cronos would bring that word about. Then you would find out what my strength is, and how these hands of mine obey me."

And Eumaeus prayed to all the gods in the same way, that clever Odysseus might come back to his own house.

---

Meanwhile the suitors were weaving death and destruction for Telemachus. But a bird came over on their left, an eagle flying high with a trembling dove in its claws; and Amphinomus said: "Friends, this plan of ours will not run smoothly — the killing of Telemachus. Let us think about dinner instead." That pleased them all, and they went into the house and laid their cloaks on the chairs and benches and set about killing sheep and goats and fat hogs and a heifer from the herd.

And Telemachus put Odysseus in the great hall itself, beside the stone threshold, on a shabby stool at a small table, and gave him a portion of the inner meat and poured wine for him in a golden cup.

"Sit there now among the gentlemen and drink your wine. I shall keep the taunts and the hands of all the suitors off you myself, because this is not a public house; it is the house of Odysseus, and he won it for me. And you, suitors, hold your tongues from insults and your hands from blows, so that no quarrel and no fighting starts."

They all bit their lips and were astonished at Telemachus, that he spoke so boldly. And Antinous said: "Hard as it is, let us take what Telemachus says, though he threatens us a good deal. Zeus did not allow it, or we should have stopped him talking in these halls before now, clear speaker though he is."

Telemachus took no notice of him at all.

And by now the heralds were bringing the holy sacrifice for the gods through the town, and the long-haired Greeks were gathering in the shady grove of Apollo, who shoots from far off.[^3]

And in the house they roasted the outer meat and drew it off the spits and divided the portions and had a splendid dinner; and the men who were serving put down a portion in front of Odysseus equal to their own, because Telemachus had told them to.

But Athena would not let the arrogant suitors leave off their bitter insulting altogether, so that the pain might sink deeper still into the heart of Odysseus.

There was a man among them with a lawless mind, whose name was Ctesippus, and he lived on Same; and trusting in his enormous wealth he was courting the wife of the long-absent master of the house. And he spoke up now to the arrogant suitors.

"Listen to me, my lords. The stranger has had an equal portion for some time, as is only proper; it would not be decent or right to cheat the guests of Telemachus, whoever comes to this house. But come — I shall give him a guest-gift myself, so that he can pass it on as a present to the bath-woman, or to one of the other slaves in the house of Odysseus."

And with that he picked up an ox's foot out of the basket where it lay, and threw it with his heavy hand.

And Odysseus avoided it by turning his head aside a little, and smiled in his heart a very grim smile indeed; and the foot hit the well-built wall.

And Telemachus rounded on Ctesippus.

"It has turned out a great deal better for your life that you did not hit the stranger; he got out of the way of your missile himself. Otherwise I should have run you through the middle with my sharp spear, and your father would have been arranging a funeral here instead of a wedding.

"So let nobody in my house make a display of unseemly behaviour. I notice everything now and I understand it, the good and the bad alike; before this I was a child.

"But even so we put up with watching all this — sheep slaughtered and wine drunk and food eaten — because it is hard for one man to hold back a great many. Still: do me no more harm out of your hostility. And if you are set on killing me with the sword, then I would rather have that. It would be far better to die than to go on watching these disgraceful things forever — guests knocked about, and men dragging the serving women shamefully through this beautiful house."

They all went very quiet. And after a while Agelaus said:

"Friends, when a just word has been spoken nobody should take offence and answer it back angrily. Do not manhandle the stranger, or any of the servants in the house of godlike Odysseus.

"But I would say a gentle word to Telemachus himself and to his mother, in the hope that it pleases them both. As long as the hearts in your breasts still hoped that Odysseus would come home, nobody could blame you for waiting and keeping the suitors in the house; that was the better course, if Odysseus had come back. But now it is perfectly plain that he never will.

"So go and sit beside your mother and tell her this: to marry the best man, the one who offers the most, so that you can keep everything your father owned, eating and drinking, while she looks after somebody else's house."

"Agelaus, I swear by Zeus and by my father's sufferings, who has died somewhere far from Ithaca or is wandering still — I am not putting my mother's marriage off in any way. I tell her to marry whoever she likes, and I offer gifts past counting.

"But I am ashamed to drive her out of the hall against her will by saying so outright. God forbid that."

---

And then Athena stirred up unquenchable laughter in the suitors and knocked their wits sideways.

They were laughing now with jaws that seemed to belong to somebody else. The meat they were eating was fouled with blood, and their eyes were filling with tears, and their hearts had turned towards wailing.

And Theoclymenus, who saw as a prophet sees, spoke among them.

"You poor wretches, what is this trouble you are suffering? Your heads and your faces and your knees are wrapped in night. There is a burning sound of wailing going up, and your cheeks are wet with tears. The walls are sprinkled with blood, and the fine crossbeams too. The porch is full of ghosts and the courtyard is full of them, hurrying down into the dark under the earth. And the sun has died out of the sky, and an evil mist has run over everything."

And they all laughed at him very merrily, and Eurymachus said: "This stranger has just come from abroad and he has lost his mind. Quick, young men, take him out of doors and see him to the assembly place, since he finds it dark in here."

"Eurymachus, I am not asking you for an escort. I have eyes and ears and both my feet and a perfectly sound mind in my breast. I shall take myself out through that door, because I can see the evil coming for you, and not one of the suitors will get away from it — not one of you who insult men and plan wanton things in the house of godlike Odysseus."

And with that he went out of the hall and came to the house of Peiraeus, who was glad to see him.

---

I must stop for a moment over that passage, because it is the strangest in the poem and a reader who is not warned may take it for nonsense. What is being described is a room in which twenty or so young men are laughing helplessly and cannot stop, so that their own jaws seem to have been borrowed from somebody else; and their eyes are streaming, as eyes will when laughter has gone past the point of pleasure; and the meat in their hands is running with blood, which is the ordinary condition of half-cooked meat and also, this evening, something else altogether. They are enjoying themselves enormously, and every single thing about the way they are enjoying themselves is wrong, and not one of them can see it. Only the prophet standing in the middle of the room sees it, and what he sees is the same room a few hours later, with the blood on the walls and the crowd of ghosts going out through the porch and down into the dark; and he tells them so, plainly and at once, and they laugh at him as well and suggest that somebody show him to the door. For twenty lines the poem lifts the roof off a dining room and lets us look at the men inside it from outside time, as the dead men they have already become; and then it puts the roof back on, and they go on with their dinner.

---

And they went on provoking Telemachus, glancing at one another and making fun of his guests. And one of the arrogant young men would say:

"Telemachus, nobody in the world has worse luck with visitors than you. Look at the tramp you keep, wanting his bread and his wine, no use at any kind of work, no strength in him, a dead weight on the good earth. And this other one gets up on his feet to prophesy at us. If you would take my advice, it would pay you much better to throw the pair of them onto a ship and send them off to the Sicilians. They would fetch you a good price."

That is what the suitors said; and Telemachus took no notice of them. He watched his father in silence, waiting for the moment when he would put out his hands against the shameless men.

And Penelope, the daughter of Icarius, had set her lovely chair opposite them, and she heard every word every man of them spoke in that hall.

They had prepared their dinner with a great deal of laughter, and it was a good one and there was plenty of it, because they had killed a great many animals.

But there could never be a dinner less pleasant than the one the goddess and the powerful man were shortly going to set in front of them. And they had brought it on themselves.[^4]

## NOTES TO BOOK TWENTY

[^1]: **"Endure, my heart."** A man lying awake in the dark, so angry that he can feel it moving in his chest, hits himself and tells the thing to be quiet — and then reminds it, as one might remind a frightened animal or a subordinate, of a worse night the two of them got through together. It is one of the earliest passages in European literature in which a person addresses part of himself as though it were somebody else in the room, and it was still being quoted a thousand years later by philosophers who took it for the model of self-command: the reasoning man speaking to his own passion, and the passion obeying him. Two things are worth noticing beyond that. The first is that it does not entirely work, since the heart obeys and the man himself lies there rolling from side to side like something on a griddle, and the poem gives that its own simile, which is the least dignified in the book and exactly right. The second is what he reaches for when he needs steadying: not his wife, not his son, not his kingdom, but the cave, and the men who were eaten in it, and the fact that he got out — which after twenty years is still the thing he uses to prove to himself that he can bear something.

[^2]: **Two omens, and the woman at the mill.** He asks for two signs and is given both at once, which is how these things tend to work in this poem: a sign from the sky, and a word spoken inside the house by somebody who has no idea they are speaking it. The thunder comes out of a clear sky, and the words come from the last woman still working at the querns after the other eleven have finished and gone to sleep, because she is the weakest of them and the slowest at the work. What she prays for is not justice, or vengeance, or her master's return, but simply that the men whose bread she is grinding should eat their last meal today, because her knees hurt. It is the only time in the whole poem that we hear from one of the women who do the grinding, and it lasts eight lines; the mills of a great house were worked by hand, all day, by slaves, and it was reckoned the hardest indoor work there was. The poem knows that perfectly well, and it gives the omen to the person in the building who is worst off, and Odysseus takes it as heaven speaking.

[^3]: **A feast day of Apollo.** The line goes past in half a sentence and is easy to miss, but the whole of the next two books rests on it. This is a public festival of Apollo, the archer god, whose particular business is the bow; the townspeople are out at his grove making sacrifice while the suitors dine. So the contest that Penelope announced last night, and everything that comes of it, falls on the god's own day — which is why nobody in the story finds it odd that a bow should be brought out, and why, when the arrow finally goes, the poem can let a reader feel that something older and larger than this household has taken a hand in the business.

[^4]: **Theoclymenus, and where he goes.** This is the last we see of him. He was picked up on a beach in Book Fifteen, a fugitive with blood on his hands and the most distinguished prophetic ancestry in Greece, and he has now done the one thing he was brought into the poem to do. It is worth noticing that of everybody in that hall he is the only man who walks out of it alive by his own choice — and that he does so immediately after being told, by the second most powerful man in the room, that he must be mad and had better be shown to the door. The suitors are offered exactly one clear warning apiece in these last books: Amphinomus got his from Odysseus and sat down again, and the rest get theirs from a prophet standing in front of them describing their own deaths, and they laugh.
