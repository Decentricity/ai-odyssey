# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK SEVENTEEN
### In Which an Old Dog Waits on a Dunghill, and a Stool Is Thrown

---

When Dawn came up, rosy-fingered, Telemachus tied his good sandals under his feet and picked up his heavy spear, ready for the town.

"Old friend, I am going to the city, so that my mother can see me. I do not think she will stop her miserable crying and her weeping until she looks at me with her own eyes. And here is what I want you to do. Take this unlucky stranger to the town so that he can beg his dinner there; whoever likes will give him a crust and a cup. I cannot possibly carry every man in the world; I have troubles enough of my own. And if the stranger is annoyed at that, it will be the worse for him. I like to say what I mean."

"My friend, I have no wish to be kept here either. It is better to beg one's dinner in a town than in the fields; whoever likes will give me something. I am not of an age to stay about a farm and do everything a master tells me. Go on. This man will guide me, as you have said, when I have warmed myself at the fire and the sun has got up. These clothes of mine are miserably thin, and the frost before dawn might finish me; and you say the town is a long way off."

So Telemachus went off out of the yard, walking fast, sowing trouble for the suitors.

And when he came to the fine house he set his spear against a tall pillar and stepped in over the stone threshold.

Eurycleia saw him long before anybody else, as she was spreading fleeces over the carved chairs, and she came straight to him crying, and the other women of the house came crowding round and kissed his head and his shoulders in welcome.

And Penelope came out of her room, looking like Artemis (goddess of the hunt) or golden Aphrodite (goddess of love), and threw her arms round her son crying, and kissed his head and both his beautiful eyes.

"You have come, Telemachus. Sweet light. I thought I should never see you again, after you went off to Pylos in a ship, secretly, against my will, to ask after your father. Come, tell me what sight you had of him."

"Mother, do not stir up crying in me, and do not set my heart going in my breast, when I have just got out from under a sheer destruction. Wash, and put clean clothes on, and go upstairs with your women, and promise a full offering to all the gods, in the hope that Zeus may grant us a day of requital. I am going to the assembly, to call in a stranger who came with me from Pylos. I sent him on ahead with my crew, and told Peiraeus to take him home and look after him and honour him until I came."

She did as he said.

And Telemachus went out through the hall with his spear in his hand, and two swift dogs went with him; and Athena poured a supernatural grace over him, so that all the people stared at him as he came. And the lordly suitors gathered round him with fine words on their lips and plans for trouble in their hearts; but he kept clear of the crowd of them and went and sat down with Mentor, and Antiphus, and Halitherses, who had been his father's friends from the beginning.

And Peiraeus came up bringing Theoclymenus through the town, and Telemachus went straight to the stranger.

"Peiraeus, keep the gifts at your house for the moment. If the suitors kill me quietly in the halls and divide up my father's goods among them, I would rather you had them and enjoyed them than any of those men. And if I manage to sow death and doom for them, then bring them to my house and be glad with me."

And he brought the stranger home; and there they bathed and oiled and dressed him and sat him down, and the housekeeper brought bread and a great many good things.

And Penelope sat opposite them by the doorpost of the hall, leaning back in her chair, spinning fine yarn.

"Telemachus, I shall go upstairs and lie down on that bed of mine which has become a bed of sorrows, always wet with my tears since the day Odysseus went off to Troy with the sons of Atreus. And you could not bring yourself, before those insolent men came into this house, to tell me plainly about your father's homecoming, if you have heard anything."

So he told her: how Nestor had received him kindly at Pylos, like a father receiving a son, but knew nothing; how he had been sent on to Sparta, and how Menelaus had told him what the old man of the sea had reported — that Odysseus was alive on an island, weeping, in the house of the nymph Calypso, who was holding him there against his will, and that he had no ship and no crew to carry him over the wide back of the sea.

And her heart was stirred in her breast as she listened.

And then Theoclymenus spoke.

"Honoured wife of Odysseus — this man has no clear knowledge of it. Listen to me instead. I shall prophesy to you exactly, and hide nothing.

"Let Zeus be my witness first of all, and this table of hospitality, and the hearth of Odysseus that I have come to: Odysseus is already here, in his own country. He is sitting still somewhere or moving about, and he is learning about these evil deeds, and he is planting the seed of destruction for every one of the suitors."

"Ah, stranger — I only wish that word may be fulfilled. Then you would very soon know my friendship, and get so many gifts from me that anybody who met you would call you a lucky man."

---

Meanwhile the suitors were amusing themselves in front of the hall with the discus and the throwing spear, on the levelled ground where they always did it, in their usual insolent style; and when it was dinner time and the flocks came in from the fields all round, Medon called them in to eat.

And Odysseus and the good swineherd were setting out from the farm for the town. Eumaeus gave him a staff, and slung a wretched wallet round him, full of holes, on a twisted cord.

And on the way, where the road runs by a spring — a fine built spring with cold water flowing out of the rock, and a grove of water-loving poplars round it in a circle, and an altar of the nymphs above it where every traveller made an offering — they met Melanthius, the son of Dolius, coming down with the pick of the goats for the suitors' dinner, and two herdsmen with him.

And when he saw them he abused them, shockingly.

"There you are: the vile leading the vile. The god always brings like to like. Where are you taking this pig, you miserable swineherd — this disgusting beggar, this spoiler of feasts? He will stand rubbing his shoulders against a great many doorposts, begging for scraps, and no swords and no cauldrons.

"If you gave him to me to look after my farm, and sweep out the stalls, and carry green stuff to the kids, he could drink whey and put some muscle on his thigh. But since he has learnt nothing but bad habits he will not go near an honest job; he would rather go cringing round the neighbourhood begging food for that bottomless belly of his.

"And I will tell you this, and it will happen. If he goes to the house of great Odysseus, a great many footstools thrown from men's hands will fly round his head and be broken on his ribs, as he gets pelted through the house."

And as he passed he kicked Odysseus on the hip, stupidly; and did not knock him off the path. He stood firm.

And Odysseus considered whether to go for him with the staff and take the life out of him, or lift him up bodily and dash his head on the ground. And he held it in and endured it.

But the swineherd looked the man in the face and cursed him, and lifted up his hands and prayed aloud.

"Nymphs of the spring, daughters of Zeus — if ever Odysseus burned you the thigh pieces of lambs and kids, wrapped in rich fat, then grant me this prayer. Let that man come back. Let some god guide him home. He would knock all this display out of you, all these airs you flaunt as you go swaggering about the town, while worthless herdsmen ruin the flocks."

"Listen to the cunning dog talk. One day I shall take him off in a black ship well away from Ithaca and sell him for a good price. And I only wish Apollo would strike Telemachus down in the halls today, or that the suitors would do for him — as surely as the day of homecoming is lost for Odysseus, far away."

And he left them going slowly along, and went on quickly and reached the house and went in and sat down among the suitors, opposite Eurymachus, who liked him best of all of them.

---

The two of them came up and stopped outside; and the sound of the hollow lyre came out to them, because Phemius was striking up a song. And Odysseus took the swineherd's hand.

"Eumaeus, this must be the fine house of Odysseus. Anybody could pick it out of a crowd of buildings. One part leads on to the next; the courtyard is finished off with a wall and a coping, and the double doors are strong. No man could force it. And I can tell there are a great many men at dinner in there, because the smell of roast is coming out, and the lyre is going, which the gods have made the companion of a feast."

"You have picked it out easily enough; you are not a fool in other things either. But let us think what we are going to do. Either you go in first and I stay here; or you stay and I go first. Do not wait about outside, in case somebody sees you and throws something at you or hits you."

"I understand. My mind takes it in. Go in first; I shall stay here. I know all about being hit and having things thrown at me. My heart is patient, because I have had a great deal of trouble on the sea and in war. Let this be added to it.

"But there is no hiding a ravening belly, that cursed thing, which gives men so much trouble. It is for the belly's sake that ships with good benches are fitted out and sent over the barren sea to bring harm to enemies."

---

And while they were talking, a dog that had been lying there raised his head and pricked up his ears.

Argos. Odysseus's own dog, whom he had bred himself and never had the use of, because he went off to holy Ilios first. In the old days the young men used to take him out after wild goats and deer and hares. Now he lay where he had been thrown, on the deep pile of mule dung and cattle dung that was heaped in front of the doors until the servants got round to carting it away to manure the great estate. There lay the dog Argos, full of vermin.

And when he saw Odysseus standing near he wagged his tail and dropped both his ears; and he had not the strength to get any closer to his master.

And Odysseus looked away and wiped a tear off, easily, so that Eumaeus should not see it, and asked him a question at once.

"Eumaeus, it is a strange thing that this dog is lying here on the dung heap. He is well made; though I cannot tell whether he had any speed to go with the looks, or whether he was one of those table dogs that gentlemen keep for show."

"That is the dog of a man who died a long way off. If he were still in body and in action what he was when Odysseus left him and went to Troy, you would be astonished if you saw his speed and his strength. Nothing he ever chased in the deep wood got away from him; he was a wonder at following a scent.

"But now he is in a bad way. His master has died somewhere abroad, and the women do not care and will not look after him. Servants stop doing their work properly the moment their masters are no longer over them. Zeus who sees everything takes away half a man's worth on the day that slavery comes on him."[^1]

And with that he went into the house, straight in among the suitors.

And the fate of black death took hold of Argos, at once, when he had seen Odysseus in the twentieth year.

---

I shall not do anything to that, because it does not need me. I shall only point out two things and then let it alone.

The first is that the dog is the first creature in Ithaca to know him. Not his son, who had to be told; not the nurse, who will find the scar in a few books' time; not his wife, who will make him prove it. A half-dead animal on a manure heap, with no evidence at all, gets it right instantly and has plainly been getting it right, and waiting, for twenty years.

The second is what Odysseus does about it. He looks away and wipes his face where the swineherd cannot see, and then asks a casual question about the animal's breeding. He does not go to him. He cannot: the whole plan depends on it, and it would cost him everything, and he knows it. So the only creature in the country who recognises him gets nothing at all — no word, no hand, no acknowledgement — and dies about ninety seconds later.

---

Telemachus saw the swineherd first, and nodded to him and called him over; and Eumaeus looked round and picked up a stool and sat down opposite, and Telemachus cut off a portion of meat and gave him bread out of the basket.

And Odysseus came in behind him, like an old and pitiful beggar, leaning on a stick, with wretched clothes on his body, and sat down on the ash-wood threshold inside the doorway, leaning against the cypress doorpost.

And Telemachus took a whole loaf and as much meat as his two hands would hold, and told the swineherd to carry it over to him and tell him to go round the suitors and beg from each of them: "Shame is a bad companion for a man in need."

So the beggar took it, and prayed for the young man, and ate.

And when the singer had finished, the suitors made a noise through the hall; and Athena came and stood by Odysseus and told him to go and gather scraps from them, so that he might learn which of them were decent and which were lawless — and even so she was not going to save a single one of them from destruction.

So he went from left to right, holding his hand out, exactly like a man who had been begging all his life. And they pitied him and gave him something, and wondered at him, and asked each other who he was and where he came from.

And Melanthius the goatherd spoke up.

"Listen to me, suitors of the famous queen. I have seen this stranger before; the swineherd brought him here. I do not know for certain who he says he is."

And Antinous turned on Eumaeus.

"You notorious swineherd. What did you bring this fellow into the town for? Have we not enough vagabonds already, disgusting beggars who spoil a feast? Do you think it a small thing that they gather here and eat up your master's living, that you have called this one in as well?"

"Antinous, that is not well said, noble as you are. Who ever goes out and invites a stranger from abroad, unless he is one of the men who work for the public good — a prophet, or a healer of sicknesses, or a carpenter, or a divine singer, who gives delight with his song? Those are the men who are asked for all over the boundless earth. Nobody would invite a beggar to make trouble for himself.

"But you are always harder than any of the suitors on the servants of Odysseus, and hardest of all on me. And I do not care, so long as sensible Penelope is alive in these halls, and godlike Telemachus."

"Be quiet. Do not answer him at any length. Antinous is always harsh, and provoking, with ugly words; and he stirs up the others too."

And then he turned on Antinous himself.

"You take care of me exactly as a father takes care of his son, telling me to drive this stranger out of the hall with a hard word. God forbid. Take something and give it to him; I do not grudge it — I tell you to. And do not worry about my mother in this matter, or any of the servants in the house of Odysseus.

"But that is not the thought in your mind at all. You would very much rather eat a great deal yourself than give any of it away."

"Telemachus, you great orator, you unstoppable temper — what a thing to say. If all the suitors gave him as much as I am going to, this house would keep him off for three whole months."

And he pulled out the footstool from under the table where he had been resting his shining feet, and showed it to him.

But all the others gave, and filled the wallet with bread and meat.

---

And Odysseus was on his way back to the threshold to eat what the Greeks had given him, when he stopped beside Antinous and spoke to him.

"Give, friend. You do not seem to me the worst of the Greeks, but the best; you look like a king. So you ought to give a better piece of bread than the others, and I should sing your praises all over the boundless earth.

"Because I too once lived in a house among men, a rich man in a wealthy house, and I gave often to a wanderer, whoever he was and whatever he came needing. I had servants past counting and everything else that men live well on and are called rich for.

"And Zeus made nothing of it all. He put it in my heart to go off to Egypt with roving pirates, a long voyage, so that I should be destroyed."

And he told him the shape of it: the crews who would not be held, the plundering, the country roused, the men killed and the men led off alive into slavery.

"And they gave me to a friend of theirs to take to Cyprus. And from there I have come here, and I have had nothing but trouble."

And Antinous answered him.

"What god has brought this nuisance here to spoil our dinner? Stand out there in the middle, away from my table, or you will find yourself back in a bitter Egypt and a bitter Cyprus. What a bold and shameless beggar you are. You go up to every man in turn, and they give recklessly, because there is no holding back and no grudging when a man is being generous with somebody else's goods, and every one of them has plenty of it in front of him."

And Odysseus drew back and said:

"Well, well. So your looks are better than your sense. You would not give a suppliant so much as a grain of salt out of your own house, you who are sitting at another man's table and cannot bring yourself to take up a piece of bread and hand it over — with all that in front of you."

That made Antinous angrier still, and he glared at him and spoke.

"Now I do not think you are going to get out of this hall in one piece, since you have started on abuse."

And he took up the footstool and threw it, and hit him on the right shoulder, at the base of the back.

And Odysseus stood there like a rock. The blow from Antinous did not so much as shift him. He shook his head in silence, deep down planning trouble.

And he went back and sat down on the threshold and put his well-filled wallet down and spoke to the suitors.

"Hear me, suitors of the famous queen, while I say what my heart tells me. There is no grief in a man's mind and no pain when he is hit fighting for his own property — for his cattle, or his white sheep. Antinous hit me because of my miserable belly, that cursed thing which brings so much trouble on men.

"But if there are gods anywhere for beggars, and avenging spirits, then let the end of death come for Antinous before ever he is married."

"Sit and eat quietly, stranger, or get out. Otherwise the young men will drag you through the house by the hand or the foot for talking like that, and skin you alive."

And every one of the others was extremely angry, and one of the arrogant young men would say:

"Antinous, you did wrong to hit that unlucky wanderer. You are a doomed man, if there happens to be some god up in the sky. And the gods do put on the shape of strangers from far countries, and take every kind of form, and go about the cities watching to see which men are violent and which are law-abiding."

Antinous took no notice whatever.

And Telemachus nursed a great grief in his heart at his father being struck; but he did not let a single tear fall to the ground. He shook his head in silence, deep down planning trouble.[^2]

---

Penelope heard about it upstairs, and said, among her women: "May Apollo the famous archer strike *you* like that, Antinous." And the housekeeper Eurynome said: "If our prayers were answered, not one of them would ever see the fair-throned Dawn again."

"Nurse, they are all hateful, because they plan wickedness. But Antinous is like black death itself. Some unlucky stranger is going through the house begging alms of the men, because want drives him; and all the others filled his wallet and gave him something. And Antinous threw a footstool and hit him on the right shoulder."

And she sent for the swineherd and told him to bring the stranger to her, so that she could greet him and ask whether he had heard anything of Odysseus.

"My queen, I only wish the suitors would be quiet. He tells such tales as would put a spell on your heart. I had him three nights and kept him three days in my hut — he came to me first, when he ran away from a ship — and he had not got to the end of the story of his troubles.

"It is like watching a singer who has been taught by the gods to sing songs of longing to men, and they cannot stop listening while he goes on. That is how he held me, sitting beside me in the hut. He says he is a friend of Odysseus's family and lives in Crete. And he says he has heard that Odysseus is close by, alive, in the rich country of the Thesprotians, and is bringing a great deal of treasure home."

"Go and call him here, so that he can tell it to my face."

And then Telemachus sneezed loudly, and the whole house rang with it; and Penelope laughed, and her words came out fast.

"Go and call the stranger to me. Did you not hear my son sneeze at everything I said? So may death be brought about for every one of the suitors; not one of them shall get away from death and the fates. And here is another thing; keep it in your mind. If I find that everything he says is true, I shall dress him in a cloak and a tunic, good clothes."[^3]

So the swineherd went and gave the message.

"Eumaeus, I should be glad to tell the whole truth to wise Penelope, because I know all about that man; the two of us have had the same troubles. But I am afraid of that crowd of hard suitors, whose insolence and violence reach the iron sky. Just now, when I was going through the hall doing no harm to anybody, that man hit me and hurt me, and neither Telemachus nor anybody else did anything about it.

"So tell Penelope to wait in the hall until the sun goes down, eager as she is. And then let her ask me about her husband's homecoming, and give me a seat nearer the fire; because the clothes I have on are poor, as you know, since it was you I first appealed to."

And the swineherd went back with that, and Penelope was surprised he had not come. "You are not bringing him, Eumaeus. What does the vagrant mean by that? Is he unusually afraid of somebody? Or is he ashamed of himself in the house? A shy beggar is a bad beggar."

And when Eumaeus explained, she said: "The stranger is no fool. He is guessing at what may very well be true. I do not think there are any other men on earth quite so outrageous, or so given to reckless behaviour."

Then the swineherd went back to Telemachus and told him he was going out to the farm to look after the pigs, and to see to their living and his; and Telemachus told him to have his supper first and come back in the morning and bring good victims for sacrifice.

So he sat down again and ate and drank his fill, and then went back to his pigs, leaving the courtyard and the halls full of feasters.

And they took their pleasure in dancing and singing, because by now the evening had come on.

## NOTES TO BOOK SEVENTEEN

[^1]: **Argos, and what Eumaeus says over him.** The dog's name simply means *swift*, or *bright* — an entirely ordinary name for a hunting dog, of the sort a man would give an animal without thinking about it, which is part of the point. — What deserves as much attention as the dog is the sentence the swineherd attaches to him, because it is one of the bleakest things anybody says in this poem: that Zeus takes away half a man's worth on the day slavery comes upon him. Eumaeus is himself a slave. He is explaining, without self-pity and as a piece of general knowledge, why the women of the house no longer bother to feed an old dog — servants stop doing their work well once nobody is over them — and in the middle of it he states, as a fact about the world, that people like him are worth half what they were. He is at that moment the most honourable person in the book, and he has just cleaned up after the suitors' goats. The poem sets his opinion of himself beside his conduct and says nothing.

[^2]: **The stool, and the suitors' own unease.** Notice who objects. It is not Telemachus, who says nothing and lets no tear fall; and it is not Eumaeus, who has already been slapped down once. It is the other suitors — the anonymous ones, the crowd — who tell Antinous he did wrong, and their reason is worth having in full: *you are a doomed man, if there happens to be a god about; the gods put on the shape of strangers and go round the cities watching who is violent and who is not.* They are exactly right, in every particular, including the disguise. And having said it they go back to their dinner. Nobody gets up. It is a very precise picture of how a bad thing carries on happening in a room full of people who can all see it is wrong: the objection gets made, out loud, by somebody with no stake in it, and then the plates are passed.

[^3]: **The sneeze.** A sneeze at the moment a wish is spoken was a good omen, and a well-attested one — the idea outlived antiquity by a very long way, and is the distant ancestor of blessing somebody who sneezes. What makes this one delightful is the timing and the fact that Penelope *laughs*. She has just finished saying she hopes every suitor in the house will die; her son sneezes somewhere down the hall; and she takes it, quite seriously and also with real amusement, as heaven signing the document. It is the only time she laughs in the poem.
