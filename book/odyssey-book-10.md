# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK TEN
### In Which There Is a Bag of Wind, a Good Deal of Bad Luck with Doors, and a Woman with a Wand

---

We are still in the hall on Scheria, after supper, and the cups are still going round. Twelve ships came out of Troy under his command, and one of them has been emptied in a cave. In this book he loses the other eleven, and I mention it now because the poem is not going to give you time to notice.

---

We came to the island of Aeolia, where Aeolus lives, the son of Hippotas, whom the immortal gods love. It is a floating island, and there is a wall of unbreakable bronze running all the way round it, and the rock goes up sheer.

He has twelve children in his palace: six daughters and six sons in the prime of their youth. And he gave the daughters to the sons as their wives. They eat, always, beside their own father and their good mother, with an endless supply of good things in front of them; and by day the house is full of the smell of cooking and echoes with the sound of the flutes in the courtyard; and at night they sleep beside their honourable wives on blankets and corded bedsteads.[^1]

We came to their city and their fine house, and he gave me a month of hospitality and asked me about everything — about Troy, and the ships of the Greeks, and the way the Greeks came home. And I told him the whole of it, in the right order.

And when I asked him for my road, he did not refuse me anything, and he got the escort ready. He gave me a bag made out of the hide of a nine-year-old ox, flayed whole, and in it he had tied up the courses of all the boisterous winds — because Zeus had made him the steward of the winds, to stop any of them or start any of them, whichever he liked. And he lashed the bag down in the hollow ship with a shining silver cord, so that not the smallest breath could get out of it. And then he sent me the West Wind to blow, to carry the ships and the men along.

He was not going to bring it off. We were destroyed by our own stupidity.

Nine days we sailed, night and day alike. And on the tenth our own fields came up over the water — and we were near enough to see men tending their fires.

And then sweet sleep came over me, because I was exhausted. I had been handling the sheet myself the whole way and never given it to anybody else, so that we would get home faster.

And my companions started talking to each other. They had decided I was carrying gold and silver home from Aeolus, and this is the sort of thing they said, looking at one another:

*It is extraordinary how this man is loved and honoured by everybody, whatever city and country he comes to. He is bringing home a great deal of fine plunder out of Troy, and we — who have made exactly the same journey — are going home with empty hands. And now Aeolus has given him all this out of friendship. Come on, let us have a quick look and see what it is, and how much gold and silver is in that bag.*

And that was the plan that won, and they untied the bag, and all the winds burst out. The storm snatched them up straight away and carried them out to sea, crying, away from their own country.

And I woke up, and turned it over in my faultless heart — whether to go over the side and die in the water, or to endure it in silence and stay among the living. I endured it, and stayed, and covered my head and lay down in the ship. And they were carried back to the island of Aeolia by that evil blast, and my companions were groaning.

---

Everybody remembers that as the crew being greedy and stupid, so it is worth looking at what they actually say to each other. Not *let us steal it*: rather, *we did the same ten years he did, and he is going home rich and we are going home with nothing* — and then, how much is in there? They think it is treasure because a king who entertained them for a month has given their captain a large heavy sealed sack, lashed down with silver cord, and in nine days and nine nights nobody has told them what is in it; and because he has not let another man touch the steering sheet in all that time, which is either magnificent seamanship or a commander who does not trust his crew, and looks identical from the rowing bench.

He never told them. That is the whole of it, and you can hear it in what is missing: nowhere does he say *I explained what Aeolus had given me and warned them*. At the Cicones his defence was precise and he repeated it — I told them to run; they would not listen. He is scrupulous about that formula, and here he cannot use it. What went wrong on that ship went wrong before anybody touched the cord.

---

We went ashore and drew water, and my companions had a meal beside the fast ships. And when we had had food and drink I took a herald and one companion and went up to the famous house of Aeolus, and found him at dinner with his wife and his children. We came in and sat down on the threshold beside the doorposts, and they were astonished.

*How did you get here, Odysseus? What evil spirit has been at you? We sent you off with great care, so that you would reach your own country and your house and anything else you love.*

And I answered them with a sick heart. *My wretched companions ruined me — and a cruel sleep along with them. Put it right, my friends. You have the power.*

That is what I said, speaking gently. And they went dead silent. And their father answered:

*Get off this island. Now. You are the most contemptible thing alive. It is against all right for me to help a man on his way when the blessed gods hate him. Get out. You have come here hated by the immortals.*

And he drove me out of the house, and I was groaning heavily.

We sailed on from there with our hearts full of trouble, and the men's spirits were worn down by the hard rowing, because of our own foolishness; and there was no sign of a passage anywhere. Six days we sailed, night and day alike. And on the seventh we came to the steep citadel of Lamus — to Telepylus of the Laestrygonians, where one herdsman calls to another as he drives his flock in, and the other answers him as he drives his out. There a man who never slept could earn double wages: one lot for herding cattle, and another for pasturing white sheep. The paths of the night and of the day are close together there.[^2]

We came into a splendid harbour, with a sheer cliff running unbroken along both sides of it, and two headlands jutting out opposite each other at the mouth, and the entrance is narrow. And every one of the other captains steered his curved ship inside, and they moored them close together in the hollow of the harbour — because there is never a swell in there, great or small, but a bright calm all round.

I alone kept my black ship outside. I moored her at the very edge, with the cables made fast to a rock.

Then I climbed a rugged lookout and stood there. There was no sign of work — no oxen, no men — nothing but smoke going up off the land. So I sent men to go and find out what sort of people ate the bread of that country: two of them, and a herald as a third.

They went ashore and followed a smooth road, the one the wagons use to bring wood down from the mountains to the town. And in front of the town they met a girl drawing water, the strong daughter of Antiphates the Laestrygonian, who had come down to the clear-running spring. They went up and asked her who was king of these people, and she pointed out her father's high-roofed house at once.

They went in and found his wife inside — as big as a mountain top — and they loathed the sight of her. And she called Antiphates in from the meeting-place, who set about arranging a miserable death for them. He grabbed one of my men on the spot and made his meal of him. The other two bolted and got back to the ships.

And he raised the cry through the town, and the powerful Laestrygonians heard it and came from every direction, thousands of them, and they were not like men at all but like giants. And they threw rocks down at us off the cliffs, boulders a man could barely lift. And an appalling noise went up out of the ships: men dying and timbers being smashed at the same time. And they speared them like fish and carried them home for a disgusting supper.

And while they were killing the men inside the deep harbour, I drew the sharp sword off my thigh and cut through the cables of my dark-prowed ship, and shouted to my own crew to fall on the oars if they wanted to get out of it. And every man of them threw the water up in terror of dying. And my ship got out past the beetling cliffs into the open sea, and was glad of it.

The rest went down there together.

---

Five hundred and forty men, give or take, and eleven ships, gone in about a page. Nobody is named; there is no last stand and no dying speech; and every one of those men had come through ten years at Troy and had somebody at home counting. They die at a pretty anchorage without a fight, and the one man who lives does so because his ship was tied to a rock outside a harbour that every other captain in the fleet had sensibly gone into. Whether that was caution or luck he does not say, and I notice he does not explain it either.

---

We sailed on, glad to be out from under our death, and grieving for the dear companions we had lost. And we came to the island of Aeaea, where Circe lives — the goddess with the braided hair, terrible, who speaks like a human being, the full sister of the deadly-minded Aeetes. Both of them were born of Helios who gives light to men, and of Perse their mother, the daughter of Ocean.

We brought the ship into a harbour that will hold a ship, and some god guided us; and there we lay for two days and two nights, eating our hearts out with weariness and pain. On the third morning I took my spear and my sharp sword and went up quickly from the ship to a place with a view, hoping to see some work of men's hands and hear a voice. And I saw smoke coming up off the wide-wayed earth, through the thick brush and the woods, from the house of Circe.

And I thought about going down to look into it; and what seemed best, thinking it over, was to go back to the fast ship and give my men a meal, and then send some of them to find out. And on the way, when I was close to the ship, one of the gods took pity on me, alone as I was, and sent a great stag with tall antlers right across my path, going down to the river out of his woodland pasture to drink, because the heat of the sun was on him.

I hit him in the middle of the back as he came out, and the bronze spear went straight through, and he dropped in the dust screaming, and the life went out of him. I put my foot on him and pulled the spear out; and then I picked willow shoots and twisted a rope of them, a fathom long, and bound the feet of that great animal together, and slung him round my neck and carried him down to the black ship, leaning on my spear — because there was no possibility of carrying him on one shoulder with one hand; he was a very big beast.

And I threw him down in front of the ship and roused my men, going up to each one and speaking to him gently. *Friends. We are not going down into the house of Hades before our day comes, whatever grief we are in. Come on: while there is still food and drink in the fast ship, let us remember to eat, and not waste away with hunger.*

They did as I said at once. They uncovered their heads on the shore of the barren sea and stared at the stag, because he was a very big beast; and when they had had their fill of looking at him they washed their hands and got a magnificent dinner ready. So all that day until sunset we sat and feasted on meat past counting and sweet wine; and when the sun went down and the darkness came, we lay down to sleep on the shore of the sea.

---

When Dawn came up I called them all together.

*Listen to me, my companions, for all the bad things you have suffered. My friends, we do not know where the darkness is and we do not know where the dawn is; we do not know where the sun that shines on men goes down under the earth, or where he comes up. So let us think quickly whether there is any plan left. I do not believe there is. I climbed a rugged lookout and I saw that this is an island, and the endless sea has it in a crown, and it lies low. And in the middle of it, with my own eyes, I saw smoke, through the thick brush and the woods.*

And their spirits broke in them, because they remembered what Antiphates the Laestrygonian had done, and the violence of the great-hearted man-eating Cyclops. They cried aloud and let the big tears fall. And no good came of their crying.

So I counted off my well-greaved companions into two parties and appointed a leader for each. I led one; godlike Eurylochus led the other. And we shook the lots quickly in a bronze helmet, and the lot of great-hearted Eurylochus jumped out. So he went, and twenty-two companions with him, crying. And they left us behind, crying too.

In the glades they found the house of Circe, built of polished stone in a place with a view all round it. And there were mountain wolves round it, and lions, which she had bewitched herself by giving them evil drugs. They did not attack my men. They stood up on their hind legs and fawned on them, wagging their long tails — the way dogs fawn round their master when he comes back from dinner, because he always brings them something. That is how the wolves with their strong claws and the lions fawned round my men. And my men were terrified, looking at those dreadful animals.

They stood in the gateway of the goddess with the beautiful hair, and they could hear Circe inside, singing in a lovely voice, going up and down in front of a great web, imperishable, the kind of work goddesses do: fine and lovely and full of light.

And Polites spoke first, a leader of men, the dearest and steadiest of all my companions. *Friends. There is somebody inside, going up and down in front of a great web and singing beautifully; the whole floor rings with it. Some goddess, or some woman. Let us call out to her at once.*

So they called, and she came out and opened the shining doors and asked them in. And they all followed her inside, in their innocence. Eurylochus alone stayed behind; he suspected it was a trap.

She brought them in and sat them down on chairs and benches, and mixed them cheese and barley meal and yellow honey into Pramnian wine — and into the food she stirred ruinous drugs, so that they would completely forget their own country. And when she had given it to them and they had drunk it off, she struck them with her wand at once and penned them in the pigsties.

And they had the heads and the voices and the bristles and the bodies of pigs. But their minds were the same as before, exactly.

So there they were, shut in, and crying; and Circe threw down acorns and beech mast and cornel berries for them to eat, the things that pigs lying in the mud always eat.

---

Eurylochus came straight back to the fast black ship to tell us about our companions and the miserable fate they had met. He could not get a single word out, however much he wanted to; his heart was struck through with a terrible grief and his eyes were full of tears. And when we had all questioned him in amazement, at last he told us what had happened.

And I slung my silver-studded sword over my shoulder, the great bronze one, and my bow, and told him to lead me back by the same road. And he took hold of my knees with both hands and begged me. *Do not take me back there against my will. Leave me here. I know you will never come back yourself, and you will not bring any of the others either. Let us get out of here quickly with the men we have got. We may still escape the evil day.*

*Eurylochus, you stay here then. Stay in this place and eat and drink beside the hollow black ship. I am going. A hard necessity is on me.*

So I went up from the ship and the sea. And when I was going through the sacred glades and was about to reach the great house of Circe, Hermes of the golden wand met me on my way, looking like a young man with the first down on his lip, at the age when youth is at its most charming. And he took my hand and said my name.

*Where are you off to now, you unlucky man, going alone over the hills, and you do not know the country? Your companions are penned in Circe's house, in close-fitting sties, like pigs. Have you come to set them free? I do not think you will get away yourself either. You will stay where the others are.*

*But listen. I will get you out of it. Take this good herb and go to Circe's house, and it will keep the evil day off your head. And I will tell you all of Circe's deadly tricks. She will mix you a drink and put drugs into the food. And even so she will not be able to bewitch you, because the good herb I am giving you will not let her.*

*And when Circe strikes you with her long wand, then draw the sharp sword off your thigh and go at her as though you meant to kill her. And she will be terrified, and she will tell you to go to bed with her. And do not refuse the bed of a goddess then — not if you want her to release your companions and look after you. But make her swear the great oath of the blessed gods first: that she will not plot any new harm against you, so that she does not make you a weakling and unman you when she has you stripped.*

And the slayer of Argus (Hermes) gave me the herb, pulling it out of the ground, and showed me what it was like. Its root was black and its flower was as white as milk. *Moly*, the gods call it. It is hard for mortal men to dig, but the gods can do anything.[^3]

Then Hermes went away to high Olympus, and I went on to Circe's house, and my heart was very dark as I went.

---

I stood in her gateway and called; and she came out at once and opened the shining doors and asked me in. And I followed her, sick at heart. She sat me down on a silver-studded chair, beautifully made, with a footstool under my feet, and mixed me a drink in a golden cup, and put a drug into it, with murder in her mind.

And when she had given it to me and I had drunk it off — and it did not bewitch me — she struck me with her wand. *Now go to the sty and lie down with the rest of your friends.*

And I drew the sharp sword off my thigh and went at her as though I meant to kill her. And she gave a great cry and ran in under the blade and got hold of my knees, and her words came out fast.

*Who are you? Where do you come from? Where is your city, and where are your parents? I am amazed that you drank these drugs and were not bewitched. No other man alive has ever withstood this drug once it has gone past the barrier of his teeth. But there is a mind in you that cannot be got round.*

*You must be Odysseus. You must be. The man of many turns, whom the god of the golden wand always told me would come here, on his way back from Troy in a fast black ship. Come — put the sword back in its sheath. Let the two of us go to bed together, so that we may lie down and make love and trust each other.*

*Circe. How can you tell me to be gentle with you? You have turned my companions into pigs in your own house, and now you have got me here and you are plotting something, and you tell me to go into your room and up into your bed — so that when you have me stripped you can make me a weakling and unman me. I am not going up into your bed unless you can bring yourself, goddess, to swear me a great oath that you are not planning any new harm against me.*

And she swore it at once, the way I told her to. And when she had sworn it and got through the whole of the oath, I went up into Circe's beautiful bed.[^4]

---

I said in Book Five that this man does not believe good news and that it would keep him alive; and there it is again, word for word, from a different mouth in a different house. But something else happens here that happens nowhere else in the poem, and I should like it on the record before we go on: he does not solve this one. Every other trap in these four books he gets out of with his own head — the false name, the sheep, the stake in the fire, the rock at the harbour mouth. Here a god walks up to him on a garden path and hands him the antidote, the tactics and the script, and he does exactly as he is told, and it works exactly as he was told it would.

---

While I was in her bed, four handmaids were working in the house — the ones who keep house for her, born of the springs and the groves and the sacred rivers that run down to the sea. One threw beautiful purple covers over the chairs with linen underneath; one drew silver tables up in front of the chairs and set golden baskets on them; the third mixed honey-sweet wine in a silver bowl and set out gold cups; and the fourth brought water and lit a great fire under a big cauldron.

And when the water boiled in the bright bronze she sat me in a bath and washed me, mixing it warm to my liking, and poured it over my head and my shoulders until she had taken the weariness that eats a man's spirit out of my limbs. And when she had washed me and rubbed me with rich oil and put a fine cloak and tunic on me, she led me in and sat me down on a silver-studded chair with a footstool.

And a maid brought water in a gold jug and poured it over a silver basin for me to wash my hands, and drew a polished table up beside me; and the housekeeper brought bread and a great many good things, giving freely of what she had. And she told me to eat.

And I did not want it. I sat there with my mind on other things, and my heart saw trouble coming.

And when Circe saw me sitting like that, not putting my hands out to the food, she came and stood beside me. *Why are you sitting there like a man who has lost his tongue, eating your own heart, and not touching food or drink? Do you suspect some other trick? There is no need. I have already sworn you a powerful oath.*

*Circe — what man with any decency in him could bring himself to taste food or drink before he had got his companions free and seen them with his own eyes? If you really mean it kindly, then let them go.*

So Circe went out through the hall with her wand in her hand, and opened the doors of the sty, and drove them out. They looked like nine-year-old boars. They stood there facing her, and she went through them and rubbed each one with a different drug. And the bristles fell off them — the bristles the ruinous drug had grown on them — and they turned back into men. Younger than they had been before, and much better looking, and taller.

And they knew me, and every man of them took my hand. And a longing to weep came over all of them, and the house rang with it, terribly. And the goddess herself was moved to pity.

---

She told me to go down to the ship and haul her out and stow the gear in the caves, and then come back with the rest of my men. I went, and found them beside the fast ship crying, letting the big tears fall.

And the way farmyard calves come round the herd of cows coming back to the yard when they have had their fill of grass — all of them skipping together in front of them, and the pens no longer hold them, and they run round their mothers lowing without stopping — that is how those men crowded round me, in tears, when their eyes saw me. And it felt to them exactly as though they had got home; as though this were their own country, and the city of rugged Ithaca itself, where they were born and brought up.

And I told them to haul the ship out and stow the gear, and then follow me to Circe's sacred house. And every one of them obeyed at once, except Eurylochus, who tried to hold them all back.

*Where are we going, you poor fools? Why are you so hungry for trouble that you would go down to Circe's house — where she will turn the whole lot of us into pigs or wolves or lions, and we will be made to guard her great house for her? It is exactly what the Cyclops did, when our companions went into his yard, and this reckless Odysseus went with them. They died there too, through his stupidity.*

And when he said that I thought hard about drawing the long sword off my sturdy thigh and taking his head off and rolling it on the ground, close relation by marriage though he was. But my companions held me back with soft words, and said to leave him by the ship to look after it. So they went up from the ship and the sea; and Eurylochus did not stay behind either. He came with us. He was afraid of my terrible anger.

Meanwhile Circe had bathed the rest of my companions in her house and rubbed them with rich oil and put fleecy cloaks and tunics on them; and we found them all having a good dinner in the hall. And when they saw each other face to face and knew each other, they wept and wailed, and the house rang round them.

And the goddess came and stood beside me. *Son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many devices. Do not go on raising this great lamentation any longer. I know myself what you have all suffered on the fish-filled sea, and how unfriendly men have done you harm on land. Come. Eat food and drink wine, until you get the spirit back in your breasts that you had at the beginning, when you first left your own country, rugged Ithaca. As you are now, you are withered and dispirited, always thinking about your hard wandering, and there is never any gladness in your hearts, because you have been through a great deal.*

And we did as she said. And we stayed there day after day, for a full year, feasting on meat past counting and sweet wine.

---

A year.

Set it beside Ogygia. He was with Calypso seven years, which is a great deal longer, and the poem takes enormous trouble to tell us what those years were like: he sat on the rocks every day and cried and looked at the water, the sweetness went out of his life, and he lay beside her unwilling beside the willing one. Here he stays a year in a beautiful house with a goddess who looks after him extremely well, and the poem records not one day of grief. No rocks, no weeping, no looking at the sea. The wine goes round and the meat comes in and the year goes by. And it is not Odysseus who ends it. It is the men — the same men he went unarmed into a witch's house to rescue — who have to take him aside and say, in effect, *sir. Ithaca.* And he goes to her that night and asks to be released, and she releases him at once, and never argues, and never held him at all.

I shall ask the question once and then leave it alone: what exactly was he doing for a year? Not because I think he had stopped wanting to go home — I do not believe he stopped for an hour in twenty years, and the poem has proved it to me more than once — but because wanting to go home and going home are different things, and this is the most honest thing in the book about how people fail to do what they want most. Not by choosing against it. By being comfortable, among friends, for a while, and then for a while longer.

---

When the year was up and the seasons had come round again, my trusty companions called me out. *Strange man. It is time you thought about your own country — if it is really laid down that you are to be saved and get back to your high-roofed house and your own land.*

And my proud heart was persuaded. So all that day until sunset we sat feasting; and when the sun went down and the darkness came, they lay down to sleep through the shadowy hall. But I went up to Circe's beautiful bed and took hold of her knees, and the goddess listened to me.

*Circe. Keep the promise you made me — to send me home. My spirit is set on it now, and so are my men's; they wear my heart away, sitting round me grieving, whenever you happen to be somewhere else.*

*Son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many devices. Do not stay in my house any longer against your will. But there is another journey you have to make first. You must go to the house of Hades and dread Persephone, to consult the spirit of Teiresias of Thebes — the blind prophet, whose mind is still whole. Persephone has granted understanding to him alone, dead as he is. The others go flitting about as shadows.*

And my heart broke when I heard it. I sat there on the bed and cried, and I did not want to go on living and see the light of the sun any more. And when I had had enough of crying and rolling about, I answered her. *Circe — who is going to guide us on that journey? Nobody has ever got to Hades in a black ship.*

And she told me, and I shall set down what she said exactly as she said it, because I did every part of it afterwards, in order, and my life turned on it.

*Do not give a thought to wanting a guide beside the ship. Set up the mast and spread the white sail and sit down; the breath of the North Wind will carry her. And when you have crossed the stream of Ocean, where there is a level shore and the groves of Persephone — tall poplars, and willows that drop their fruit — then beach your ship there and go yourself to the mouldering house of Hades. There the river of fire and the river of wailing run into Acheron, and there is a rock where the two roaring rivers come together.*

*Then go close in, hero, and do exactly as I tell you. Dig a pit, about a forearm's length each way. And pour a drink offering round it to all the dead: first with milk and honey, then with sweet wine, and the third time with water. And sprinkle white barley meal over all of it.*

*And make a long prayer to the strengthless heads of the dead. Promise them that when you get to Ithaca you will sacrifice a barren heifer in your halls, the best you have, and pile the altar with good things; and that to Teiresias by himself you will sacrifice a ram, a black one, all black, the finest in your flocks.*

*And when you have finished praying to the great crowds of the dead, then sacrifice a ram and a black ewe, turning their heads towards the darkness — but turn yourself away, facing towards the streams of the river. And then a great many souls of the dead and gone will come.*

*Then call your companions and tell them to flay the sheep that are lying there killed by the pitiless bronze, and to burn them, and to pray to the gods. And you: draw the sharp sword off your thigh, and sit there, and do not let the strengthless heads of the dead come near the blood until you have asked Teiresias. And the prophet will come to you very soon, and he will tell you your way, and the stages of it, and how you are to get home over the fish-filled sea.*

---

And the Dawn came up on her golden throne while she was still speaking. And she put a cloak and a tunic on me, and dressed herself in a long shining robe, finely woven and lovely, and put a beautiful golden belt round her waist and a veil over her head.

And I went through the house rousing my men, standing over each one and speaking gently. *Do not go on lying there sleeping. Let us go. Lady Circe has told me everything.*

And their proud hearts were persuaded. But not even from there did I get all my men away unhurt.

There was one called Elpenor, the youngest of them, not very brave in a fight and not very sound in the head, who had lain down apart from the others on the roof of Circe's house, wanting the cool air, because he was heavy with wine. And he heard the noise and the stir of his companions moving about, and jumped up suddenly, and forgot to go back down by the long ladder, and fell headlong off the roof; and his neck was broken out of its spine, and his soul went down to the house of Hades.[^5]

And I said to the others as they came: *You think you are going home now. Circe has marked out another road for us — to the house of Hades and dread Persephone, to consult the soul of Teiresias of Thebes.*

And their spirits broke in them when they heard it. They sat down where they were and cried and tore their hair. And no good came of their crying.

And while we were on our way down to the fast ship and the shore of the sea, grieving and letting the big tears fall, Circe went on ahead of us and tied a ram and a black ewe beside the black ship.

She passed us easily. Who could see a god going to and fro, if the god did not wish it?

## NOTES TO BOOK TEN

[^1]: **The house of Aeolus.** Six sons, six daughters, and the daughters married to the brothers — stated in one line, with no comment, as part of a description of a happy and well-run household. It is worth stopping on, because the poem plainly does not intend it as horror. Aeolus is a favourite of the gods, generous, hospitable and helpful, and his home is the most perfectly self-contained thing in the book: an island that floats free of any coast, with a wall of unbreakable bronze round it, where the family eat together every day and there is music in the courtyard and nobody ever has to go outside for anything, including a spouse. Greek myth has other such families and generally attaches no scandal to them at the level of gods and half-divine beings; the rules that governed actual Greek households were not thought to apply upward. What the detail is *for*, I think, is the enclosure. This is a household that needs nothing from the world — which makes what happens next exact. The one thing Aeolus gives away is a sealed bag, and when it is opened and the man comes back, the door shuts and does not open again. Perfectly self-sufficient people can afford to be perfectly unforgiving.

[^2]: **"Where the paths of night and day are close together."** A herdsman driving his flock in at dusk calls out and is answered by one driving out at dawn, and a man who could do without sleep might draw two wages a day. That is a description of a place where the nights are so short they barely happen — high-latitude summer, where the light never entirely goes. Nobody knows what to do with it. It sits in the middle of a Mediterranean poem, unexplained, and it has been argued over for two thousand years: a garbled traveller's report carried south along the amber routes; a memory of a Norwegian summer that reached Greece through four sets of hands; a coincidence; an invention that happens to describe something real. I have no answer and neither has anybody else. But it is worth knowing that the strangest line in the strangest book of this poem is not about a monster. It is about the light.

[^3]: **Moly.** Black root, milk-white flower, hard for mortals to dig — and the poem tells you that *moly* is what the gods call it, which is one of a small handful of places in Homer where we are told that the immortals have their own names for things and that this is not the human one. People have been trying to identify the plant for a very long time, and the most persistent modern candidate is the snowdrop, which grows wild in Greece, has a dark bulb and a white flower, and contains a compound that is a genuine antidote to the class of poisons that produce delirium and hallucination — the sort of thing you might get from thorn-apple or henbane, both of which grow in the region and both of which have been used to make people compliant. On that reading Circe's drink is a real drug and Hermes' herb a real counter to it, and somebody in the Bronze Age Aegean knew it. It is a lovely theory and I would not bet the house on it. What is certain is the shape of the thing: the goddess who knows every drug is defeated by a better one, handed over by a god who does not stay to watch.

[^4]: **What Circe threatens.** Hermes' warning is specific, and the Greek is blunter than English likes to be: when she has him naked and unarmed she will make him worthless and not a man. It carries both senses at once, cowardice and unmanning, and the poem does not separate them, because in this world they were not separate. What is being described is the standard fear about a woman with power over a man in a bedroom, and the standard defence is a binding oath sworn before the act. — Note what the scene does structurally. He arrives with a sword out, and the encounter is settled on his terms: she swears, he goes to bed with her, and afterwards she is his ally for a year and gives him the sailing directions that save his life. It is a seduction converted into a treaty. Whether one should read her as beaten, or as having got precisely what she wanted from the start, the poem leaves entirely open — and I notice that she was expecting him. Hermes had told her, years before, that he would come.

[^5]: **Elpenor.** The youngest man aboard, no good in a fight, not clever, drunk, asleep on a flat roof for the cool air; he hears everyone moving, jumps up, forgets there is a ladder, and breaks his neck. It is the least heroic death in the poem and quite possibly in all Greek literature, and it takes four lines. Every other man who dies in these four books is eaten by something, or drowned, or crushed by a rock thrown by a giant; this one falls off a roof at a party. I mention it because it would be easy to read as a throwaway, and it is not: he is going to matter, very shortly, and the poem's willingness to spend real feeling on a man it has just told you was neither brave nor bright is one of the more decent things about it.
