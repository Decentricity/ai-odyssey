# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

---

## BOOK TWENTY-ONE
### In Which a Bow Comes Down Off Its Peg

---

Then Athena put it into the mind of Penelope to set the bow and the grey iron before the suitors in the halls of Odysseus, as a contest for them and the beginning of their killing.

She went up the high staircase of her house and took the bent key in her strong hand, a good one, made of bronze, with an ivory handle on it; and she went with her women to the innermost store-room, where her husband's treasures lay, the bronze and the gold and the worked iron. And there lay the back-bent bow and the quiver for the arrows, with a great many grievous shafts in it, which a friend had given him when they met once in Lacedaemon (Sparta): Iphitus, the son of Eurytus, a man like the immortals.

The two of them had met in Messene, in the house of Ortilochus. Odysseus had come there over a debt the whole people owed him, because Messenian men had carried off three hundred sheep from Ithaca in their benched ships, and the shepherds with them; and he had come the long journey after them while he was still a boy, sent by his father and the other elders. And Iphitus had come looking for twelve brood mares he had lost, with hardy mules still at the teat. Those mares were the death of him in the end, because when he came to the house of the son of Zeus — Heracles, the man of the great labours — Heracles killed him, guest though he was under his roof. He had no fear of the gods' anger in him and no respect for the table he had set in front of the man; he killed him anyway, and kept the strong-hoofed mares in his own stables.

It was while he was looking for those that Iphitus met Odysseus and gave him the bow, which great Eurytus had carried in his day and left to his son when he died. And Odysseus gave him a sharp sword and a heavy spear, the beginning of a friendship between two houses; but they never came to know each other at the table, because the son of Zeus killed Iphitus first.

And Odysseus never took that bow with him in the black ships when he went to the war. It lay at home in his halls as a keepsake of a friend, and he carried it only in his own country.[^1]

---

Penelope came to the room and stepped up onto the oak threshold, which a carpenter had planed skilfully long ago and made true to the line, and had set the doorposts on it and hung the shining doors. She slipped the thong off the handle and pushed the key in and shot the bolts back, aiming carefully.

And as a bull bellows when he is grazing in a meadow, so those beautiful doors bellowed when the key struck them; and they flew open in front of her.

She stepped up onto the high floor where the chests stood with the sweet-smelling clothes in them, and reached up and took the bow down off its peg, in the bright case that was round it. And she sat down there with the case on her knees and cried aloud, and took her husband's bow out of it.

And when she had had her fill of weeping she went off to the hall, to the lordly suitors, carrying the back-bent bow and the quiver, with a great many grievous shafts in it; and her women carried a chest behind her with a quantity of iron and bronze in it, her husband's prizes.

She came among the suitors and stood by the doorpost of the great hall, holding her shining veil in front of her face, with a faithful woman standing on either side of her; and she spoke to them at once.

"Listen to me, my lords. You have fastened on this house to eat and drink in it, without a break, while its master has been a long time gone; and you have not been able to find any other pretext for it except that you want to marry me.

"So here is your prize, gentlemen, set out in front of you. I shall put down the great bow of godlike Odysseus. And whichever man most easily strings it in his hands and shoots an arrow clean through all twelve axes — with him I shall go, and leave this house of my marriage, so beautiful, and so full of everything a life needs, which I think I shall remember even in my dreams."

And she told Eumaeus to set the bow and the grey iron down in front of them. He took them from her, crying, and set them down; and the cowherd cried too when he saw his master's bow.

And Antinous rounded on the pair of them. "You stupid peasants, who think no further than today. You wretched creatures, what are you snivelling for? You are upsetting the lady, whose heart is already lying low with grief because she has lost her husband. Sit down and eat in silence, or go outside and cry there, and leave the bow here for us — an unambiguous sort of contest, because I do not think this polished bow will be strung at all easily. There is no man among the whole company here of the sort Odysseus was. I saw him myself and I remember him, though I was only a child."

So he said; and the heart in his breast was hoping he would string it himself and shoot through the iron. And in fact he was going to be the first man to taste an arrow from the hands of the great Odysseus, whom at that moment he was dishonouring where he sat, and urging all his friends to do the same.

---

And Telemachus said: "Zeus must have made me stupid. Here is my dear mother, sensible as she is, saying she will go off with another man and leave this house — and I laugh, and I am glad, with my wits gone.

"So come along, gentlemen, since here is your prize set in front of you: a woman such as there is not now in all the Greek country, not in sacred Pylos, not in Argos or Mycenae, not in Ithaca itself nor on the dark mainland. You know that perfectly well yourselves. What need is there for me to praise my own mother?

"Come, no putting it off with excuses, and no turning away from the drawing of the bow any longer; let us see how it goes. And I should like a try at the bow myself. If I string it and shoot an arrow through the iron, then my honoured mother would not be leaving this house to go off with another man and grieve me, and leave me behind — now that I am old enough to take up my father's prizes."

And he sprang up and threw off his purple cloak and unslung his sharp sword.

First he set up the axes. He dug one long trench for the whole row of them, and lined them all up to a string, and stamped the earth down round them. And everybody was astonished at how neatly he set them out, though he had never seen it done in his life.

Then he went and stood on the threshold and tried the bow.

Three times he made it quiver, straining to draw it, and three times he let the effort go, though in his heart he was hoping to string it and shoot through the iron. And he would have got it strung at the fourth attempt, hauling at it with all his strength — but Odysseus shook his head and stopped him, keen as he was.

"Well," he said, "I am going to be a weakling and a coward all my life, or else I am simply too young, and cannot trust my hands yet to keep off a man who starts a quarrel. But come along, you who are stronger than I am, and try the bow, and let us settle this contest."

And he put the bow down on the ground, leaning it against the smooth-jointed doors, and stood the swift arrow against the fine curve of the bow, and went and sat down again on the chair he had got up from.

---

I should stop here for a moment, because a modern reader is very likely to be picturing something quite different from what is happening in that hall, and the picture matters a good deal over the next hour.

Nobody is entirely certain what shooting through twelve axes means, and I would not trust anyone who tells you they are. Homer's audience knew, and never needed telling, so the poem describes the trench and the lining-up and the stamping down and never once explains what the target actually is; and we have been guessing ever since. The likeliest arrangement, and the one most people who have handled the tools have settled on, is this. The heads of these axes were not the shape of a modern woodsman's axe. They had a socket or a ring at the base where the handle went in, and if you take a dozen of them and bed the handles in a trench in the floor with the heads standing up in a row, all lined true and all the same height, you have a line of twelve rings hanging in the air one behind another, and a very narrow tunnel of daylight running through them. The shot is to put an arrow down that tunnel from the far end of a long hall, from a sitting position, without touching any of the twelve.

Which tells you two things at once. The first is that this is not a test of strength at all; it is a test of an almost absurd precision, of a kind that would be a fine trick at any distance and is barely credible at that one. And the second is that the man who used to do it for entertainment in this room, standing a long way off and putting the arrow through, is the man now sitting by the door in rags — and that his son, who has never once seen it done, sets the axes up so exactly that a hall full of hostile men stops talking to watch him do it.

---

Then Antinous said: "Get up in turn, all of you, from left to right, starting from where the wine is poured."

And Leiodes stood up first, the son of Oenops, who was their soothsayer and always sat by the fine mixing bowl at the far end of the hall. He was the only one of them to whom their outrages were hateful, and he was indignant with the whole company. He took the bow and the swift arrow and went and stood on the threshold and tried it; and he could not string it, because he wore his hands out with pulling first — they were soft hands, and unused to work.

"Friends, I cannot string it. Let somebody else take it. This bow is going to rob a great many of our leading men of their spirit and their lives — because it is far better to be dead than to go on living and fail to get the thing we gather here for every single day, waiting and hoping. There is many a man here now hoping and wanting to marry Penelope. But when he has tried this bow and seen for himself, then let him go and court some other well-dressed Greek woman with gifts; and Penelope can marry whichever man gives most, and comes as the one fate has appointed."

And he set the bow aside, leaning it against the doors.[^2]

Antinous rounded on him too. "Leiodes, what a thing to let past the barrier of your teeth. A grim word, and an ugly one, and it makes me angry to hear it — that this bow is going to rob our best men of their lives, just because you cannot string it. Your honoured mother did not bear you to be a drawer of bows and a shooter of arrows. There are other lordly gentlemen here who will string it soon enough."

And he called to Melanthius the goatherd. "Get a fire going in the hall and put a big stool beside it with a fleece on it, and bring out a great cake of fat from the store, so that we young men can warm the bow and rub the grease into it and try it, and finish this contest."

So Melanthius lit the fire and put the stool there with the fleece over it and brought a great cake of fat out of the store; and the young men warmed the bow and tried it.

And they could not string it. They were a very long way short of the strength for it. But Antinous still held back, and so did godlike Eurymachus, the leaders of the suitors, who were far the best of them.

---

Here, if you will bear with me, is the other thing worth knowing, because without it the whole afternoon looks like a simple trial of muscle, and it is not.

A bow of this kind was not a bent stick. It was a composite: a wooden core with strips of animal horn glued along the belly and layers of sinew laid along the back, the whole thing bound and seasoned and left to cure for a very long time — a year or two, in the making — and the finished weapon curves the wrong way when it is unstrung, so that stringing it means bending it backwards against itself. Such a bow is enormously powerful for its size, and it is also fussy. It hates damp, which is why this one has lived in a locked room in a case; the glue goes soft with heat, which is why warming it at the fire and rubbing it with fat is a real technique and not a piece of superstition; and it can be ruined by worm in the horn while nobody is using it, which is exactly what Odysseus will be checking for in a few pages when he turns it over and over in his hands and the suitors laugh at him for a connoisseur.

And stringing one is a knack. It is not done by hauling on both ends. It is done by bracing the bow against the body in a particular way, with a particular leg through it or a particular grip, so that the strength you have goes exactly where it is needed; and a man who has never been shown will fail at it however strong he is, and will look foolish, and will not know why. That is what a hall full of young athletes is discovering. They are not weaklings. They are amateurs with somebody else's specialist equipment, and the one man in the room who has been shown is sitting by the door with a bag full of holes, watching them heat his bow.

---

While they were at it, the cowherd and the swineherd went out of the house together; and Odysseus went out after them.

When they were past the door and outside the courtyard he spoke to them gently.

"Cowherd — and you, swineherd — shall I say a certain thing, or keep it to myself? My heart tells me to say it. What sort of men would you be for defending Odysseus, if he were to come from somewhere, quite suddenly, and some god were to bring him? Would you stand with the suitors, or with Odysseus? Say what your heart and your spirit tell you."

"Father Zeus," said Philoetius, "I only wish you would bring that about — that the man might come, and some god bring him home. Then you would find out what my strength is, and how these hands of mine obey me."

And Eumaeus prayed to all the gods in the same way, that clever Odysseus might come back to his own house.

And when he had seen their minds were sound and true, he answered them.

"Here he is. I am the man. I have come home in the twentieth year to my own country, after suffering a great deal. And I know that of all my servants you two alone are glad of my coming; I have not heard one of the others praying that I might come home again.

"So I shall tell you the truth about what is going to happen. If the god beats the lordly suitors down under me, I shall bring the two of you wives, and give you property and houses built close to my own; and after that you shall be companions to me and brothers to Telemachus.

"Come — I shall show you a plain sign as well, so that you know me for certain and are sure in your hearts."

And he pulled the rags aside from the great scar.

And when the two of them had looked it over and taken in every part of it, they burst into tears and threw their arms round Odysseus and kissed his head and his shoulders in welcome; and he kissed their heads and their hands in the same way. And the light of the sun would have gone down on their weeping, if Odysseus himself had not stopped them.

"That is enough crying and wailing, or somebody may come out of the hall and see us and go in and tell them. Go back in one at a time, not together. I shall go first and you come after.

"And let this be the signal. All the rest of them, all the lordly suitors, will refuse to let the bow and the quiver be given to me. But you, good Eumaeus, carry the bow through the hall and put it into my hands; and then tell the women to bar the close-fitting doors of their quarters, and if any of them hears the sound of groaning or of men fighting inside our walls, she is not to come running out but to stay where she is, in silence, at her work.

"And you, good Philoetius (the cowherd), I put in charge of the courtyard gate. Bolt it with the bar, and get the cord round it quickly."

And he went in and sat down again on the chair he had got up from; and the two servants of godlike Odysseus came in afterwards.

---

Eurymachus by now was handling the bow, warming it this way and that in the light of the fire. And even so he could not string it, and his proud heart groaned aloud.

"This is a bad business, and I am sorry for myself and for all of us. I do not grieve so much about the marriage, distressed as I am; there are plenty of other Greek women, some here in sea-girt Ithaca and some in the other towns. What I mind is that we should turn out so far short of godlike Odysseus in strength that we cannot even string his bow. That is a shame men will hear about for a long time to come."

"Eurymachus," said Antinous, "it will not be like that, and you know it yourself. Today is a holy feast day of the god throughout the country. Who would be bending a bow on a day like this? Put it down quietly. As for the axes, we may as well leave them standing where they are; I do not think anybody is going to walk into the hall of Odysseus and carry them off.

"Come, let the wine-steward pour a few drops into the cups so that we can make our libations and put the curved bow away. And in the morning tell Melanthius to bring in goats, the very best in all his herds, so that we can lay thigh pieces before Apollo, the archer god, and then try the bow and finish the contest."[^3]

That pleased them all; and the heralds poured water over their hands, and the young men filled the mixing bowls to the brim and served the wine round, pouring the first drops into each cup for the libation.

---

And when they had poured and drunk as much as they wanted, Odysseus spoke, with his own purposes in his mind.

"Listen to me, suitors of the glorious queen, and let me say what my heart tells me. I appeal to Eurymachus in particular, and to godlike Antinous, since he has just spoken very sensibly: leave the bow for now and let the gods decide it; in the morning the god will give the strength to whichever man he chooses.

"But hand me the polished bow, so that I can test my hands and my strength among you — and see whether I still have the force I once had in my supple limbs, or whether the wandering and the neglect have worn it out of me."

That made all of them furious, because they were afraid he might string it.

And Antinous turned on him.

"You wretched stranger, you have not the smallest scrap of sense left. Is it not enough for you to sit and eat at your ease among us gentlemen, going short of nothing, and listen to our talk and our conversation, when no other stranger and no other beggar gets to hear us?

"It is the wine that has your wits, honey-sweet wine, which does harm to any man who takes it in great gulps and drinks past what is decent. Wine was what maddened the famous centaur Eurytion, in the hall of great-hearted Peirithous, when he came among the Lapiths; and when the wine had turned his wits, in his madness he did wicked things in that house. Grief took hold of the heroes, and they leapt up and dragged him out through the porch and cut off his ears and his nose with the pitiless bronze; and off he went with his mind still deranged, carrying his own ruin about with him in his witless heart. And out of that came the long feud between men and centaurs — but he brought the first evil on himself, being heavy with wine.

"And I promise you great trouble in the same way, if you string this bow. You will get no kindness from anybody in this country. We shall put you straight on a black ship and send you to king Echetus, who mutilates everybody, and you will not get away from him alive. So drink your wine in peace, and do not set yourself against men who are younger than you."[^4]

And Penelope answered him.

"Antinous, it is neither decent nor just to cheat the guests of Telemachus, whoever comes to this house.

"Do you really suppose that if this stranger strings the great bow of Odysseus, trusting to his hands and his strength, he will take me home and make me his wife? I do not believe he has any such hope in his own breast. So let none of you sit here eating his dinner with a heavy heart on that account. It is not in the least a reasonable thing to think."

And Eurymachus said: "Daughter of Icarius, sensible Penelope, we do not think this man is going to marry you; that would be an absurdity. What we mind is the talk of men and women — that some day one of the baser Greeks might say: *Truly these are much meaner men courting the wife of a blameless man, and they cannot even string his polished bow; and then some beggar came wandering in and strung it easily and shot through the iron.* That is what they will say, and it will be thrown at us."

"Eurymachus, there is no possible way that men can have a good name in this country who consume and dishonour the house of a prince. Why do you count this particular thing a disgrace?

"This stranger is a big man and well built, and he says he comes of a good father. Give him the polished bow, then, and let us see.

"And I shall tell you plainly, and it will be brought about: if he strings it, and Apollo grants him the glory of it, I shall dress him in a cloak and a tunic, good clothes, and give him a sharp javelin to keep off dogs and men, and a two-edged sword, and sandals for his feet; and I shall send him wherever his heart wants to go."

And Telemachus said:

"Mother, as far as the bow goes, no Greek has a better right than I have to give it or refuse it to whoever I like — not one of the men who are lords in rocky Ithaca or in the islands over towards Elis. Not one of them will thwart me against my will, not even if I choose to give this bow to the stranger outright, to carry away with him.

"So go to your own part of the house and see to your own work, the loom and the distaff, and tell your women to get on with theirs. The bow is men's business, and mine most of all; because the authority in this house is mine."

And she went back up in astonishment, taking her son's wise saying to heart. And she went up to her upper room with her women and cried for Odysseus, her dear husband, until Athena cast a sweet sleep on her eyelids.

---

Then the good swineherd picked up the curved bow and started carrying it; and all the suitors began shouting at him through the hall, and one of the arrogant young men would say:

"Where are you carrying that curved bow, you miserable swineherd, you crazy creature? The swift dogs you reared yourself will eat you out there among your pigs, away from everybody, if Apollo and the other immortals are kind to us."

And he was frightened, and put the bow down where he stood, because a great many of them were shouting at him in the hall.

And Telemachus shouted at him too, from the other side, and threatened him.

"Old man, take that bow forward. You will find out soon enough that you cannot obey everybody. Take care I do not chase you back to the fields with a shower of stones, young as I am and strong as you are. I only wish I were as much stronger than all the suitors in this hall; I would very soon send a good few of them out of this house in a miserable state, because they plan wicked things."

And at that they all laughed merrily at him, and stopped being angry with Telemachus. And the swineherd carried the bow through the hall and put it into the hands of clever Odysseus.

Then he called Eurycleia out and said to her: "Telemachus tells you, sensible Eurycleia, to bar the close-fitting doors of the hall; and if any of the women hears the sound of groaning or of men fighting inside our walls, she is not to come out but to stay where she is, in silence, at her work."

So she said nothing, and barred the doors of the great hall.

And Philoetius went silently out of the house and barred the gates of the fenced courtyard. There was a ship's cable of papyrus fibre lying under the colonnade, and he used that to lash the gates fast; and then he came in and sat down again on the chair he had got up from, watching Odysseus.

---

And Odysseus was already handling the bow, turning it round and round and trying it here and there, in case worms had eaten into the horn while its owner was away.

And one man would glance at his neighbour and say: "Here is a real connoisseur, an expert on bows. I dare say he has bows like it lying at home himself, or is planning to make one, the way he turns it over and over in his hands — this vagabond, so well practised in trouble."

And another of the arrogant young men would say: "I hope he gets as much profit out of his life as he is ever going to get out of stringing that bow."

That is what the suitors said.

And Odysseus, when he had lifted the great bow and looked it over on every side —

as a man skilled at the lyre and at singing stretches a string easily round a new peg, fastening the twisted sheep-gut at either end —

just so, without any effort at all, did Odysseus string the great bow.

And he took it in his right hand and tried the string; and it sang sweetly under his touch, with a note like a swallow's.

A great grief came over the suitors, and the colour changed in all their faces. And Zeus thundered loud, showing his signs.

And the much-enduring godlike Odysseus was glad, because the son of Cronos had sent him an omen.

He picked up a swift arrow that was lying on the table beside him, out of the quiver; the rest were still inside the hollow quiver, the ones the Greeks were shortly going to taste. And he laid it on the bridge of the bow and drew the string and the notched arrow back — sitting exactly where he was, in his chair — and let the shaft go, aiming true.

And he did not miss a single one of the axes, from the first handle onwards. The arrow with the bronze head went clean through the whole line and out at the far end.

And he said to Telemachus:

"Telemachus, the stranger sitting in your halls does not shame you. I did not miss the target, and I was not long about stringing the bow. My strength is still where it was — not what the suitors say when they insult me.

"But now it is time to get supper ready for these gentlemen, while there is still light. And after that we must make other sport, with the lyre and with singing; because those are the things that go along with a feast."

And he signalled with his eyebrows. And Telemachus, the dear son of godlike Odysseus, slung his sharp sword about him and put his hand round his spear; and there he stood beside his father's chair, armed in gleaming bronze.

## NOTES TO BOOK TWENTY-ONE

[^1]: **The bow, and the man who gave it.** The poem stops for thirty lines to tell us where this weapon came from, which is a long time in a book that is moving as fast as this one, and the detail is doing several things. First it establishes that the bow is a guest-gift — the formal exchange between two men that made their houses friends for good, which we have been watching people honour and violate since Book One — and that Odysseus gave a sword and a spear in return. Then it tells us that the friendship never had time to grow, because Heracles murdered Iphitus in his own house, over some horses, having fed him first. That is the worst crime available in this world, and it is dropped in without comment, about the most famous hero in Greek legend, in a single sentence. — And last, the detail that decides everything: Odysseus did not take this bow to Troy. He left it at home as a keepsake of a dead friend and used it only on his own land. Which is why it is in a locked room upstairs rather than at the bottom of the sea with his ships, and why there is exactly one man alive who has ever strung it.

[^2]: **Leiodes.** The suitors' own diviner, who sat apart at the mixing bowl and disliked what the others did — the nearest thing to a decent man among them after Amphinomus, and the first to try the bow and fail. What he says as he sets it down is worth marking: that this bow will cost a great many of them their lives. He means it as a figure of speech, in the way a man might say a thing is the death of him; he is a professional reader of signs, and he does not notice that he has just made a true prophecy about his own house. He will ask Odysseus for his life in Book Twenty-Two, on the grounds that he never joined in, and it will not save him.

[^3]: **The excuse about the feast day.** Antinous produces a reason for adjourning that is entirely respectable and, as far as it goes, perfectly true: this is a public holy day of Apollo, the archer god, and bending a bow on it might well look like poor form. Everyone accepts it with relief. What none of them notice is what the reader was told in the last book — that it being the archer god's own festival is not an argument against the contest at all, but the single most ominous fact in the building. They are postponing an archery competition out of respect for the god of archery, on the day he might be expected to be paying attention, in a hall where the man who owns the bow is sitting by the door.

[^4]: **The centaur at the wedding.** Antinous's warning story would have needed no explaining to his listeners. The Lapiths were a people of Thessaly, and at the wedding feast of their king Peirithous the centaurs among the guests got drunk and attempted to carry off the women, and a general slaughter followed; it was one of the best-known stories in Greece and was carved on temples all over the country as an image of civilisation against savagery. — The joke, which Antinous is much too pleased with himself to see, is the exact fit of the parallel and which end of it he is standing at. He is warning a stranger about a guest who abused his host's hospitality and got mutilated for it, at a feast, in a hall he has been eating out of for three years without paying, while threatening to have the stranger shipped off to a torturer. Every particular of the story he chooses is about to be true of him, and he has the details the wrong way round.

*Book Twenty-Two: In Which the Doors Are Shut.*
