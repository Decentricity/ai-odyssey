# THE ODYSSEY
### Retold in English, with Explanations

## BOOK TWENTY-FOUR
### In Which the Dead Compare Notes, an Old Man Is Found Digging in His Orchard, and the Last Fight Is Called Off

Hermes (the messenger of the gods, and — an office we have not yet seen him at — the guide of dead souls) was calling the souls of the suitors out. He held in his hand the beautiful golden wand with which he seals men's eyes in sleep or wakes them as he pleases, and with it he stirred them and led them, and they followed, squeaking. As bats in the depths of a wonderful cave flit squeaking when one drops off the chain of them where they hang together from the rock, so the souls went squeaking after him, and Hermes the kindly led them down the dank ways: past the streams of Ocean and the White Rock, past the gates of the Sun and the country of dreams, until they came quickly to the meadow of asphodel, where the souls live, the images of worn-out men.[^1]

There they found the soul of Achilles, and with him Patroclus, and blameless Antilochus, and Ajax, the finest of all the Greeks in beauty and stature after the peerless son of Peleus. These were gathered round Achilles; and now there came near them, grieving, the soul of Agamemnon (the king who led the Greeks to Troy, and was murdered on the day he came home), with all those around him who died beside him in Aegisthus's house.

The soul of Achilles spoke first: "Son of Atreus (Agamemnon), we used to say that of all heroes you were dearest, your whole life long, to Zeus who delights in thunder, because you were lord over many mighty men in the Trojans' country, where we Greeks suffered. And yet the deadly doom that no man born escapes was to come to you, too, and early. How much better if you had met your fate at Troy, in the fullness of the honour you commanded there; then all the Greeks together would have made you a tomb, and you would have won great glory for your son as well. Instead it was appointed that you should die the most pitiable death there is."

And the soul of Agamemnon answered: "Fortunate son of Peleus, godlike Achilles, who died at Troy, far from home — and around you the best sons of the Trojans and the Greeks died fighting over your body, while you lay in the swirl of dust, great in your greatness, your horsemanship forgotten. All day we fought, and would never have stopped, had Zeus not stopped it with a storm. We carried you out of the battle to the ships and washed your fine body with warm water and oil, and the men shed hot tears over you and cut their hair. And your mother came up out of the sea with the deathless girls of the water, when she heard, and a huge crying went out over the deep, and trembling took all the Greeks — they would have bolted for the hollow ships, if one man had not held them, a man wise in the old things: Nestor, whose counsel had proved best before. 'Hold, Greeks,' he said; 'do not run, young men; it is his mother, coming out of the sea with the immortal sea-nymphs, to meet her dead son.' So the great-hearted Greeks stood their ground, and the daughters of the old man of the sea stood round you weeping, and dressed you in the clothing of the gods; and the Muses, all nine, sang the dirge over you, answering one another in their lovely voices, and you would not have seen one Greek dry-eyed, the clear song stirred them so. Seventeen days and seventeen nights we mourned you, deathless gods and mortal men together, and on the eighteenth we gave you to the fire, and slaughtered fat sheep and shambling cattle around you. You burned in the gods' own clothing, in quantities of oil and sweet honey, and heroes in armour, on foot and horsed, moved in procession round your burning pyre, and the sound of it was very great. And when the flame of Hephaestus (the smith of the gods, whose element the fire is) had finished you, at dawn we gathered your white bones, Achilles, into unmixed wine and oil. Your mother had given a golden two-handled jar; she said it was the gift of Dionysus (the god of wine), and the work of famous Hephaestus. In that your white bones lie, shining Achilles, mingled with the bones of dead Patroclus, and apart, but near, those of Antilochus, whom you honoured beyond all your companions after Patroclus died. And over them all we heaped a huge and perfect mound, we the sacred army of Greek spearmen, on a jutting headland above the wide Hellespont, so that it might be seen far out to sea by men now living and by men to come. Your mother begged splendid prizes from the gods and set them in the middle of the assembly, for the best of the Greeks to contend for; and I have stood at the funeral games of many heroes, but never saw my heart so full of wonder as at what the gods gave for your sake. So not even in dying did you lose your name; your fame will be great among all mankind forever, Achilles. But what pleasure was there for me in winding up the war? For at my homecoming Zeus was planning my dismal destruction, at the hands of Aegisthus and my accursed wife."

While they talked in this way, the guide, the killer of Argus, came near, bringing down the souls of the suitors Odysseus had slain; and the two kings, wondering, went straight toward them. And the soul of Agamemnon knew one of them: Amphimedon, the dear son of Melaneus, who had been his host once on Ithaca — for even in the grey place a man remembers whose roof he slept under. "Amphimedon," he said, "what has happened to you, that you all come down under the dark earth together, picked men, and all of an age? A man choosing through a city would not choose better. Did Poseidon raise a hard wind against your ships and sink you? Or did enemies cut you down on some coast, where you were driving off their cattle and their flocks of sheep, or fighting for a town and its women?"

And the soul of Amphimedon answered him, and told the whole story from the other end of it: the wooing of the wife who would neither refuse a marriage she hated nor conclude one, and the great web on the loom — how she wove the huge cloth by day and unwove it by torchlight for three years, and how in the fourth a woman who knew the truth told them, and they caught her unravelling the shining web, and she finished it against her will. "And then," he said, "some malicious god brought Odysseus from wherever he was — to the edge of the estate first, where the swineherd lived, and there his own son joined him, back from sandy Pylos in his black ship, and the two of them plotted our evil deaths and came into the famous town, Odysseus last, and late, in wretched rags, like an old ruined beggar, leaning on a stick, so that not one of us could know him when he appeared, not even the older men. We threw things at him and gave him hard words, and he endured it in his own halls with a patient heart. But when the will of Zeus roused him, he and his son took the beautiful armour out of the hall and locked it in the store-room, and then in his great cunning he told his wife to set before the suitors the bow and the grey iron — the contest that was the beginning of our slaughter.[^2] Not one of us could string that mighty bow; we fell far short of it. And when it came round to his hands, we all shouted that he should not be given it, whatever he said, and only Telemachus urged him on and told him to take it. And he took it in his hand, the much-enduring man, and strung it easily, and shot through the iron, and went and stood on the threshold and poured out the swift arrows, glancing terribly around him, and shot the king Antinous first. And then he sent his groaning shafts into the rest, aiming ever opposite, and men fell thick and fast, and you could tell some god was helping them, for they raged through the house cutting men down on every side, and there rose a hideous groaning as heads were struck, and the whole floor smoked with blood. So we died, Agamemnon, and our bodies are lying uncared-for even now in the halls of Odysseus; for the friends in each man's house do not yet know — the friends who would wash the black blood from our wounds, and lay us out, and mourn us, which is the honour owed the dead." I do not know a sadder small thing in the poem than that: a man's last recorded grievance, delivered in the underworld, is that nobody has washed him yet.

And the soul of Agamemnon cried out: "Fortunate son of Laertes, Odysseus of the many devices! What a wife you won, and what great virtue is in her — how sound the wits of blameless Penelope, the daughter of Icarius, and how well she kept the memory of the husband of her youth. And so the fame of her virtue will never die, and the immortals themselves will make for the people of the earth a lovely song for wise Penelope. Not so the daughter of Tyndareus, who devised evil and killed the husband of her youth: a hateful song she will be among men, and she has laid a hard name on all women after her, even on the ones who do good."

The reader has now heard the last movement of an argument that began on the first page. The very first scene of this poem was Zeus on Olympus, complaining about Aegisthus, and from that moment the story of Agamemnon's house has stood behind the story of Odysseus's house as the pattern everything is measured against — told in pieces, each time a little closer, in Books One and Three and Four and Eleven; and here at the end the poem gives the verdict to the murdered king himself, standing in the meadow of the dead among the fresh souls of the suitors, and the verdict is two songs. One wife will be a hateful song forever; the other will have a lovely song made for her by the immortals. And I would like you to notice something, quietly, before we go up into the daylight: the lovely song Agamemnon prophesies has in fact been made, and you are holding it. It is a strange and wonderful thing for a poem to do — to reach its last book and tell you what it is — and it is stranger still whose song it turns out to be. This is a poem in which a man crossed the world so that his name would be in every mouth, and heard himself sung in a stranger's hall, and shouted that name across the water at the cost of ten years; and its final word on fame is given to a woman who never left the house.

While the dead talked, the living were walking down from the town, and when they had left the city they came quickly to the fine well-tended farm of Laertes, which he had won for himself long ago, and laboured over greatly. His house was there, and running all round it the sheds where the slaves who did his work ate and sat and slept; and in the house was an old Sicilian woman who looked after the old man carefully, out there on the land far from the town.

There Odysseus spoke to the herdsmen and his son: "Go into the well-built house now, and kill the best of the pigs for our dinner. I will make trial of my father — whether he will know me when he sees me with his eyes, or fail to, after so long a time."

He gave his weapons to the slaves, and they went inside; and Odysseus went down into the fruitful orchard, testing as he went. He did not find Dolius as he came down into the great garden, nor any of Dolius's sons or slaves — they had gone off, and the old man at the head of them, to gather stones for a wall. He found his father alone in the tidy orchard, digging round a young plant, dressed in a dirty tunic, patched and shameful, with patched oxhide leggings tied round his shins against the scratches, and gloves on his hands because of the brambles, and on his head a goatskin cap; and he was feeding his sorrow. When much-enduring Odysseus saw him, worn out by old age and with that great grief on him, he stood under a tall pear tree and wept. And then he turned it over in his mind and heart: whether to kiss his father and take him in his arms, and tell him everything, how he had come home to his own country — or first to question him, and try him. And as he turned it over, this seemed to him the better way: to try him first, with words that would sting.

With that in mind, godlike Odysseus went straight up to him. His father was keeping his head down, digging round the plant, and his glorious son stood beside him and said: "Old man, there is no lack of skill in the way you keep this orchard. The care in it is everywhere; there is not one thing in the whole garden — not a plant, not a fig, not a vine, not an olive, not a pear, not a vegetable bed — that goes untended. But I will tell you something else, and do not take offence at it: you yourself are not well cared for. Hard old age is on you, and you are dismally unwashed, and dressed in rags. It is not for idleness, certainly, that your master neglects you; and there is nothing of the slave in your looks or your bearing — you have the look of a king. You have the look of a man who ought to bathe, and eat, and sleep soft; that is the right of the old. But come, tell me truly: whose slave are you, and whose orchard is this you keep? And tell me this exactly, because I need to know it: is this country I have come to really Ithaca, as a fellow told me just now on my way here — not a very obliging fellow, for he would not hear my questions out. For I am trying to find out about a guest-friend of mine, whether he is alive somewhere, or dead and in the house of Hades. I will tell you plainly: once, in my own dear country, I entertained a man in my house, and no mortal who ever came travelling to it was dearer. He said his family was of Ithaca, and that his father was Laertes, son of Arceisius. I took him in and entertained him well, with all the plenty that was in the house, and I gave him guest-gifts, such as were fitting: seven talents of worked gold, and a mixing-bowl of solid silver with flowers on it, and twelve single cloaks, and as many rugs, and as many fine mantles and tunics besides, and, apart from all that, four handsome women skilled in fine work, whom he chose for himself."

And his father answered him, weeping: "Stranger, this is indeed the country you ask about; but violent and reckless men hold it now, and the gifts you gave, those countless gifts, you gave for nothing. If you had found him alive in the land of Ithaca, he would have sent you on your way with gifts to match, and good entertainment; that is the right of whoever begins. But tell me this, and tell it exactly: how many years is it since you hosted him — that unlucky guest of yours, my son, if he ever existed — the ill-fated man whom the fish have eaten, somewhere far from his friends and his country, or the beasts and the birds have had, on land? His mother did not shroud him and mourn over him, nor his father, we who gave him life; nor did his richly-dowered wife, steady Penelope, keen over her husband on his bier, as is fitting, and close his eyes — which is the honour owed the dead. And tell me this too, truly, for I want to know it: who are you, and from where? Where is your city, and your parents? And where is the swift ship standing that brought you here with your godlike companions? Or did you come as a passenger on another man's ship, and they set you ashore and went on?"

And resourceful Odysseus answered: "I will tell you everything exactly. I am from Alybas, where I have a famous house, and I am the son of Apheidas, the son of lord Polypemon; my own name is Eperitus. A god drove me off my course from Sicania, to come here against my will; and my ship is standing over there, off the open country away from the town.[^3] As for Odysseus, it is five years now since he left my country. Poor man — and yet the birds were good when he went, on the right hand, and I was glad as I sent him off, and he was glad as he went; and the hearts in both of us hoped we would meet again in friendship, and give glorious gifts."

He said it, and a black cloud of grief covered the old man over; he took up the grimy dust in both hands and poured it down over his grey head, groaning without stopping.

Nobody has ever explained this scene to everyone's satisfaction, and I do not intend to pretend to. The poem gives no motive; Odysseus says only that trying his father first seemed the better way, and the poem walks on. Some readers have found it unforgivable, and I understand them: the war is over, the suitors are dead, the wife is won, and there is no imposter left in the world to guard against, and still he stands under the pear tree working out the sharpest lie, to an old man in gloves, alone, digging round a sapling and feeding his grief. But there are at least two kinder readings, and the reader should have them. The first is Athena's, delivered a long way back on a misty beach, when she said with open admiration that any other man come home from wandering would have rushed straight to his house and family, and that this man cannot do it — that testing is not a policy with him but the deepest habit he has, worn into him like the scar, and an orchard cannot be entered any differently than a kingdom. The second is harder to say of him, and I offer it carefully: this is a man who has never in his life believed good news without proof, and it may be that the proof he cannot do without here is the grief itself — that he needs to see what his absence cost before he can believe what his presence is worth. I decline to choose between these, and I note only what the poem does next, which is that Odysseus lasts about a minute. The sharp sting goes up through his nostrils as he watches his father, and the trial is abandoned unfinished, and he springs at the old man and kisses him.

"I am he, father — I myself, the man you are asking about, home in the twentieth year, in my own country. Stop your weeping and your tears now, for I tell you — and there is need of haste — I have killed the suitors in our house, and paid out their heart-stinging outrage and their evil deeds."

And Laertes answered him: "If you are really Odysseus, my son, come home, then tell me some sign, a plain one, so that I can believe."

For it is worth a half-sentence to observe that in this family absolutely everyone demands the papers — the wife, the father, and the son too, in his time. And resourceful Odysseus answered: "First, then, look at this scar with your own eyes — the one the boar gave me with his white tusk on Parnassus, when you and my honoured mother sent me to her dear father Autolycus, to collect the gifts he promised me when he came here. And come, I will tell you besides the trees in the tidy orchard that you gave me once. I was a small boy, following you through the garden, begging for this and that, and as we walked along among these very trees you named them for me and told me about each one. You gave me thirteen pear trees, and ten apple trees, and forty fig trees; and you named fifty rows of vines that were to be mine, each of them ripening in its own time, with grapes on them at every stage, whenever the seasons of Zeus came down heavy from above."

At that the old man's knees went loose where he stood, and the heart in him, as he recognised the sure signs Odysseus had told him; he threw his arms round his dear son, and much-enduring Odysseus caught him to himself, fainting.

You will allow me one paragraph here, because the poem has now finished doing something it has been doing slowly for eleven books, and it deserves to be seen whole. Odysseus has come home three times over, by three tokens, and no two of them are alike. The scar proved the body: an old wound, read by a nurse's hands in the dark. The bed proved the marriage: a made thing with a living root, known to two people, that cannot be moved without being killed. And the trees prove the land, and the tenderest of the three claims: an inheritance counted out to a small boy on a walk through this same orchard, thirteen pears and ten apples and forty figs, by a father patient enough to name them one by one to a child begging for this and that at his heels. The man has now been re-established in every register in which a man exists — flesh, marriage, ground — and each time by something old, particular, and true, which no god could counterfeit and no stranger could learn (*on tokens, see Book Nineteen, note 1*). The poem's answer to twenty years of beautiful lying turns out to be a short list of fruit trees.

When Laertes had got his breath back and his spirit was gathered in his heart again, he said: "Father Zeus — you gods are still there on high Olympus after all, if the suitors have truly paid for their reckless insolence. But now a terrible fear is on my heart, that the men of Ithaca will all be on us here very soon, and sending messengers everywhere to the Cephallenian (island next to Ithaca) towns besides."

"Take heart," said Odysseus; "do not let it trouble your mind. Let us go to the house here by the orchard; I sent Telemachus ahead, and the cowherd and the swineherd, to make dinner as quickly as they could."

So the two of them went to the fine house, and found Telemachus and the herdsmen carving quantities of meat and mixing the bright wine. And the Sicilian woman bathed great-hearted Laertes in his own house, and rubbed him with oil, and put a fine cloak about him; and Athena came near and filled out the limbs of the old shepherd of the people, and made him taller and sturdier to look at than before. He came out of the bath, and his dear son marvelled at him, seeing him like the deathless gods to look at, and said: "Father, surely one of the everlasting gods has made you better to see, in beauty and stature."

And sound-hearted Laertes answered: "Ah, by Zeus and Athena and Apollo — if only I could have stood beside you yesterday in our halls, with armour on my shoulders and the strength I had when I took the strong citadel of Nericus, on the mainland cape, leading my Cephallenians! I would have loosened the knees of many of them in the hall, and warmed your heart inside you." So they talked; and when the work was done and the meal was ready, they sat down in order on the chairs and benches. And as they were putting their hands to the food, the old man Dolius came in, and his sons with him, tired from their work, for the old Sicilian woman, their mother, had gone out and called them. When they saw Odysseus and took it in, they stood stock-still in the hall, astonished; but Odysseus spoke to them gently: "Sit down to your dinner, old man, and all of you forget your amazement; we have been waiting a long while in this hall, eager for the food, and expecting you every minute." But Dolius came straight at him with both arms out, and took Odysseus's hand and kissed it at the wrist, and asked the one practical question in the room: did Penelope know, or should a messenger run? "She knows already, old man," said Odysseus; "there is no need for you to trouble." And Dolius sat back down on his polished stool.

Meanwhile Rumour the messenger went swiftly all through the city, crying the news of the suitors' ugly death and their fate; and the people heard it and gathered from every side, with groaning and lamentation, in front of Odysseus's house. They carried out the dead and buried them, each family its own, and the dead from other cities they loaded on fast ships for fishermen to carry home; and then they went in a body to the meeting-place, grieving at heart. And when they were assembled and gathered together, Eupeithes stood up among them and spoke — the father of Antinous, the first man godlike Odysseus killed, and grief for his son lay on him past forgetting. Weeping, he addressed them: "Friends, this man has contrived a monstrous thing against the Greeks. Some of us, many and good, he took away in his ships, and he lost the hollow ships and lost the men, every one; and others, and much the best of the Cephallenians, he has killed on his return. Come then, before he can slip away quickly to Pylos, or to sacred Elis where the Epeians rule — let us move, or we shall be hung with shame forever after. This is a disgrace even for men to come to hear of, if we do not take vengeance for the murder of our sons and our brothers. For my part there would be no sweetness in living; I would sooner die at once and be with the men who are gone. Let us move, before they get across the water ahead of us." So he spoke, weeping, and pity took all the Greeks. It is perhaps worth knowing about Eupeithes — whose name, more or less, means the Persuasive — that many years before, when the people of Ithaca were minded to kill him for a piece of piracy, the house he fled to for protection was Odysseus's, and that house held the mob off him. The man now raising the town against the house is a man that house once saved from the town; the poem mentioned it quietly some books ago and does not remind anyone of it here, and neither, at any length, will I.

But now Medon (the herald who was spared in the hall) and the divine singer came to them out of Odysseus's house, where sleep had released them, and stood in the middle of the assembly, and amazement took every man. And Medon, a man of sound sense, said: "Hear me now, men of Ithaca. Odysseus did not devise these deeds against the will of the deathless gods. I myself saw an immortal god, who stood close beside Odysseus in the exact likeness of Mentor; and now he would appear in front of Odysseus, heartening him, and now he went storming down the hall, stampeding the suitors, and they fell thick and fast." So he spoke, and pale fear took hold of them all. And then the old hero Halitherses, the son of Mastor, spoke among them — the one man who saw both forward and back, and who had read the bird-sign to this same assembly years before, and been laughed at: "Hear me now, men of Ithaca, and what I say. It is by your own cowardice, friends, that these deeds were done. You would not obey me, nor Mentor, the shepherd of the people, when we told you to make your sons stop their folly — your sons, who did a monstrous wickedness in their reckless pride, wasting the goods and dishonouring the wife of a great man, because they said he would come home no more. Now let it be this way; obey me as I say it: let us not go, or a man may find a trouble he has drawn down on his own head."

So he spoke; but more than half of them leapt up with a great shout — the rest stayed seated together — for his speech was not to their liking, and Eupeithes had persuaded them, and they ran at once for their armour. When they had got the shining bronze about their bodies, they gathered in a mass in front of the wide-wayed town, and Eupeithes led them, in his folly. He thought he would avenge his son's killing; but he was not to come back again himself, and was to meet his own fate there.

And Athena said to Zeus, the son of Cronos: "Our father, son of Cronos, highest of the powers — answer me, since I ask: what is your mind hiding now within you? Will you make more evil war and dread battle, or will you set friendship between the two sides?"

And Zeus who gathers the clouds answered her: "My child, why question me on this? Was it not your own idea, this plan — that Odysseus should indeed come home and take his payment of these men? Do exactly as you wish; but I will tell you what is fitting. Now that godlike Odysseus has paid the suitors out, let them cut faithful oaths, and let him be king all his days; and let us, for our part, put a forgetting of the killing of their sons and their brothers into the townsmen's minds; and let them love one another as before, and let there be wealth and peace in plenty."[^4]

So he set Athena going where she was already eager, and she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus.

At the farm, when they had put away their desire for the honey-sweet food, much-enduring Odysseus began: "Someone go out and look — in case they are coming, and already close." A son of Dolius went out as he was told, and stood on the threshold, and saw them all near at hand, and his words came out winged with alarm: "They are here, close by — arm, quickly!" Up they got and into their armour: the four around Odysseus, and the six sons of Dolius, and with them Laertes and Dolius put on armour too, grey-headed as they were, fighters because they had to be. And when they had the shining bronze about them, they opened the doors and went out, and Odysseus led them.

And Athena, the daughter of Zeus, came near them, likened to Mentor in body and in voice; and much-enduring Odysseus was glad when he saw her, and said at once to Telemachus his dear son: "Telemachus, now you are come yourself to the place where the best men are sorted out in the fighting, and you will know how not to shame the house of your fathers — we who in strength and courage have been marked out over all the earth."

And sensible Telemachus answered him: "You will see me, dear father, if you care to look, in this present spirit of mine, shaming the house of my fathers not at all — as you put it."

And Laertes was delighted, and said: "What a day this is for me, dear gods! I am a very happy man: my son and my grandson are quarrelling over which of them is braver."

And grey-eyed Athena stood beside the old man and said: "Son of Arceisius, dearest by far of all my companions — pray to the grey-eyed girl, and to father Zeus, and then swing back your long-shadowed spear at once, and let it fly." So she spoke, and breathed great strength into him; and he prayed to the daughter of great Zeus, and swung back his long-shadowed spear at once and let it fly, and struck Eupeithes through the bronze cheek-piece of his helmet, and the bronze did not hold the spear, and the point went through; and he fell with a crash, and his armour clattered on him.

The poem has one killing left in it after four hundred deaths at Troy and a hall full of dead men, and it gives that last spear-cast to the oldest man in the story — to the grandfather, praying first like a pious man, with his son on one side of him and his grandson on the other, all three in bronze in the same line of battle in front of the same farm. The story of this poem, said once at the beginning and once at the bed, is the story of a house; and this is the poem's last picture of the house it means: three generations of one family, standing together in arms on their own land. I will not add anything to that, because it does not want anything added.

Then Odysseus and his glorious son fell on the front rank of them, and struck with swords and leaf-headed spears; and they would have killed them all, and cut every man of them off from his homecoming, if Athena, the daughter of Zeus who bears the aegis, had not cried out in a great voice and held the whole host: "Break off this hard fighting, men of Ithaca — part, now, quickly, and without blood!"

So Athena spoke, and pale fear took hold of them; the weapons flew from their frightened hands and fell, all of them, to the ground at the goddess's voice, and they turned back toward the city, wanting their lives. But much-enduring Odysseus screamed terribly, and gathered himself, and swooped like a high-flown eagle. And then the son of Cronos threw down a smoking thunderbolt, and it fell in front of the grey-eyed goddess, the daughter of the mighty father. And grey-eyed Athena said to Odysseus: "Son of Laertes, seed of Zeus, Odysseus of the many devices — hold, and stop this quarrel of levelling war, or Zeus of the wide brows, the son of Cronos, will be angry with you."

So Athena spoke; and he obeyed her, and his heart was glad.[^5] And oaths of peace between the two sides, for all the days to come, were cut by Athena, the daughter of Zeus who bears the aegis, in the likeness of Mentor, in body and in voice.

## NOTES TO BOOK TWENTY-FOUR

[^1]: **The road of ghosts, and the unburied dead.** This journey appears nowhere else in Homer: the White Rock, the gates of the Sun and the district of dreams belong to no other map in either poem, and Hermes appears as conductor of souls only here — the office became standard later, but in Homer this is its one performance. And a reader with a good memory will notice a difficulty: when we visited the dead in Book Eleven, Elpenor could not rest until his body was burned and mourned, and begged for it as the unburied dead's one need; here the suitors, whose bodies are still lying unwashed in the hall — Amphimedon complains of exactly this — walk straight down to the asphodel with no trouble at all. It is a seam, like the others we have seen (*see Book Eleven, note 4*), and I show it rather than smooth it: the tradition kept more than one belief about the dead, and this book sews with a different thread than Book Eleven did.

[^2]: **Amphimedon's version.** The dead man says that Odysseus, in his great cunning, ordered his wife to set the bow before the suitors. That is not what we watched: we watched Penelope conceive the contest herself, in Book Nineteen, and carry it out in Book Twenty-One, with the beggar doing no more than warmly approving. Either the poet nodded, or — and this is the reading I prefer, though I hold it loosely — a freshly dead man is doing what people do, which is telling the story in the shape that hurts least: to have been beaten by one man's cunning is a better tale in the meadow of the dead than to have been dismantled by a household, wife included. Whether Penelope suspected more than she said is the one question this poem refuses to open for anybody (*see Book Nineteen, note 4*), and it is fitting that the last word muddying it comes from a man with every reason to muddy it.

[^3]: **Wandertown, son of Unsparing.** The names in the last lie are, in Greek, next door to being a joke told with a straight face: he is from Alybas, which sounds like the word for wandering; his father is Apheidas, the Unsparing; his grandfather is Polypemon, Much-suffering; and his own name, Eperitus, is close to a word for a man picked out, or a man of strife. It is the last of the Cretan-type tales (*see Book Thirteen, note 3*), and much the thinnest: his own biography at one degree of separation, barely dressed. Whether the thinness means the habit is running on empty at last, or means the old man was meant to have a fair chance of seeing through it, the poem does not say.

[^4]: **The imposed forgetting.** Zeus's settlement should be allowed its full strangeness: the town is full of fathers and brothers of the dead, the feud-machinery we saw priced in the hall (*see Book Twenty-Two on the blood-price*) is armed and running, and no payment, exile or vengeance will be made — instead the gods will put a *forgetting* of the killings into the townsmen's minds. Our word *amnesty* means exactly this: a deciding-not-to-remember. Centuries after Homer, real Greek cities ended civil wars with a sworn amnesty, the oath at Athens being famously worded "not to remember the evils"; what those cities demanded of human discipline, this poem, more honestly perhaps, hands to a god, because it knows what it is asking. Peace in Ithaca is not achieved in this poem. It is installed.

[^5]: **The end, and the man who was glad to stop.** Ancient scholars doubted this book, as they marked a possible end at the bed in the last one (*see Book Twenty-Three, note 4*), and it is true that the poem stops with almost shocking speed — the treaty is cut in the final sentence, and there is no feast, no dispersal, not one line of afterward. I think the abruptness is right, and I will say why briefly: the book has just paid the poem's three outstanding debts — the pattern of Agamemnon's house closed, the father in the orchard found, the feud settled — and with the accounts clear there is nothing left to narrate except happiness, which Greek epic does not narrate. But the poem does spend four words on its hero before the door shuts, and they are the right ones: told by a goddess to stop fighting, he obeyed, *and his heart was glad*. The man of the many devices ends the poem relieved to be given an order — the first order in twenty years it costs him nothing to obey. And Teiresias's oar is still leaning in the corner of all this, unpaid; the gentle death is still a long way off, off the sea or away from it; the poem ends with its hero home, at peace, and owing one more journey, which is perhaps the truest thing it knows about peace.

*Here the story of the house of Odysseus ends — with the house standing, and everyone in it home.*
